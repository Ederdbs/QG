## What the post-2018 literature adds beyond the four attached papers

Several papers move past the Fhom/Fdrift and A_T>H_T results to show that the *specific formulation* of the genomic relationship/coancestry matrix used for management, not just whether pedigree or markers are used, materially changes outcomes. [1] shows that under genomic optimal-contribution (GOC) management, drift-based inbreeding and homozygosity-based inbreeding "could differ substantially" unless management is IBD-based: constraining a homozygosity-based (molecular kinship) matrix pushes allele frequencies toward 0.5 (low increase in homozygosity for the managed panel, but high drift and poor behaviour at unmanaged neutral loci), whereas constraining a VanRaden/GCTA drift-based matrix keeps drift low at the cost of a high rate of increase in homozygosity. Only IBD-based matrices avoided this trade-off. [16] and [59] quantify this directly by comparing a deviation-based coancestry matrix (θLH, based on observed-vs-expected allele sharing under HWE) against VanRaden's θVR: θLH retained more genetic variability, but θVR kept allele frequencies closer to the base population — a genuine trade-off between "more heterozygosity" and "less frequency drift" that the reader's accepted facts do not resolve. [59] extends this to subdivided populations and shows allelic diversity was comparatively similar across matrix choices while He differed more, when within-subpopulation weight λ≥5. [61] adds a new comparator, a linkage-and-pedigree-informed matrix (G_FGLA), which had the highest correlation with true IBD, the most accurate ΔF, the best gain-per-unit-inbreeding, and was the only scheme keeping frequency drift down while meeting the inbreeding target — direct evidence that matrix choice changes both trajectories and efficiency.

On allelic vs heterozygosity dynamics, [12] gives the sharpest new theoretical result: allelic variation is far more sensitive to bottlenecks than heterozygosity, there is a linear relationship between reduction in census size and reduction in expected number of alleles at drift-mutation equilibrium, and standard Ne-correction formulas for non-ideal populations do not predict allelic loss well — especially for multiallelic loci important for adaptation. They explicitly recommend expanding the Ne>500 conservation guideline to include an allelic-variation criterion. [34] adds that under *optimal* molecular-coancestry management the concept of Ne "loses its meaning" — Ne can be negative in early generations and shows no long-term asymptote, an important extension of the Ns=1/(2θ) static-descriptor point. [4] finds pedigree and genomic selection lose similar total genetic variance after 50 generations, but genomic selection retains more segregating loci (of smaller effect) — a genetic-architecture-level nuance to the Fhom/Fdrift divergence. Finally, computational-cost papers are new: [66]/[67] report a Mendelian-sampling (co)variance package up to 332-fold faster than existing tools, and [49]/[65] report sub-0.01 s mate-allocation solves and provably-global-optimal integer-programming solutions for pairwise relationship thresholds — directly relevant to "cheap enough for an inner loop."

## Consensus on which coancestry / relationship matrix to manage

There is broad convergence that some form of optimal-contribution/optimal-cross selection (OCS/OCC), operating on a marker-based coancestry/kinship matrix rather than pedigree, is the right optimisation *framework* [0,3,6,7,10,11,15,25,32,59,60,69]. There is **no consensus on which specific genomic matrix formulation** to place inside that framework. Multiple post-2018 papers explicitly report that the choice of matrix changes allele-frequency trajectories and/or diversity/inbreeding outcomes: [1] (IBD vs homozygosity- vs drift-based), [16] (θLH vs θVR), [59] (deviation matrix vs GRM vs pedigree in subdivided populations), [61] (G_FGLA vs G_VR vs G_ROH vs IBD), [22]/[72] (a reformulation favouring heterozygote×homozygote pairs improves short-term gain and raises the long-term selection plateau relative to standard VanRaden), [25] (nine variants of VanRaden method 1/2 with different reference-allele-frequency bases give different efficiency of gain per unit inbreeding), and [15] (ROH-based genomic relationship in mating outperforms SNP-based relationship for limiting inbreeding accumulation at equal gain, in pigs). The consensus, such as it is, is that molecular/IBD-flavoured coancestry outperforms simple VanRaden-style drift-based relationship for diversity management, but the field has not converged on a single preferred matrix.

## Allelic richness / rare alleles vs heterozygosity: current position

Post-2018 work supports and sharpens the A_T-over-H_T recommendation rather than contradicting it. [12] gives the clearest mechanistic support: heterozygosity gives good short-term estimates of adaptive-change rate, but allelic variation carries the long-term-adaptive-potential information, and Ne (which governs heterozygosity dynamics) is a poor predictor of allelic loss, particularly for multiallelic loci. [53], working across pig subpopulations, found that "contributions to allelic richness were more consistent" than contributions to gene diversity for identifying genetic reservoirs, whether computed on the whole dataset or subsets — an empirical vote for A_T's greater robustness/informativeness. [64] specifically evaluates genomic-selection strategies for their ability to retain rare alleles and de-novo mutations, flagging that markers used in genomic selection are not strongly associated with rare alleles and that selecting genotyped-only young candidates ignores de-novo mutation effects — reinforcing that heterozygosity/coancestry-based control alone is not sufficient protection for rare variation. [16] adds a genuine qualification: the matrix (θLH) that maximizes retained heterozygosity/variability is not the same one (θVR) that best preserves original allele frequencies, meaning "manage for diversity" and "manage for rare-allele/frequency preservation" are not automatically aligned objectives, even before deciding between H_T and A_T as the target statistic itself.

## Effective size and status number: what is defensible to report

Beyond the accepted Ns=1/(2θ), the literature adds two cautions. First, [34] shows that under *active optimal* molecular management, rate-based Ne is not just theoretically inappropriate (as already accepted) but numerically pathological — negative values and no long-term asymptote — reinforcing that only a static status-number descriptor should be reported under active management. Second, [12] shows Ne (however estimated) is informative about heterozygosity loss but not a good predictor of allelic-variation loss, and that corrections for non-ideal populations don't fix this, especially for high-allele-count loci. Several applied papers still compute and report ΔF-based Ne routinely as a monitoring statistic in real or simulated populations (e.g., pedigree- and ROH-based Ne in dairy cattle [24], sheep [30], gazelle [17,85], pigs [53]) — this remains common descriptive practice, but none of these papers claims rate-based Ne is valid under active selection/management; it is used as a retrospective/monitoring quantity, consistent with the reader's accepted position.

## Optimisation machinery used in practice

AlphaMate (evolutionary algorithm, multi-objective) remains the most cited applied tool, used at wheat/maize/pig scale in [0,3,56]. Newer machinery includes: [49] GOCSMA, a two-stage pipeline — OCS formulated as a linear program with quadratic constraints solved with the conic operator-splitting method (COSMO), followed by mate allocation as a mixed-integer program solved via SCIP's branch-cut-and-price — with mate-allocation runtimes reported at <0.01 s on the simulated QTLMAS2010 dataset. [65] reformulates a strict pairwise-relationship-threshold selection problem as binary integer linear programming (rather than the classical nonlinear quadratic OCS), noting the problem remains NP-hard but that modern solvers certify global optimality for typical instances, tested on a large Norway spruce breeding population and a German Black Pied cattle conservation population; dense, very large instances remain computationally demanding. [60] directly compares linear programming against OCS in simulated beef cattle: LP gives higher short-term gain but worse (higher) inbreeding; OCS better limits inbreeding at somewhat lower gain. [35] is the most direct DE-relevant comparison: genetic algorithm (GA), differential evolution (DE), particle swarm optimisation (PSO) and simulated annealing (SA) were benchmarked for parent selection in a 583-line, ~30k-SNP, 7645-haploblock wheat dataset — GA gave the best convergence speed and fitness; **DE was robust but required longer runtime and careful tuning**; PSO worked for one objective type but not another; SA was lighter computationally but less consistent. [68] reports a binary quadratic-programming multi-trait index outperforming a linear-programming index by ≥53.8% on a gain-to-relatedness ratio metric. [58] introduces a differentiable breeding simulator (DiffBreed) enabling gradient-based optimisation of progeny allocation. On constraint handling specifically with many simultaneous restrictions, only [65] (many pairwise thresholds, reformulated linearly) and [49] (joint quadratic + integer constraints) explicitly discuss this; neither is at the 1e5–1e6-evaluation DE scale for maize hybrids.

## Cross-selection / mating-plan criteria relevant to hybrid selection

The usefulness criterion (UC) underlies most cross-selection frameworks in the retrieved set: it combines a cross's expected mean performance with a term for the genetic variance expected among its progeny, typically the mean plus a selection-intensity-weighted standard deviation of progeny genetic values [18][29][31][33][39][45][50][83]. This variance component is often decomposed into Mendelian sampling variance or its gametic analogue, capturing within-family segregation variance generated by recombination and linkage disequilibrium among QTL in the parents [27][66][67][80]. [2]/[84] extended UC to "UCPC" by explicitly accounting for within-family/progeny variance and within-family selection intensity, showing this converts genetic diversity into short- and long-term gain more efficiently than approaches that ignore within-family selection. [18] frames the choice of criterion (posterior mean variance, UC, optimal haploid value, or mean GEBV/index value) as central to balancing gain and variance retention, finding progeny-variance-based criteria superior for long-term gain. [29] directly compared cross-selection criteria based on progeny-mean versus progeny-variance estimation in inbred line breeding populations, finding variance-based criteria increased the genetic value of superior progeny relative to mean-based selection. [50] (MateR) similarly derives family mean and within-family variance consistently under alternative parameterizations, integrating them with a diversity metric estimating the proportion of additive standard deviation lost.

A diversity term enters these criteria in two main ways. First, some formulations add a genome-wide diversity component directly into the UC-based objective — e.g., "EUCD" in potato, a linear combination of UC and genome-wide diversity that matches UC-only gain while retaining higher diversity and fewer fixed QTL [39]. Second, diversity is controlled at the level of parental contributions or mate allocation rather than within the per-cross criterion itself, as in AlphaMate's multi-objective optimization of gain against group coancestry or inbreeding across a whole mating plan [3][0], or in haplotype/similarity-matrix approaches that quantify complementarity among candidate parents [41][66][67].

Regarding F1 maize hybrids specifically: none of the retrieved abstracts state that the progeny variance of an F1 between two inbred parents is zero, nor do they explicitly contrast this with inbred-line recycling, where non-zero progeny variance is exploited by UC/UCPC-type criteria — this specific point is not established in the retrieved set. The maize-hybrid-relevant abstracts retrieved [31][83] apply the usefulness criterion to donor × elite BC1S2 families and report contrasted means and variances across crosses, but this variance pertains to segregating BC1S2 progeny used to generate new inbred lines, not to F1 hybrids of two already-fixed inbred parents. [37] simulates hybrid mean and GS on F1-derived data in sweet corn but does not address the collapse of the progeny-variance term for hybrid selection. Consequently, how a diversity term should be formulated once the Mendelian-sampling/progeny-variance component vanishes — leaving only mean performance and inter-hybrid complementarity as informative — is not addressed here.

## Gaps this project would be filling

**Per-evaluation computational cost of diversity metrics.** The retrieved set reports efficiency claims for related but distinct quantities: PyMSQ reports up to 332-fold faster computation of Mendelian sampling variance/covariance and a haplotype similarity matrix than an existing tool [66][67]; GOCSMA reports mate-allocation runtimes under 0.01 s using linear/mixed-integer solvers [49]; and APY-related work addresses cubic computational complexity of genomic relationship matrices at mega-scale via core-subset approximation [14]. None benchmarks the per-evaluation cost of diversity metrics themselves (e.g., cost per fitness-function call inside a mating-plan optimizer) as a function of population size or metric type — not established in the retrieved set.

**Diversity metrics defined on hybrids rather than lines/parents.** All diversity/coancestry/relationship measures in the retrieved abstracts are computed on parents, lines, or candidates — pedigree- or marker-based coancestry, ROH/IBD-based inbreeding, haplotype similarity matrices [3][22][41][61][65][66][67]. None defines or evaluates a diversity metric computed directly on hybrid (F1) genotypes or on a population of hybrids as the unit of management — not established in the retrieved set.

**Combinatorial equal-contribution (0/1) selection versus continuous contributions.** AlphaMate optimizes under constraints including equal, minimal, or maximal contributions per parent [3], and other tools formulate selection/mate-allocation as mixed-integer or binary integer linear programs [49][65]. None directly compares combinatorial equal-contribution (0/1) designs against continuous-contribution optimal-contribution designs for gain/diversity outcomes — not established in the retrieved set.

**Constraint handling with many simultaneous restrictions at 1e5–1e6 evaluations.** [35] compares metaheuristics (GA, DE, PSO, SA) on fitness, convergence speed, and computational efficiency, and [65] notes that large, dense pairwise-relationship-threshold problems "remain computationally challenging" despite certifiable global optima under linear formulations. Neither this nor any other retrieved abstract quantifies behavior at evaluation budgets of order 1e5–1e6 — not established in the retrieved set.

**Empirical redundancy among diversity metrics.** Several abstracts compare inbreeding/relatedness estimators against one another (e.g., correlation of 0.95 between F_HOM and F_ROH [9]; comparisons among F_NEJ, F_L&H, F_VR1, F_VR2, F_YAN, F_ROH, F_PED [43][77]; theoretical reconciliation of allele-sharing estimators [78][79]), but none systematically quantifies redundancy among diversity metrics as used within cross- or mate-selection criteria — not established in the retrieved set.
