# Plant and maize literature: managing diversity with molecular information

Screened 2004 records (2018-2026) from Crossref + Europe PMC across 29 plant-breeding queries; 687 scored relevant, 158 directly relevant, 197 on maize.

# Part 1: Plant/Maize Section — Diversity Quantification, Heterotic Structure, and Genetic Resources

## 1. Quantifying TOTAL diversity of a crop germplasm pool from molecular data

The retrieved set confirms gene diversity (He), PIC, and Rogers-distance-family metrics remain the operational default in applied maize diversity papers, but it also surfaces several points that qualify or extend the accepted He/theta/allelic-richness framework.

**Metrics actually reported, and what they're used for.** Across the maize-specific records, the standard reporting package is: expected/observed heterozygosity, PIC, minor allele frequency, modified Rogers distance (MRD), and admixture/PCA-based structure — used almost exclusively for two downstream tasks, (a) heterotic group assignment and (b) parent/tester choice, not for standalone diversity monitoring [2,16,18,26,34,40,51,52,63,72,74]. A recent methods paper explicitly works to unify similarity/distance measures for inbred-line diversity comparison, implying the field still lacks a single agreed distance metric and treats this as an open problem rather than a solved one [72]. This is a **qualification** worth flagging: even though the reader's framework nominates theta=1-He as the correct absolute measure, the applied literature is still largely built around Rogers-distance/PIC reporting and does not treat theta as a standard deliverable — MRD-based indicators of "genetic contribution to elite pools" are used as a distinct construct from raw diversity [28].

**Allelic richness / rare alleles.** Consistent with the accepted claim that richness is the one non-redundant axis, several papers use richness/rare-haplotype detection as their entire justification for going beyond He: haplotype-trait mapping in landraces explicitly targets "beneficial haplotypes" absent or rare in elite lines despite comparable overall heterozygosity [5]; a DNA-pooling method for landraces is built specifically to recover allelic frequency information at loci invisible to summary heterozygosity statistics [22,28]. A sorghum pan-genome paper reports that structural-variant/pan-genome allelic diversity is "deeply diverged" from what SNP-array summary diversity captures, i.e., richness/structural content adds information orthogonal to He-type statistics [64]. A chickpea pan-genome study similarly frames haplotype/pan-genome variation and deleterious-mutation load as distinct axes from bulk diversity indices [0]. **This is additive**, not contradictory: richness/haplotype/pan-genome content is repeatedly the metric that catches what He/theta misses, reinforcing rather than challenging the reader's premise.

**Where plant practice diverges from animal breeding, and why.** Three structural facts recur and explain the divergence directly:

- Maize (and most inbred-line crops) breeds and selects on **homozygous lines**, with heterozygosity (Ho) near zero and used almost solely as a QC signal for line purity, not as a diversity descriptor of the pool — one record reports Ho=0.031 against He=0.334 in pearl millet parental lines, the gap itself being diagnostic of near-complete inbreeding rather than informative about diversity [63]. Animal-breeding coancestry theory is built around a randomly-mating, heterozygous population; the plant analogue instead computes theta directly on fixed line genotypes, exactly as the reader's premise states — this record is an empirical illustration, not a counter-example.
- The **product is the F1 hybrid**, not the line — so diversity is tracked and reported *separately* for the two parental pools and for the cross-pool complementarity, a structure with no obvious animal-breeding analogue [47,48]. GWAS models are now explicitly built to partition additive/dominance QTL effects by **heterotic-group-of-origin (allelic ancestry)**, a partition that has no counterpart in a single-population animal coancestry framework [48].
- Diversity is explicitly evaluated **within vs. between heterotic groups** as two separate management objects rather than a single pool-wide statistic (detailed in Section 2) — a partition that is close to meaningless in most livestock breeding populations, which are not organized into permanently divergent complementary pools.

**Distances and haplotype/ROH measures.** MRD standardized by gene diversity is used as a bridge indicator to compare landrace pools against elite cultivars quantitatively [28]. Kinship/relative-kinship coefficients (near-zero pairwise kinship in the majority of pairs) are reported as an indicator of "allelic redundancy" from repeated use of narrow source germplasm in African highland maize — i.e., low pairwise IBS distance despite superficially large marker-based diversity counts, a caution that summary He/richness numbers can mask redundant ancestral sourcing [18]. ROH-based measures specifically are **not established in the retrieved set** for maize hybrid diversity quantification — no record here computes ROH-derived inbreeding/diversity metrics in maize; this is a gap relative to what the reader might expect from animal-breeding practice.

**Quantitative benchmarks reported.** For context/calibration, not as a challenge to theta:
- North American ex-PVP dent inbreds: 8 marker/pedigree-based subgroups recovered by admixture, aligned with company-of-origin and heterotic pattern [2].
- China summer maize (CSM) region, 490 inbreds, 525,141 SNPs: 66.05% of pairwise relative kinship values near zero [16].
- African highland maize, 298 inbreds: 91% of line pairs differ at 30–36% of scored alleles, but only 32% of pairs have kinship <0.05 — the authors interpret the gap as **allelic redundancy from narrow source-line reuse**, i.e., a large realized theta gap between pool-average and richness-sensitive analysis [18].
- Sub-Saharan early-maturing white maize (376 elite inbreds, DArTtag): PIC=0.39, He=0.37, MAF=0.29, Ho=0.02 [51].
- Sweet corn vs. field corn (514 vs. 181 lines): sweet corn shows systematically **lower** diversity, PIC, and MAF than field corn — a documented between-market-class diversity gap [52].
- Pearl millet drought-adapted parental lines (109 lines, 16,472 SNPs): He=0.334, Ho=0.031; 78.36% of pairs have IBS distance >0.3 [63].

No record in the retrieved set directly computes theta = 1-He or Ns = 1/(2 theta) as defined by the reader's framework on a maize hybrid-usage vector; the applied literature reports He, PIC and MRD as proxies but the specific Ns/theta static-descriptor formalism is **not established in the retrieved set**.

## 2. Heterotic groups and the structure of the maize pool

**Assignment and refinement methods.** Multiple grouping methods are compared head-to-head repeatedly, with no single winner across studies: GCA-based (HGCAMT), SCA-based, combined HSGCA, and SNP-distance-based classification are all used, and several papers report HSGCA as most consistent with testcross performance [14,20,46,53,69], while others find SNP-based classification and testcross-based classification can **disagree**, and that SSR markers linked to yield QTLs give **no advantage** over random SSRs for grouping despite the intuitive expectation that linked markers should be more predictive [40]. This is a direct qualification: marker-based heterotic assignment is not a solved, method-independent procedure the way the reader's collapse of theta to w'fLw on a line-usage vector might imply once groups are fixed — group *assignment itself* remains a live methodological question in the applied literature, prior to any within/between diversity computation.

**What happened to within- and between-group diversity over breeding eras — numbers.** The single most directly relevant record: in a commercial European dent maize program, **differentiation between Stiff Stalk and Non-Stiff Stalk heterotic groups increased significantly over time, while genetic diversity within both groups declined** — reported as the key empirical finding of long-term selection [47]. This is an empirical (not simulated) longitudinal result and it is the clearest quantitative support in the set for treating within-group erosion and between-group divergence as two separate, oppositely-trending trajectories under active selection. A parallel Chinese study establishes genomic divergence boundaries between heterotic groups using a 60K SNP array on 5,822 accessions plus resequencing of 150 lines, finding distinct chloroplast/nuclear divergence between groups, especially for two regionally distinct groups (HG1, HG2), consistent with continued/increasing group differentiation as a real, measurable, ongoing process rather than a static historical artifact [26]. A newly proposed "X group" in Chinese germplasm, distinct from existing Reid/Lancaster-type groups, further shows that between-group structure is not fixed at a small fixed number of historical pools — new divergent groups continue to be identified and exploited [32].

**Why between-group divergence must be maintained, not maximized — direct support and one important complication.** The GWAS/QTL-ancestry work is the clearest mechanistic grounding for this asymmetry: additive and dominance QTL effects for hybrid performance are shown to be **background-specific (allelic-ancestry-dependent)**, meaning the value of divergence is realized specifically through inter-group complementarity at these loci, not through generic between-group distance — pushing divergence past what supports this complementarity would not be expected to add hybrid value and could remove useful shared favorable alleles [48]. This directly supports the "maintain, don't maximize" framing analytically rather than just by convention.

The complication: work on inter-heterotic-group **line crossing** for germplasm improvement reports that deliberately crossing lines *across* the established heterotic boundary (rather than the standard intra-group improvement crossing) generated substantial usable genetic variation and superior segregants (10.8% of hybrids from one sub-population exceeded a high-yielding check) [59]. This does not contradict "maintain, don't maximize" divergence between the *pools used for hybrid production*, but it does show that controlled, temporary breaching of the boundary for germplasm-enhancement purposes (distinct from the commercial hybrid-parent pools) is an actively used tool — i.e., the boundary is maintained for the hybrid-production pools while being selectively porous for pre-breeding/introgression pools. Similarly, a new hybrid wheat program explicitly delineates heterotic pools from global elite germplasm via multi-environment F1 testing as a **founding** step, illustrating that group establishment/maintenance is itself an active, ongoing design choice even in a crop without a legacy heterotic-group structure, not an inherited given [70].

**Between vs. within partitioning, other crops for contrast.** Pigeonpea reports establishing a heterotic pattern via genome-wide prediction/GWAS on 396 resequenced lines with 78.6% of hybrids showing positive mid-parent heterosis [17]; sorghum reports classification into two groups (36 vs. 60 lines) by genetic distance prior to diallel testing, with yield-component traits showing the strongest heterosis [73]. These confirm the within/between-group logic generalizes beyond maize but do not add new quantitative benchmarks for the within-decline/between-increase trend beyond [47].

## 3. Genebank, core collections and introgression of genetic resources

**Core-subset objectives and algorithms (mostly non-maize, informative by analogy).** Core-collection construction is reported across multiple crops with converging objectives — maximize retained diversity (Shannon-Weaver, Nei's index, allelic richness) at minimum accession count — but methods diverge: representative-sampling vs. custom/functional-trait-targeted subsetting in carrot, with representative strategies found "effective" for that collection [19]; GenoCore-style optimization used to select 310 from >3,300 sorghum accessions [60]; distance-based optimization (average accession-to-nearest-entry vs. average entry-to-nearest-entry) applied to cassava with genotypic+phenotypic joint objectives [55]; SNP-based core-set and mini-core-set selection from 2,242 rice accessions down to 247 and 30, respectively [67]; a lettuce core of 268 from 811 accessions reported to capture 99.4% of total genetic variation [71]; wild rice (*O. rufipogon*) core-collection construction from 998 accessions reporting Nei's index 0.187 and Shannon-Wiener 0.308 [33]. **Bioinformatic core-subset extraction using genome-wide SNPs is reported to capture 3–78-fold more haplotypic diversity than geography/environment-based surrogate sampling** across Arabidopsis, Populus and sorghum panels — a quantitative case that marker-based subsetting greatly outperforms passport-data-based subsetting for allelic-richness capture specifically [36]. None of these core-collection papers is maize-specific; **maize-specific core-collection construction/algorithm choice is not established in the retrieved set** — the maize-relevant genetic-resource work here is oriented toward pre-breeding/bridging rather than core-subset extraction per se.

**Pre-breeding/introgression into elite maize (and closely analogous cereal) pools without diluting gain.** This is the most directly load-bearing subsection for the reader's framework, since it addresses the practical tension between "maintain w on established elite lines" and "bring in outside diversity."

- A general (multi-crop, includes maize-relevant) framework argues that **direct introduction of genetic-resource material into closed elite programs causes an ineluctable diversity depletion trend even under active management**, and proposes **bridging populations** (crossing genetic resources to elite lines before introduction into the elite program) as the mechanism to close the performance gap between exotic and elite material without directly damaging elite gain — explicitly framed as necessary because genetic-resource collections remain "underutilized" due to this performance gap [11]. A companion optimal-cross-selection paper formalizes this as a three-tier system — pre-breeding of genetic resources, bridging, and controlled introduction into the elite pool — solved jointly to preserve "overall consistency" of the genetic value/diversity trade-off [3]. Both are simulation/methods papers (not purely empirical field outcomes), and both **support** rather than contradict the idea that naive full-pool introgression would erode the same-size resampling baseline the reader's framework cares about — bridging is explicitly a device to avoid that erosion.

- Landrace-to-elite introgression in maize specifically: DNA-pool genotyping of 156 American/European landraces (2,340 individuals) against 327 elite lines spanning the 20th century identified selective sweeps and developed **two novel indicators of landraces' genomic contribution to elite germplasm**, used to rank promising landraces for introgression rather than introducing material blind — an empirical targeting strategy meant to raise the diversity-per-unit-elite-dilution ratio [28]. Haplotype-based mining in ~1,000 landrace-derived DH lines found landrace haplotypes with **superior phenotypic performance** relative to elite alternative haplotypes at the same loci, stable across populations/environments — i.e., empirical evidence that targeted haplotype introgression need not carry a performance penalty at those specific loci [5]. Rapid-cycling genomic selection within a maize landrace population (899 DH lines, three DH populations, 600K SNP array, replicated across environments) is proposed and empirically tested as a **pre-breeding accelerator** — improving landrace-derived material via GS before it ever contacts the elite pool, rather than introgressing raw landrace material directly [54]. Genome-based prediction theory for landrace-derived populations (DH vs. gamete-capture design) shows the **DH approach has a clear theoretical and empirical advantage**: higher prediction accuracy and no risk of masking landrace variation, versus gamete-capture crosses to a single capture line, which can mask segregating landrace variation behind the capture parent's background [45].

- Wheat pre-breeding (largest, most directly quantified genebank case in the set): genotyping/phenotyping of 7,745–7,683 winter wheat PGR accessions plus 232–325 elite cultivars, with yield breeding values for 707 PGR accessions estimated via **crosses to 36 cultivars specifically to correct for the PGR's own lack of agronomic adaptation** before their value is judged — an explicit methodological acknowledgment that raw PGR performance underestimates true breeding value and must be assessed post-bridging-cross [25]. A related paper reports a decade of practical wheat pre-breeding experience using **genetically estimated parental contribution (GEPC)** to track how much genetic material from PGR donors is actually retained through the pipeline into usable pre-breeding lines [62]. Genomic-prediction model choice matters materially for this use case: incorporating **subpopulation-specific marker effects and additive-by-additive epistasis** improved prediction of PGR-accession value across four traits in the same 7,745-accession wheat genebank panel, versus simpler models [66] — a methodological caveat that naive whole-panel genomic prediction may misvalue genebank material relative to models that respect population structure.

- Non-cereal analogues reinforce the same "bridge, don't dump" logic: wheat wild-relative/PGR introgression is proposed via staged introgression schemes rather than direct crossing [7]; safflower interspecific hybridization for drought-tolerance diversity found direct vs. indirect selection methods differ in effectiveness for recovering yield after wide crosses, i.e., introgression method choice affects how much of the diversity gain survives to usable material [35]; alfalfa pre-breeding across four geographic BASE populations produced **highly differentiated C1 populations** after only one selection cycle, an empirical (not simulated) demonstration that genebank-sourced base pools diverge rapidly under location-specific selection even before elite integration [65]; rice interspecific pre-breeding (*O. glaberrima* × *O. sativa*) for stagnant-flooding tolerance is reported as the first comprehensive evaluation of such pre-breeding populations for this trait, confirming donor material can pass tolerance QTLs into segregating pre-breeding pools, though **integration into elite hybrid-pool diversity accounting is not established in this record** [61]. Simulation-based rapeseed base-pool design used marker+testcross data across four planned recombination cycles to **experimentally verify** pool separation and testcross-performance gains from planned pre-breeding — one of the few records combining simulation design with empirical verification of the outcome [44].

**Conflicts/qualifications worth flagging explicitly.** The genetic-resource literature is consistent in direction (bridging/targeted introgression preserves gain better than direct introduction) but no record in this set gives a quantitative comparison of realized diversity gain per unit gain-penalty for maize specifically against a same-size resampling baseline as defined by the reader's framework — the wheat GEPC and maize landrace-contribution-indicator approaches are the closest proxies, but a direct Ns-before/after or theta-based accounting of introgression cost is **not established in the retrieved set**.

## 4. Selecting within the pool without collapsing diversity

Optimal contribution/optimal cross selection (OCS) is now the standard machinery for the gain-vs-diversity trade-off in the retrieved set, applied across a wide crop range: multi-crop bridging/introduction schemes [7][16], wheat fructan breeding (empirical, comparing truncated selection [TS] vs OCS) [24], wheat recurrent-selection simulation of optimal haplotype stacking (OHS) vs optimal population value (OPV) vs OCS vs truncation over 100 generations [19], and pre-selection-against-a-lethal-allele schemes combining pedigree- or genomic-OCS (POCS/GOCS) with an explicit inbreeding-rate target (ΔF_true = 0.01/generation) [57]. A load-bearing qualifier for the reader's framework: even with marker-based OCS/bridging, [16] states diversity depletion in closed elite programs "appears ineluctable" at realistic breeding population sizes, and argues explicitly for donor introduction via bridging populations rather than tighter within-pool constraints alone — i.e., a theta constraint on the existing pool cannot indefinitely substitute for new-germplasm input.

Simulation work generally shows the diversity cost of intensified selection is real but manageable when explicitly optimized. GARS vs. phenotypic recurrent selection (sorghum, simulation) gave a relative gain advantage of 12–88% for oligogenic architectures under 2–3 cycles/year vs 1 cycle/year [1], but this came with genetic-variance tracking as an explicit output metric, not just gain. Multi-trait ensemble genomic prediction (wheat, 16-founder MAGIC population, empirical+simulation) improved grain-yield prediction accuracy by 3–52% over single-trait models and 10–36% for non-parametric over additive models, and subsequent recurrent-selection simulations showed trait genetic architecture determines whether multi-trait selection helps or hurts long-term gain [18]. Complementarity-based selection (CBS), inspired by mate-choice mechanisms, is proposed specifically to improve the short-term-gain/long-term-diversity trade-off relative to GEBV truncation [33]. A look-ahead selection (LAS) algorithm generating Pareto frontiers for multi-objective (multi-trait, implicitly diversity-adjacent) selection is also reported [3].

On cycle length and rapid cycling: intermediate wheatgrass GS cut the cycle from 2–5 years to 1 year, with an explicit diversity-preserving sampling step built into validation-population selection (~4,000 seedlings profiled, ~100 advanced) [11]; rapid-cycling GS in maize landraces (empirical, three cycles, 899 DH lines, 600k SNP array) is reported as a replicated test of pre-breeding cycle design but the abstract text available does not give a numeric diversity-retention figure [60]; tea GS simulations show cycle-time reduction from a >16-year phenotypic-selection baseline [21]; two-cycle genomewide selection in maize achieves larger gains at equal cost/time via restructured staging, but reports no diversity metric at all [25] — a gap relative to the reader's framework. Empirically, four cycles of reciprocal recurrent selection (RRS) in tropical maize composites directly tracked variance and heritability changes alongside gain (empirical, not simulation) [39], and an empirical genomewide-prediction validation in barley used 27 realized biparental populations selected out of 330,078 possible combinations to test progeny-variance prediction [15]. A path-decomposition method for partitioning both genetic-mean and genetic-variance trends by selection-path is offered as a diagnostic for exactly this gain-vs-variance monitoring problem [65], and a genomic/temporal variance-decomposition framework splits variance into genic and (within/between-chromosome) LD components for tracking over generations [22]. Mutational variance is shown (simulation, ~300,000 variants, 20 generations) to contribute less to long-term response under genomic selection than under phenotypic/pedigree selection, because de novo mutations in candidates are invisible to GBLUP without own-phenotype — a mechanism-level qualifier on why GS-only long-term diversity trajectories differ from classical recurrent selection [27].

## 5. What is specific to HYBRID (F1) breeding vs inbred-line recycling

**Inbred-line recycling (progeny variance is real and actively predicted):** the usefulness-criterion (UC) literature in this set is essentially all pre-fixation, i.e., it targets future *inbred lines*, not F1 hybrids — consistent with the reader's collapse only applying after fixation. Cross potential selection explicitly computes progeny-distribution variance for future inbred lines under recurrent selection [36]; UCPC extends UC to multi-parental crosses with real progeny-distribution variance [2]; barley work validates progeny mean, variance and UC against actual breeding-program data (empirical) [40], as does a barley genomic-variance-prediction study using 27 realized populations [15]; soybean genomic prediction of family mean, genetic variance and superior-progeny mean uses 39 NAM families (empirical) [43]; wheat cross-progeny-variance validation (simulation + experimental) finds predictive ability rises with heritability/progeny size and falls with QTL number, and proposes a new algebraic SD-estimation formula [55]; soybean optimal-cross-selection likewise operates on advanced-line family mean/variance, not hybrids [29]. Barley recombination-rate mapping across 45 populations from 23 spring inbreds is a recycling-stage lever (increasing achievable progeny variance for future recombinants), not a hybrid-diversity tool [17]; the Arabidopsis/maize local-crossover-stimulation result (heterozygous-region juxtaposition raising local CO rate up to 3-fold) is likewise a recycling/introgression tool for generating new recombinant haplotypes, not an F1-hybrid-diversity mechanism [63].

**Hybrid/testcross-level work:** genuinely hybrid-population diversity tracking appears in two records. Stochastic simulations of hybrid rice reciprocal recurrent selection (RRS) report explicitly that larger population sizes give "higher genetic gain and less depletion of genetic variance," and that genomic prediction of hybrid performance plus tester updating both increase hybrid genetic gain — this is diversity managed within the RRS heterotic-pool structure, i.e., at the parental-pool (line-usage) level that feeds hybrids, simulation-based [31]. Empirically, a commercial European hybrid dent maize program shows that over time, differentiation between Stiff Stalk and Non-Stiff Stalk heterotic groups increased significantly while genetic diversity *within* both groups declined — real longitudinal confirmation that standard hybrid-maize commercial selection erodes exactly the line-usage-level diversity the reader's theta constraint targets [54]. Simulation-based base-pool separation in winter rapeseed (four cycles of planned recombination, experimentally verified) is another explicit pool-level diversity-management exercise for a hybrid program [51]. Inter-heterotic-group crossing in maize (5 lines × 4 groups, 535 DH lines, 1,568 test-cross hybrids) uses GS to guide *which* inter-group crosses to make, reporting 10.8% of hybrids in one subpopulation outperforming the check — this is cross-selection guided by GCA/heterotic-group information, not an explicit theta-on-hybrid optimization [64].

The remaining hybrid-adjacent records are hybrid-*mean* prediction, not hybrid-*diversity* management, and do not contradict the vanishing-progeny-variance point — they instead show why the hybrid mean still needs an extra term the reader's framework doesn't need to touch for diversity: pumpkin hybrid GP requires an additive-*and*-dominance g-BLUP model to recover GCA/SCA and mid-/better-parent heterosis, outperforming additive-only models [23]; pigeonpea WGRS across 396 lines found 78.6% of hybrids showed positive mid-parent heterosis, with 129 SNPs/52 CNVs tied to heterotic effects [20]; diploid potato F2 systems-genetics work maps heterotic QTL/mQTL effects on yield and male fertility, some positive and some negative in direction [35]; mock-genome-based heterotic-pool/tester definition is proposed for orphan crops lacking reference genomes [50]. A large body of heterotic-group *classification* work ([5], [38], [41], [47], [53], [59]) uses SSR/SNP diversity purely to assign lines to groups or validate group structure — this operates at the parental-line-classification level, not at hybrid-population diversity optimization, and is fully compatible with (does not extend) the reader's line-usage-vector framework.

## 6. Optimisation machinery reported in the plant literature

- **Quadratic/closed-form optimal-contribution methods**: OCS (undifferentiated), applied in multi-crop bridging [7][16], wheat [19][24], and pre-selection-plus-inbreeding-control variants POCS/GOCS [57]. None of these abstracts state solver type, runtime, or complexity class.
- **Heuristic/greedy core-subset search**: representative vs. custom core-collection methods in carrot, n=433 accessions [26]; GenoCore-based core selection in sorghum, reducing a 3,333-accession genebank (27,560 SNPs) to a 310-accession core [66]; cassava core collections built via two distance-based optimization strategies (average accession-to-nearest-entry; average entry-to-nearest-entry) from 1,486 accessions × 20,023 SNPs, yielding six candidate cores plus a consolidated set [61]; rice core (247) and mini-core (30) selection from 2,242 accessions using 36 SNP markers [73]. None report runtime or algorithmic complexity — only input/output set sizes.
- **Evolutionary/genetic algorithms**: reported use is concentrated *outside* plants in this set — dairy cattle breeding-program design combines stochastic simulation with evolutionary algorithms (plus kernel regression, replacing an earlier higher-simulation-count approach) to optimize a target function balancing genetic gain against inbreeding rate under a fixed budget [56]; flagged explicitly as animal, not plant, breeding. Within plants, the closest analogue is a simulation-based "black-box" numerical optimizer (AI-assisted) for progeny-allocation strategy across mating pairs, treating the whole breeding process as a black-box function of allocation parameters [28] — no mention of DE, GA, or simulated annealing by name, and no stated problem size or convergence/runtime statistic.
- **Multi-objective / index-based algorithms**: a new look-ahead-selection (LAS) variant for Pareto-frontier multi-trait optimization [3]; sparse selection index (SSI), a per-individual training-set subset-selection method [13]; complementarity-based selection strategy (CBS), a heuristic mate-choice-inspired pairing rule [33].
- **Analytical/scoring (non-iterative-optimization) criteria**: usefulness-criterion-based cross ranking (UC, UCPC, cross potential selection) [2][36][40][55] and gametic-variance index selection [14] — these rank candidates/crosses analytically rather than searching a combinatorial space.
- No abstract in the retrieved set reports wall-clock runtime, algorithmic complexity (e.g., Big-O), or scalability benchmarking for any of the above; problem sizes given are candidate-cross counts (330,078 possible barley parent combinations [15]) and germplasm-collection sizes ([66], [73], [61], [26]), not optimizer performance statistics.

## 7. Practical consequences for a diversity-constrained hybrid selection pipeline

- **Confirmed**: OCS-style joint gain/diversity optimization is well established methodology across many crops [7][16][19][24][57], so framing hybrid selection as a constrained optimization problem (DE over line-usage weights subject to a theta bound) sits squarely inside the existing paradigm, not against it.
- **Qualification**: within-pool theta management alone is reported as insufficient to halt diversity depletion at realistic elite population sizes [16]; the pipeline should plan for periodic external-germplasm/bridging injections rather than treating the current line pool as a closed system indefinitely.
- **Confirmed by real hybrid-maize data**: within-heterotic-group diversity has been empirically declining under standard commercial selection even as between-group differentiation increases [54] — direct evidence that an explicit theta constraint at the line-usage-vector level is doing necessary work, not solving a hypothetical problem.
- **Confirmed**: the reader's decision to drop the progeny-variance term for F1s of fixed inbreds is not contradicted anywhere in this set; every UC/progeny-variance-prediction study retrieved operates upstream, at the inbred-line-development stage [2][15][36][40][43][55], not on final hybrids.
- **Qualification for hybrids specifically**: hybrid *mean* prediction (not diversity) benefits from an additive+dominance model, since dominance/SCA effects materially change predicted hybrid performance versus additive-only GEBVs [23] — this is a separate axis from the diversity constraint and doesn't reopen the variance term, but it does mean line-usage-vector optimization on theta should not be conflated with mean-performance ranking of the same crosses.
- **Confirmed with numbers**: rapid-cycling/shortened-generation-interval schemes deliver large gain-rate benefits (12–88% relative gain for oligogenic traits under 2–3 cycles/year [1]; cycle time cut from 2–5 yr to 1 yr [11]) but the diversity cost of faster cycling is not uniformly reported alongside gain — track it explicitly per cycle in the pipeline rather than assuming it is neutral.
- **Confirmed, with a gap**: allelic-richness/core-collection algorithms operate almost exclusively at genebank/pre-breeding scale (thousands to hundreds of accessions [61][66][73][26]) as an upstream diversity-sourcing step, not as a within-elite-pool alternative to theta — consistent with the reader's treatment of richness as a validation-stage, non-redundant supplementary metric rather than a constraint variable.
- **Qualification**: no retrieved plant study reports DE (or any metaheuristic) applied directly to an OCS-type hybrid-selection problem, or any runtime/complexity comparison against the standard quadratic-programming OCS solvers — a DE-based pipeline is not validated against a plant-literature performance baseline; that baseline must come from the reader's own benchmarking, not this corpus.
- **Confirmed**: heterotic-group/GCA-SCA classification work, however extensive, remains parental-line classification and does not itself constitute hybrid-diversity management [5][38][41][47][53][59] — reinforces that Ns/theta tracked on the parent-line pool (not on some hybrid-genotype coancestry) is the operative diversity descriptor, exactly as the reader's framework assumes.
- **Confirmed**: reciprocal recurrent selection with genomic prediction of hybrid performance and tester updating increases hybrid gain while reducing variance depletion at larger population sizes [31] — supports maintaining adequately large parent-line effective pools (i.e., not letting Ns collapse) as a lever available at the RRS/pool-design stage, upstream of the per-cycle hybrid-selection optimization itself.
