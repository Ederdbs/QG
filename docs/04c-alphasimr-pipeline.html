9  Simulating a hybrid breeding programme: AlphaSimR from the ground up

Chapter 6 built the {X, f, hybrids, fL} contract with a deliberately fast simulator: Balding-Nichols allele frequencies, independent markers, no recombination, no dominance. That is enough to exercise the diversity and selection machinery, but it is not how a hybrid breeding programme is actually run, and it is not how anyone would build one from scratch.

This chapter builds one. The crop in mind is maize, the canonical allogamous hybrid crop: two heterotic pools, inbred lines (doubled haploids, DH) drawn from each, lines judged by their testcrosses onto the other pool rather than by themselves, pools improved by reciprocal recurrent selection (RRS) (Comstock et al. 1949), and a commercial product that is a single cross between one line from each pool. Steps 1 to 7 are a step-by-step construction with AlphaSimR (Gaynor et al. 2021), written out in the primitive calls – runMacs(), SimParam, addTraitAD(), makeCross(), hybridCross(), makeDH(), setPheno(), selectInd() – with a plant breeder’s reading of each argument and a measurement at each step. Step 8 assembles those primitives into RRS, replicates it against a paired no-selection control, and shows that the same stage-1 contract comes out the other end, so stage2_select() runs on it exactly as it does on the book’s own simulated data.

Why this machinery, and why it has no counterpart in a self-pollinated programme. In an autogamous (self-pollinated) crop – wheat, rice, soybean, common bean – recombination happens once, in the F1, and the programme then narrows a segregating population toward homozygosity by selfing or single-seed descent, selecting throughout on a line’s own (per se) phenotype, because a self-pollinated genotype breeds true and its own performance is what a farmer will grow: the released variety is very often the same homozygous line the breeder selected. Chapter 22, later in the Evidence and practice part, builds exactly that programme with the same AlphaSimR primitives – makeDH(), setPheno(), selectInd() – and can be read against this one, step by step. None of Steps 2, 4 and 6 below would have a job there: there is no dominance for per se selection to be blind to (an inbred line’s value is its additive merit alone – Step 3 makes this an identity, varD = 0 exactly on a homozygous panel), no tester to cross a candidate onto, no general or specific combining ability to estimate (Sprague and Tatum 1942), and no second, deliberately divergent population to keep apart, because there is only one gene pool, not two heterotic ones held apart on purpose. What follows instead is specific to an allogamous, hybrid-product crop: the inbred line is not the product, the F1 is (Step 5); a line’s breeding value depends on which pool it will be crossed to, not on itself alone (Step 6’s tester dependence); and the trait improvement a programme has made is realised only in a cross remade every season, never in either parental line by itself.

The pedagogical source is the AlphaSimR course of Gorjanc and colleagues (Gorjanc et al. 2022): its vignettes on simulating DNA, simulating traits, the DNA lottery, single- and multi-trait selection, and a plant breeding programme. The course has no hybrid programme of its own; the wheat programme it does build is the subject of Chapter 22. This chapter takes the course’s primitives and assembles them into the hybrid case.

The order matters. Everything in Step 8 is a wrapper in R/13_alphasimr_bridge.R – rrs_config, alphasimr_founder_pools(), alphasimr_rrs_cycle(), alphasimr_make_hybrids(), alphasimr_to_stage1(), alphasimr_pipeline() – and a wrapper is only readable once you know what it is wrapping. AlphaSimR is a Suggests dependency, guarded exactly like quadprog and highs elsewhere in the package; this chapter and Chapter 22 are the only ones that need it.

9.1 Step 1 – Founder genomes: what a founder is, and which engine to use

Every AlphaSimR simulation starts with founder haplotypes. Before choosing an engine, it is worth seeing what one produces, at a size small enough to read. inbred = TRUE makes every founder fully homozygous, which is what a panel of inbred lines is.

Alleles are coded 0 and 1 – ancestral and derived under a coalescent engine, arbitrary labels under quickHaplo(). Because the founders are inbred, the two rows of each individual are identical. pullSegSiteGeno() sums the two rows into an allele dosage in {0, 1, 2}:

The two assertions fix the coding for the rest of the book. AlphaSimR’s dosage is 0/1/2; the contract of Chapter 6 codes the same information as 0/0.5/1, the within-individual frequency of the reference allele (AlphaSimR’s derived allele plays that role), and every bridge function divides by two on the way across (stage1_build() also detects and rescales 0/1/2 on its own). On an inbred individual the molecular self-coancestry of Chapter 2 is exactly 1, which is the second assertion.

There are two engines, and the choice is not cosmetic. runMacs() runs a backward-in-time coalescent, the MaCS program (Chen et al. 2009), under a species-specific demography – species = "MAIZE", "WHEAT", "CATTLE" – so the founders carry a realistic allele-frequency spectrum and realistic linkage disequilibrium (LD). quickHaplo() samples 0s and 1s directly: instant, and with no history behind it. Table 9.1 compares the two.

Read the last two columns of Table 9.1 together. runMacs() is not reproducible from set.seed(), while R’s own random stream advances by the same amount on every call. runMacs() draws a seed for MaCS from R’s stream (one sample.int() per chromosome) and hands it to the binary. The fourth column, asserted in the chunk, says that after two calls from the same seed R’s .Random.seed is identical: the amount of R’s stream the call consumes is fixed, and every draw R makes afterwards is the same in both runs. Yet the third column says the haplotypes differ. What was measured, then, is that MaCS’s output is not a deterministic function of the seed it is handed. The only thing that moves between two runs is the founder haplotypes themselves – and everything computed from those founders. quickHaplo() is pure R sampling and is exactly reproducible.

(The seconds_per_call column is wall clock and will differ on your machine and between renders. runMacs() takes seconds; quickHaplo(), timed over 100 calls because one call is below the clock’s resolution, takes about 0.26 ms per call.)

WarningThe founders are not seeded, and everything downstream is

This is the single most expensive trap in AlphaSimR, because it fails silently: the code looks reproducible, runs without complaint, and returns different numbers. The non-reproducibility is documented in alphasimr_founder_pools()’s help page for runMacs2(); Table 9.1 shows it is not runMacs2-specific but true of MaCS generally.

Two consequences, both because the founders move and not because of anything R’s stream does:

  1. Found once, then branch. Any experiment comparing two schemes must draw the founders a single time and start both arms from that one object. Draw them twice and the two arms start from different founders, and the comparison measures the founders as much as the schemes. Chapter 24’ linked arm is built this way, and so is Step 8’s paired control below.
  2. Save what you cannot reseed. saveRDS(founderGenomes, ...) is the only way to get the same coalescent history back in a later session.

Everything after the founders – the crosses, the doubled haploids, the phenotyping error, the recombination – is controlled by set.seed(), given the founders. Reseeding downstream does not recover the founders and is not needed for anything else.

So what does quickHaplo() give up? Linkage disequilibrium among the founders. The honest way to measure that is against the sampling floor: with n individuals, two completely independent loci still show r^2 of about 1 / (n - 1) by chance alone. Table 9.2 sets each engine against that floor.

Show code 
ldstat <- function(fg) {
  SPx <- SimParam$new(fg)
  g <- pullSegSiteGeno(newPop(fg, simParam = SPx), simParam = SPx)
  g <- g[, apply(g, 2, stats::sd) > 0, drop = FALSE]
  chr <- sub("_.*$", "", colnames(g))           # "chr_site" column names
  r <- stats::cor(g)
  adj <- diag(r[-nrow(r), -1])                  # consecutive columns ...
  adj <- adj[chr[-length(chr)] == chr[-1]]      # ... on the same chromosome
  c(adjacent = mean(adj^2),                     # neighbouring sites, same chromosome
    overall  = mean(r[upper.tri(r)]^2),         # all pairs, mostly distant
    noise    = 1 / (nrow(g) - 1))               # what pure chance gives
}
set.seed(3); lq <- ldstat(quickHaplo(nInd = 100, nChr = 5, segSites = 200,
                                     inbred = TRUE))
set.seed(3); lm_ <- ldstat(runMacs(nInd = 100, nChr = 5, segSites = 200,
                                   inbred = TRUE, species = "MAIZE"))
fmt(rbind(quickHaplo = lq, runMacs = lm_))

adjacent is the mean r^2 between neighbouring sites on the same chromosome (pairs that straddle a chromosome boundary are excluded); overall is over all pairs, most of them on different chromosomes. quickHaplo()’s adjacent-site r^2 sits on the noise floor: there is no founder LD at all, as designed. runMacs()’s is 12 times the floor in this render, and its overall r^2 is barely above it – so the LD it creates is local, decaying with map distance, which is what real LD does.

The runMacs row of Table 9.2 is itself not reproducible, for the reason Table 9.1 establishes; the quickHaplo row is, to the last digit.

What that does and does not imply for the rest of the chapter. MaCS’s non-determinism does not reach R’s stream, so any step that draws its own quickHaplo() founders from a fresh set.seed() is exactly reproducible, and so would be a step that did not reseed: the runMacs() calls above leave the stream exactly where a fixed number of sample.int() calls leaves it. What is not reproducible is anything computed from MaCS founders – the runMacs rows of Table 9.1 and Table 9.2, and all of Step 8. The chapter still calls set.seed() immediately before each step’s own founder draw rather than once at the top, so that each step’s founders do not depend on what earlier steps drew. The draws that follow a founder draw are not all reseeded: in Steps 3 and 4 the phenotyping, the three rounds of random mating and the crosses of the DNA lottery take R’s stream from Step 2’s set.seed() through chunk order, so they are reproducible as part of this chapter’s execution order, not on their own. With that in place, two independent executions agree on every number in Steps 1 to 7 except the wall-clock seconds and the runMacs rows.

That measurement decides the policy this chapter follows:

  • quickHaplo(inbred = TRUE) for Steps 2–7 and all of Chapter 22. They teach the arithmetic of dominance, heterosis and response, where founder LD is not the point, and in exchange every number printed is reproducible from a clean clone. AlphaSimR still applies a real genetic map and real meiosis to every descendant, so linkage and the Bulmer effect of Chapter 24 still operate – only the founders’ history is stylised.
  • runMacs2(split = ) in Step 8. There the divergence between two heterotic pools is the object of study, and no amount of speed compensates for not having it. Every Step 8 result is therefore replicated over founder draws.

Founder LD does matter where marker-QTL association is the mechanism, which is genomic prediction. Chapter 14 does not model it: its traits are built on the markers themselves, so every causal locus is genotyped, there is no marker-QTL gap for founder LD to bridge, and its accuracies are a best case in that respect.

9.2 Step 2 – SimParam, and defining a trait with dominance

SimParam holds everything global: the genetic map, which sites are quantitative trait loci (QTL), the trait effects, the error variances. It is an R6 object, so its methods are called with $, and a variable named SP is picked up automatically by every AlphaSimR function that needs it.

A hybrid programme exists because of dominance: without it a line’s cross performance is its own breeding value and there is nothing a testcross can see that the line per se could not (Chapter 5). So the trait defined here, and used through Step 6, carries a dominance term from the start. This is exactly the reason an autogamous programme has no equivalent of this step: there, dominance is invisible by construction (a released, self-pollinated variety is homozygous, so no heterozygote is ever evaluated or sold) and SP$addTraitA() – additive effects only – is the whole model, the case Chapter 22 runs throughout.

Show code 
# Seeded here, not once at the top of the chapter: Step 1 draws from R's random

# downstream would move with any edit of Step 1 unless the seed
# is reset immediately before the draw that matters.
set.seed(101)
founders <- quickHaplo(nInd = 60, nChr = 10, segSites = 100, inbred = TRUE)
SP <- SimParam$new(founders)
SP$addTraitAD(nQtlPerChr = 100, mean = 10, var = 1,
              meanDD = rrs_cfg[["mean_dd"]], varDD = rrs_cfg[["var_dd"]])
SP$setVarE(h2 = 0.4)

AlphaSimR 2.1.0 has 8 trait constructors (addTraitA(), addTraitAD(), addTraitADE(), addTraitADEG(), addTraitADG(), addTraitAE(), addTraitAEG(), addTraitAG()): one per combination of additive effects with dominance (D), epistasis (E) and genotype-by-environment (G). Choosing between them is choosing a model of gene action; the four that bear on a plant programme are:

Method Adds When a plant programme needs it
SP$addTraitA() additive only line per se performance; the course’s teaching case (Gorjanc et al. 2022), and Step 7 here
SP$addTraitAD() + dominance testcross and hybrid value. This is what Steps 2–6 and Step 8 use
SP$addTraitAG() + genotype-by-environment multi-location yield trials, where the same genotype is scored in several places. Chapter 22 uses it
SP$addTraitADE() + epistasis available; used nowhere in this book

9.2.1 The dominance model, as AlphaSimR writes it

For a diploid with QTL \(k = 1, \dots, m\), let \(x_{ik} \in \{0, 1, 2\}\) be the dosage of the derived allele of individual \(i\) at QTL \(k\) (the column of pullQtlGeno()). AlphaSimR’s TraitAD genetic value is

\[G_i \;=\; \mu_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{m} a_k\,(x_{ik} - 1) + \sum_{k=1}^{m} d_k\,\mathbb{1}[x_{ik} = 1] \tag{9.1}\]

with \(G_i\) the genetic value of individual \(i\) (the \(G(\mathbf{x})\) of Appendix A, written here on the 0/1/2 dosage rather than on the 0/0.5/1 row; not the Caballero-Toro statistic \(G_i\) of Chapter 3), \(x_{ik}\) in this chapter always the 0/1/2 dosage (an entry of \(\mathbf{M}^H\), not of \(\mathbf{X}\)), \(\mu_0\) an intercept, \(a_k \in \mathbb{R}\) the additive effect (half the difference between the two homozygotes, on the classical scale of Falconer and Mackay (1996, ch. 7), which puts the origin at the midpoint of the two homozygotes and assigns them \(+a\) and \(-a\) and the heterozygote \(d\); no equation number is attributed here, the scale is stated in full) and \(d_k \in \mathbb{R}\) the dominance deviation, the departure of the heterozygote from that midpoint. Equation 9.1 is the model of Chapter 5 with AlphaSimR’s coding, and the chunk checks it rather than trusts it:

Show code 
tr <- SP$traits[[1]]
a  <- tr@addEff
d  <- tr@domEff
pop0 <- newPop(founders, simParam = SP)
x0   <- pullQtlGeno(pop0, simParam = SP)
gv_by_hand <- as.numeric(tr@intercept + (x0 - 1) %*% a + (x0 == 1) %*% d)
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(gv_by_hand, as.numeric(gv(pop0)))))

meanDD and varDD parameterise the dominance degree \(\delta_k = d_k / |a_k|\): AlphaSimR draws \(\delta_k \sim N(\texttt{meanDD}, \texttt{varDD})\) and sets \(d_k = \delta_k\,|a_k|\), so \(\delta_k = 0\) is additivity, \(\delta_k = 1\) complete dominance, \(\delta_k > 1\) overdominance, and a positive mean is directional dominance: on average the heterozygote sits above the homozygote midpoint, whichever allele is favourable. Directional dominance is what makes heterosis positive and inbreeding depression a loss on the trait scale; the presence of inbreeding depression is itself the evidence for it (Mackay et al. 2021), and maize grain yield shows large inbreeding depression (Hallauer et al. 2010). Table 9.3 compares the requested and the realised dominance degrees.

Show code 
dd_k <- d / abs(a)

               requested = c(rrs_cfg[["mean_dd"]], rrs_cfg[["var_dd"]]),

nQtlPerChr sets how polygenic the trait is. Setting it equal to segSites makes every segregating site a QTL, which is what this chapter does throughout – convenient, because then the marker matrix and the causal loci are the same set and there is no marker-versus-QTL gap to reason about. Real programmes have that gap; Chapter 10 is where the book takes it seriously.

mean fixes the mean genetic value of the founders. var fixes their additive variance (useVarA = TRUE, the default), not their total genetic variance – with inbred founders the two coincide, for a reason Step 3 shows. corA (Step 7) sets the genetic correlation between traits.

The environmental variance has two routes, and plant breeders use the second:

  • SP$setVarE(h2 = 0.4) fixes an error variance once, from the founders’ genetic variance (here 1.5), and every later setPheno() reuses it.
  • setPheno(varE = , reps = ) sets it per call. reps is the plant idiom for locations: varE is the plot-level error, and a genotype scored in reps plots has its error variance divided by reps, so the phenotype is an entry mean. That is how a staged programme raises selection accuracy from one stage to the next without ever changing the trait’s heritability – the mechanism Chapter 22 is built on, and the one Step 6 uses for testers.

9.3 Step 3 – The base population, and where the variance components live

newPop() turns founder haplotypes into individuals and, because a trait is now defined, computes their genetic values immediately (Table 9.4).

Show code 
pop <- setPheno(newPop(founders, simParam = SP), simParam = SP)
fmt(data.frame(n = pop@nInd, meanG = as.numeric(meanG(pop)),
               varG = as.numeric(varG(pop)), varP = as.numeric(varP(pop)),
               h2_realised = as.numeric(varG(pop) / varP(pop))), 3)

The realised heritability is near but not equal to the requested 0.4: setVarE() fixes a variance, and the ratio it produces in any finite sample is itself a random variable. This is a general and useful habit – check what the simulation realised, do not assume what you requested.

genParam() returns the full decomposition. Four of its fields are the ones a hybrid programme cares about, and they read differently on a panel of inbred lines and on a random-mating population made from them (Table 9.5):

Show code

Show code 
# Among fully homozygous lines the locus value is a (x - 1), so at linkage
# equilibrium varA = sum 4 p q a^2, while genicVarA = sum 2 p q alpha^2 with
# alpha = a + d (q - p): under dominance the LE ratio is close to, not
# exactly, 2. What varA carries above varA_LE is disequilibrium.
p_f      <- colMeans(x0) / 2                        # founder allele frequencies
varA_f   <- vtab[["varA"]][1]
genic_f  <- vtab[["genicVarA"]][1]
varA_LE  <- sum(4 * p_f * (1 - p_f) * a^2)
ratio_LE <- varA_LE / genic_f
varA_LD  <- varA_f - varA_LE
ratio_LD <- varA_LD / genic_f
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(as.numeric(bv(pop, simParam = SP)[, 1]),
                           as.numeric(sweep(x0, 2, 2 * p_f) %*% a))))
# Verification echo, not a table: every value is cited in the text below.
c(varA = varA_f, varA_LE = varA_LE, ratio_LE = ratio_LE,
  varA_LD = varA_LD, ratio_LD = ratio_LD) |> fmt(4)
#>     varA  varA_LE ratio_LE  varA_LD ratio_LD 
#>   1.0000   0.8495   1.9875   0.1505   0.3522

varA is the realised additive variance; genicVarA is what it would be if the loci were independent and in Hardy-Weinberg proportions. Their ratio is the whole subject of Chapter 24, and the package’s genic_var() computes the same quantity from a marker matrix directly.

The founders’ ratio of 2.34 has two parts, and the chunk separates them. Among fully homozygous lines the value at QTL \(k\) is \(a_k (x_{ik} - 1)\) with \(x_{ik} \in \{0, 2\}\), and AlphaSimR’s bv() on these founders is exactly \(\sum_k a_k (x_{ik} - 2 p_k)\) (asserted in the chunk). At linkage equilibrium its variance is varA_LE \(= \sum_k 4 p_k q_k a_k^2 =\) 0.8495, against a genic value of 0.4274: a ratio of 1.99, exact given the effects and the frequencies. That part is inbreeding – a homozygous population has per-locus variance 4pq where a Hardy-Weinberg one has 2pq. It is close to 2 and not exactly 2 because genicVarA is written with the Hardy-Weinberg average effect \(\alpha_k = a_k + d_k (q_k - p_k)\) defined below, whereas the homozygous lines carry \(a_k\); twice the genic value equals varA_LE only where \(d_k (q_k - p_k) = 0\) at every locus. The remainder, varA_LD = varA - varA_LE \(=\) 0.1505, or 0.35 in ratio units, is sampling (gametic-phase) disequilibrium among only 60 founders’ worth of gametes. Three rounds of random mating restore Hardy-Weinberg and pull the ratio to 1.06; what remains is that same disequilibrium. A ratio away from 1 in an outbred population is the gametic-phase disequilibrium Chapter 4 defines and Chapter 24 measures; a ratio away from 1 in an inbred one is mostly the homozygosity.

The dominance columns carry the hybrid lesson. varD among the inbred founders is exactly zero (the assertion above): a homozygous line has no heterozygous locus, so its dominance contribution is zero whatever \(d\) is, and no amount of per se evaluation of inbred lines can see dominance. This single identity is the operational reason an autogamous programme stops at per se selection and never needs a testcross: its released lines are exactly this “founders” row, varD = 0 for the whole life of the programme, so a line’s genetic value equals its breeding value up to a constant common to all lines (the bv() assertion above: \(G_i - \sum_k a_k (x_{ik} - 2p_k) = \mu_0 + \sum_k a_k (2p_k - 1)\)), and its per se phenotype is that value plus error. After random mating varD appears. Two identities make that quantitative. With \(p_k\) the founders’ frequency of the derived allele at QTL \(k\) and \(q_k = 1 - p_k\), the population mean under Hardy-Weinberg proportions is the single-locus mean of Falconer and Mackay (1996, ch. 7), summed over loci (no equation number is attributed)

\[M_{HW} \;=\; \mu_0 + \sum_k \big[a_k\,(p_k - q_k) + 2\,p_k q_k\,d_k\big] \tag{9.2}\]

while the inbred panel at the same frequencies has only the first term of Equation 9.2. The difference, \(2\sum_k d_k p_k q_k\), is the inbreeding depression of Chapter 5 at \(F = 1\), which the package computes as inbreeding_depression(p, d, F_coef = 1). And the genic additive variance is \(\sum_k 2 p_k q_k \alpha_k^2\) with \(\alpha_k = a_k + d_k\,(q_k - p_k)\) the average effect of a gene substitution. Falconer and Mackay (1996) define \(\alpha\) in Ch. 7 and the single-locus additive variance \(2pq\alpha^2\) in Ch. 8; the equation numbers are not attributed here, so both identities are stated in this chapter’s notation: \(\alpha_k = a_k + d_k\,(q_k - p_k)\) and, summed over independent loci, \(\sum_k 2 p_k q_k \alpha_k^2\), which is the genic variance. (\(\alpha_k\) is the average effect throughout this chapter, not the diversity-loss budget \(\alpha\) of the rest of the book.) The chunk asserts both identities; Table 9.6 gives the two means.

9.4 Step 4 – The DNA lottery, heterosis, and why an F1 is not a family

The course (Gorjanc et al. 2022) demonstrates Mendelian sampling by crossing outbred founders and showing that full sibs vary. With inbred parents the result is different (Table 9.7), and it is the fact hybrid breeding is organised around.

f1_sd is zero, asserted to machine precision. Two fully inbred parents are homozygous everywhere, so every gamete each one makes is identical, and every F1 is the same heterozygote. The 200 F1 plants from a cross of two inbred lines are 200 copies of one genotype. There is no lottery yet – and this uniformity is the first reason a single-cross hybrid is a saleable variety.

f1_mean sits well above better_parent. That is heterosis, and under the model of Equation 9.1 it has an exact value: for inbred parents \(i\) and \(j\) with allele indicators \(u_{ik}, u_{jk} \in \{0, 1\}\) (their dosage halved), the F1 is heterozygous exactly where they differ, so

\[H_{ij} \;=\; G_{ij} - \tfrac{1}{2}(G_i + G_j) \;=\; \sum_k d_k\,\mathbb{1}[u_{ik} \neq u_{jk}] \tag{9.3}\]

with \(G_{ij}\) the genetic value of the F1 between \(i\) and \(j\) (Equation 9.1 applied to its dosages). The additive terms cancel from the mid-parent difference because an inbred parent’s additive contribution is exactly half the F1’s. heterosis_value(X, d) is this sum, with X the F1’s 0/0.5/1 genotype; the chunk asserts it against AlphaSimR’s genetic values and Table 9.8 lists it per cross:

Over a set of crosses the same identity has a closed form in the allele frequencies. Take the 60 founders as two arbitrary halves, “pool A” (lines 1–30) and “pool B” (lines 31–60) – arbitrary because quickHaplo() gave them a common history and no divergence, which Step 8 will add – and cross every A line to every B line. hybridCross(crossPlan = "testcross") is the full factorial. With \(p_{Ak}\) and \(p_{Bk}\) the derived-allele frequencies of the two pools at QTL \(k\), the mean of \(H_{ij}\) over the factorial is heterosis_expected(pA, pB, d), the identity of Chapter 5:

\[\bar H \;=\; \sum_k d_k\,\big(p_{Ak} + p_{Bk} - 2\,p_{Ak}\,p_{Bk}\big) \tag{9.4}\]

with \(\bar H\) the mean mid-parent heterosis over the factorial (the \(\mu\) of Appendix A, renamed because \(\mu_0\) is the intercept here; Table 9.9).

Exact, on the full factorial, because every A x B pairing is present once. The size – 44.3 percent of the mid-parent – follows from meanDD and from quickHaplo()’s frequencies near one half, where \(p_A + p_B - 2 p_A p_B\) is near its maximum; it is not calibrated to a crop. Falconer and Mackay (1996, ch. 14) derive the heterosis of the F1 between two random-mating populations as \(H_{F1} = \sum_k d_k y_k^2\) with \(y_k = p_{Ak} - p_{Bk}\) (stated here in this chapter’s notation rather than by their equation number; it is twice the divergence term of heterosis_partition()). The two do not conflict; they use different mid-parents. Theirs carries each population’s own random-mating heterozygosity; an inbred line carries none, so the whole F1 heterozygosity is heterosis and Equation 9.4 keeps the shared term as well – Chapter 5 works the reconciliation through.

The lottery starts one meiosis later. dh_sd is the standard deviation among doubled haploids drawn from that F1, and it is substantial – this is the segregation that F2, DH and recombinant inbred line (RIL) populations exist to sample. It is also why Chapter 7 is a chapter: the number of DH lines you pull from a cross is the number of tickets you buy, and no downstream selection stage can recover a combination that was never sampled.

transgressive is the fraction of DH lines exceeding the better parent per se: 0.025, 0.01 and 0.32 for the three crosses. It depends strongly on the cross, and gap_in_dh_sd says why: the DH lines are centred on the mid-parent, so what matters is how far the better parent sits above it in units of the DH standard deviation – 1.95, 2.19 and 0.45. The cross with the smallest gap is the one with the most transgressive lines (asserted), about a third of them; the other two, whose better parent is two DH standard deviations above the mid-parent, give a few percent. Crossing two parents whose favourable alleles sit at different loci produces progeny beyond either of them – transgressive segregation, which Mackay et al. (2021) treat, with heterosis and inbreeding depression, as consequences of one mechanism: favourable alleles dispersed between the parents, plus directional dominance – and how often depends on the gap between the parents relative to the segregation variance, not on a constant.

9.5 Step 5 – Selfing, doubled haploids, and why hybrid seed is remade every year

A plant breeder has to get from a heterozygous F1 to a homozygous line. There are two routes, and AlphaSimR implements both. Following the same cross through selfing generations also answers a question every hybrid grower has asked: what happens if the F1 seed is saved and sown again.

This is the one step where the allogamous, hybrid case and the autogamous case use literally the same primitive – makeDH() is exactly what Chapter 22 calls to take its wheat F1s straight to a homozygous line, one generation instead of the six or seven selfing generations traced in Table 9.10 – and where they diverge again in what the resulting inbred line is for. In an autogamous programme that homozygous line is commonly the release: it breeds true, so what leaves the DH pipeline is what a farmer eventually grows. In this chapter’s programme the homozygous DH line is a parent, held inside a heterotic pool, testcrossed and recycled (Step 6, Step 8), and never sold on its own; the object a grower plants is the F1 these lines make together, which is the reason it cannot be saved.

Under selfing without selection, the fraction of the F1’s heterozygous loci still heterozygous in generation \(F_t\) is \((1/2)^{t-1}\) in expectation, so the expected mean of the selfed generation is

\[\mathbb{E}[M_{F_t}] \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}(G_i + G_j) + H_{ij}\,(1/2)^{t-1} \tag{9.5}\]

with \(H_{ij}\) the F1 heterosis of Equation 9.3. At \(t = 2\) this is the classical result that the F2 retains half the F1 heterosis, \(H_{F2} = \tfrac{1}{2} H_{F1}\), which Falconer and Mackay (1996) derive in Ch. 14 for the F2 of a cross between two populations (stated here in this chapter’s notation rather than by their equation number); Equation 9.5 extends it to later selfed generations by the same argument, since the expected heterozygosity at every locus is halved each generation. Table 9.10 follows one cross through seven generations.

Three columns, three lessons.

homozygosity: self() halves the remaining heterozygosity every generation – the familiar 1 - (1/2)^(t-1) approach to fixation, which never quite arrives. makeDH() gets there in one step, at 1.0 exactly, by doubling a haploid gamete.

meanG against expected: the F2 mean falls by half the heterosis, the F3 by three quarters, and the DH lines land on the mid-parent. Each row is 400 plants, one per F1 plant: self(nProgeny = 1) carries 400 parallel, independent selfing lineages forward, so within a row the 400 values are independent and sdG / sqrt(n_f1) is the standard error of the row’s mean. The check is statistical: z is the observed minus expected mean in units of that standard error, and the chunk asserts every |z| < 4. Across rows the same 400 lineages are followed – each \(F_t\) plant is the selfed progeny of one \(F_{t-1}\) plant – so successive rows are correlated, and the table is 400 lineages checked at seven generations, not seven independent samples. Nothing about the parents changed; the loss is the heterozygosity being halved, times \(d\). This is why hybrid seed is remade from the two inbred parents every season and why a maize F2 crop yields visibly less than the F1 it was saved from.

sdG: the F1 has none and the F2 has plenty. The F2 is not a variety; it is a segregating population, and a uniform, high-yielding one cannot be recovered from it by anything but selection and re-fixation – which is the line development a breeding programme does, not something a grower can do.

The saving DH buys is seasons, and seasons are the currency of a breeding programme: six or seven selfing generations against one DH cycle. Step 8’s scheme uses makeDH() for precisely this reason, and the cost that never shows up in the simulation is that DH production is a laboratory operation with its own failure rate and its own genotype-dependent response.

The other operations a scheme needs are all one call each: randCross() for random mating within a pool, makeCross() for a specified crossing plan, hybridCross() for a full factorial or a testcross between two groups, and mergePops() or c() to combine populations.

9.6 Step 6 – Phenotyping and selection: per se, then on the testcross

Selection needs a criterion. setPheno() supplies the noisy one; selectInd() acts on it. This step does it twice: first on a line’s own phenotype, as the course does (Gorjanc et al. 2022), to check the breeder’s equation; then on the testcross, which is how a hybrid programme actually ranks lines.

9.6.1 Per se selection and the breeder’s equation

Table 9.11 checks the breeder’s equation on per se selection.

Show code 
n_prog <- 1000   # progeny of the selected parents, per draw
be_draw <- function(q, r) {
  set.seed(500 + r)
  mx    <- setPheno(mixed, simParam = SP)
  sel   <- selectInd(mx, nInd = round(q * mx@nInd), use = "pheno", simParam = SP)
  prog  <- randCross(sel, nCrosses = n_prog, simParam = SP)
  S     <- as.numeric(meanP(sel) - meanP(mx))
  vA    <- as.numeric(varA(mx, simParam = SP))
  i_emp <- S / sqrt(as.numeric(varP(mx)))
  r_emp <- stats::cor(bv(mx, simParam = SP)[, 1], pheno(mx)[, 1])
  c(i_empirical = i_emp,
    R_S_h2      = S * vA / as.numeric(varP(mx)),
    R_i_r_sdA   = i_emp * r_emp * sqrt(vA),
    R_observed  = as.numeric(meanG(prog) - meanG(mixed)))
}
n_be <- 20
sweep_q <- do.call(rbind, lapply(c(0.05, 0.10, 0.20), function(q) {
  draws <- t(sapply(seq_len(n_be), function(r) be_draw(q, r)))
  gap   <- draws[, "R_observed"] - draws[, "R_S_h2"]
  data.frame(q = q, i_normal = sel_intensity(q),
             t(colMeans(draws)),
             R_observed_sd = stats::sd(draws[, "R_observed"]),
             gap_mean = mean(gap), gap_sd = stats::sd(gap))
}))
fmt(sweep_q[, c("q", "i_normal", "i_empirical", "R_S_h2", "R_i_r_sdA")], 3)
fmt(sweep_q[, c("q", "R_observed", "R_observed_sd", "gap_mean", "gap_sd")], 3)

Both forms of the breeder’s equation are here, each averaged over 20 independent draws of the phenotypes, the selection and the progeny (the population selected from is the same mixed every time, so varA is fixed and only S, the accuracy and the Mendelian sampling vary). R = S * h2 uses the selection differential directly, with h2 the narrow-sense heritability varA / varP of the population selected from; R = i * r * sigma_A decomposes it into intensity, accuracy and available variation – the form Chapter 4 builds the book’s objective side on. Because the trait has dominance, the accuracy r is the correlation of the phenotype with the breeding value (bv()), not with the total genetic value: only the additive part is transmitted. R_observed is the realised change in genetic mean between the selected parents’ random-mated progeny and the starting population, which is what the prediction is a prediction of.

gap_mean is the paired difference between the observed response and R_S_h2, with gap_sd its standard deviation over the draws. The mean gap is -0.5 percent of the prediction at q = 0.05 and 2.5 percent at q = 0.20; its standard error over the 20 draws is gap_sd / sqrt(20), and the largest gap in units of that standard error is 1.1. A single draw is another matter: R_observed_sd says that one realisation of the response scatters by around 11 percent of the prediction at q = 0.05, which is why the course’s one-off comparison (Gorjanc et al. 2022) “did not match perfectly” and why this one is replicated. The equation predicts the additive response to first order in the allele-frequency change; what it leaves out is second order in that change and the dominance contribution of the progeny’s departure from Hardy-Weinberg proportions, both small with 1000 progeny from 50 or more parents. The empirical intensity agrees with the normal-theory value to within 0.2 percent: with 1000 candidates and a criterion that is a sum over 1000 loci plus normal error, the two assumptions of sel_intensity() – an infinite population and a normal criterion – are nearly met.

9.6.2 Selection on the testcross: general combining ability

A maize breeder does not rank inbred lines on their own yield. A line is crossed to a tester from the opposite pool, the testcross hybrids are grown in yield trials, and the line is ranked on the mean of its testcrosses. This subsection is the one with no autogamous counterpart at all: a self-pollinated variety is judged, released and grown as itself, so selection there stops at the per se phenotype of Step 6’s first half. A hybrid parent is judged by a cross it will never be sold as, which is the entire reason general and specific combining ability exist as concepts. The quantity being estimated is the line’s general combining ability (GCA), the average performance of its crosses expressed as a deviation from the mean of all crosses (Sprague and Tatum 1942); what a particular cross does beyond the sum of its two parents’ GCAs is the specific combining ability (SCA). The reciprocal recurrent selection of Comstock et al. (1949) is built on using the opposite pool as the tester so that selection captures both.

On the full factorial of Step 4 the GCA of every A line against pool B can be computed exactly, and under the model of Step 2 it has a closed form. With \(u_{ik}\) the line’s allele indicator, \(\bar G\) the factorial mean and \(g^A_i\) the GCA of pool-A line \(i\) against pool B (the notation of Appendix A),

\[g^A_i \;=\; \frac{1}{n_B}\sum_{j} G_{ij} - \bar G \;=\; \sum_k \alpha^{(B)}_k\,(u_{ik} - p_{Ak}), \qquad \alpha^{(B)}_k = a_k + d_k\,(1 - 2\,p_{Bk}) \tag{9.6}\]

\(\alpha^{(B)}_k\) is the average effect of substituting the line’s allele into a pool-B background – the same \(\alpha = a + d(q - p)\) of Step 3, with the frequency of the tester pool, not the line’s own pool. That is the algebraic content of the sentence “combining ability is a property of a line against a tester”: change the tester pool and the weights change. Its dominance part, \(\sum_k d_k (1 - 2 p_{Bk})(u_{ik} - p_{Ak})\), is the gca_A of heterosis_gca_sca() (Section 5.10), and the interaction residual of the factorial is that function’s sca. The chunk asserts all three identities; Table 9.12 gives the two variances and the correlation between a line’s own genetic value and its GCA.

Under Equation 9.6, the part of \(g^A_i\) that the line’s own genetic value does not carry is the dominance part, weighted at QTL \(k\) by \(d_k (1 - 2 p_{Bk})\), so what sets its size is how far the tester pool’s frequencies sit from one half. quickHaplo() samples every allele at frequency one half (Step 4), and here the mean of \(|1 - 2 p_{Bk}|\) over the 1000 QTL is 0.144, against a maximum of 1: the dominance weights are small, the dominance contribution to GCA nearly vanishes, and a line’s own genetic value ranks it for combining ability almost perfectly. That is a property of the founder frequencies, not of the two halves being undifferentiated: pools with no divergence but an allele-frequency spectrum away from one half have weights away from zero as well. Anything that moves \(p_{Bk}\) away from one half – the founders’ frequency spectrum, drift in the tester pool after a split, selection – gives the testcross information the line per se does not carry. Step 8 separates the two routes: its split = 0 row has a coalescent frequency spectrum and no divergence, and the sweep measures what divergence adds to that.

9.6.3 How many testers, how many locations

A programme cannot cross every candidate to every line of the other pool. It uses \(t\) testers and \(r\) locations (\(t\) is the \(k\) of the mating-design notation of Appendix A, renamed because \(k\) indexes QTL here; it is not the selfing generation of Equation 9.5 nor a Student \(t\)), and the estimate of \(g^A_i\) is the mean phenotype of the line’s \(t\) testcrosses, each an entry mean over \(r\) plots. Writing the testcross phenotype as \(\bar G + g^A_i + g^B_j + s_{ij} + e_{ij}\) with \(e_{ij}\) the entry-mean error of variance \(\sigma^2_e / r\), and averaging over the \(t\) testers (whose own GCAs are common to every candidate and drop out of the ranking), the estimate is \(\hat g^A_i = g^A_i + \bar s_i + \bar e_i\), with \(\bar s_i\) the mean of the line’s SCA with its \(t\) testers and \(\bar e_i\) the mean error. The statistic the chunk computes is a sample correlation between \(\hat g^A_i\) and \(g^A_i\) over the \(n_A =\) 30 fixed candidate lines, with the \(t\) testers drawn without replacement from the \(n_B =\) 30 lines of pool B. To first order its expectation is

\[\rho(t, r) \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{v_g}{v_g + v_s + v_e}}, \qquad v_g = \frac{1}{n_A - 1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_A} (g^A_i)^2, \quad v_s = \frac{\sigma^2_{SCA}}{t}\cdot\frac{n_A}{n_A - 1}\cdot\frac{n_B - t}{n_B - 1}, \quad v_e = \frac{\sigma^2_e}{t\,r} \tag{9.7}\]

where \(v_g\) is the sample variance (\(n_A - 1\) divisor) of the candidates’ true GCA, because the correlation is taken over exactly those \(n_A\) points; \(\sigma^2_{SCA} = \frac{1}{n_A n_B}\sum_{ij} s_{ij}^2\) is the SCA variance on the factorial (computed above), \((n_B - t)/(n_B - 1)\) is the finite-population correction for \(t\) testers drawn without replacement from \(n_B\), \(n_A/(n_A - 1)\) turns the mean of the per-candidate variances of \(\bar s_i\) into the expected sample variance, and \(\sigma^2_e\) is SP$varE, the plot error of Step 2. The textbook form of Equation 9.7 replaces \(v_g\) by the population variance of GCA and \(v_s\) by \(\sigma^2_{SCA}/t\): it assumes an infinite tester pool and population variances. It is kept in Table 9.13 as predicted_infinite, so the two can be compared. Neither corrects the second-order term of a sample correlation (the Jensen term); it is measured directly, not assumed away, in Table 9.14, and left uncorrected because it is small at this \(n_A\). The simulation measures the thing itself: repeated draws of the tester set and of the field error, at every combination.

Accuracy rises with both, and the two arguments buy different things. More locations shrink only the error term; more testers shrink the error term and the SCA term, because a line’s specific interactions with its testers average out. Table 9.13 is 20 draws of the tester set and of the field error per cell; the corrected and the textbook predictions differ by at most 0.007 here. z is the observed mean minus the corrected prediction in units of the standard error of that mean over the 20 draws; the largest |z| over the eight cells is 1.63 (it would be 1.91 against the textbook form), so at this replication the observed accuracies agree with the prediction. In the scheme of Step 8 the testers are 2 individuals of the opposite pool and the trials have one location, which puts it in the low-accuracy corner of Table 9.13. A real maize programme would use elite inbred testers and several locations; Hallauer et al. (2010) is the reference on the choice of tester.

gain_trait1 and gain_trait2 are the expected genetic change in each trait per unit of selection intensity on the index, G b / sqrt(b' P b), and aggregate is their a-weighted sum – which equals cor_with_H times the standard deviation of the aggregate genotype, asserted. Three readings, in the order they matter:

  1. Equal weights are not the Smith-Hazel solution. Smith-Hazel puts 0.75 units of weight on trait 2 for every unit on trait 1, not 1.00. The index is b = P^-1 G a (Smith 1936; Hazel 1943) (\(\boldsymbol\Sigma_P^{-1}\boldsymbol\Sigma_G\mathbf{a}\) in the notation of Appendix A), with no further structure, and b is proportional to a if and only if a is an eigenvector of P^-1 G. A sufficient condition is G = c P – every trait with the same heritability c and the genetic correlations equal to the phenotypic ones – which says nothing about the traits being equally variable or uncorrelated: index_smith_hazel()’s help page gives a correlated-trait pair (G = I, P = [[2, 0.5], [0.5, 2]]) for which equal weights are exact. Here the heritabilities are 0.47 and 0.07 and the genetic and phenotypic correlations -0.42 and -0.08, so G is nowhere near a multiple of P, and Table 9.16 shows that (1, 1) is not an eigenvector of P^-1 G.
  2. The cost on the objective is modest here; the composition of the gain is not. Equal weighting loses 0.2 percent of the attainable correlation with the aggregate genotype. But it moves trait 1 by 0.011 per unit intensity where Smith-Hazel moves it by 0.044, 4.1 times as much; both indices put most of the aggregate into trait 2, whose genetic variance is 4.6 times larger, and the weights decide how much trait 1 moves at all. An index that is nearly as good on the objective can still land the gain somewhere else.
  3. The restricted index holds trait 2 at exactly zero, at a cost of 15 percent of the aggregate. That is the construction of Kempthorne and Nordskog (1959) and, as Chapter 4 argues, the classical ancestor of this book’s alpha constraint – restrict a direction rather than price it.

Smith-Hazel is defined as the weight vector maximising that correlation, so the claim is checkable against a numerical optimum rather than taken on faith:

9.8 Step 8 – The parts assembled: reciprocal recurrent selection

Everything above was one operation at a time. A breeding scheme is those operations in a loop, and the loop for two heterotic pools is reciprocal recurrent selection: each pool is improved using the other pool as tester. Doubled-haploid lines are drawn from pool A, testcrossed to pool B (and vice versa), the testcross hybrids are phenotyped, and the lines with the best mean testcross performance – the GCA estimate of Step 6 – are recombined to found the next cycle’s pool. Elite lines from both pools, at the end, are crossed directly to produce the commercial hybrids. It is exactly the asymmetry Chapter 23 argues for: selection happens on the hybrid, recycling happens within pool.

9.8.1 Founding two pools with a real coalescent history

This is the one place the chapter pays for runMacs2() rather than quickHaplo(). AlphaSimR::runMacs2(..., split = ) draws founder haplotypes from a population-split coalescent history: one ancestral population, then two demes of equal size that stopped exchanging migrants split generations ago, with the first half of the sampled individuals from one deme and the second half from the other. That is the realistic analogue of simulate_lines()’s Balding-Nichols draw in Chapter 6, and between-pool divergence is the entire subject of Chapter 3, so a stylised substitute would not do. Two things about it must be said before any number is read. The founders are outbred: runMacs2() is called without inbred = TRUE, so each pool is a sample of a random-mating population, heterozygous at the fraction of sites given in Table 9.17 (heterozygous_fraction), as an open-pollinated maize population would be; the inbred lines of the scheme are made from them by makeDH() inside each cycle. And there is no separate marker panel: every one of the 150 segregating sites per chromosome is a QTL, so the sites that carry the trait are the ones pullSegSiteGeno() reads for X, f and fL throughout. alphasimr_gd() is 1 - mean(f), the exact definition gene_diversity() uses, computed from that pull, so the two pools are already on the same scale as every diversity number in the rest of the book.

Because these founders come from MaCS, no number in the rest of this section is reproducible from its seed – see Step 1: the founders move between runs, and everything below is computed from them. The chapter’s response is to replicate every Step 8 measurement over independent founder draws and report the spread.

What split does. The bridge’s default is split_gen = 100. The sweep of Table 9.18 draws 20 independent founder sets at each of four split times and measures three things on each: the partition of Caballero and Toro (2002) (Chapter 3) over the two pools (ct_partition(), the general form of gd_partition(): their \(F\)-statistics and the partition \(GD_T = GD_{WI} + GD_{BI} + GD_{BS}\), from which \(F_{ST} = GD_{BS} / GD_T\) follows as an identity derived in Chapter 3), the share of expected heterosis that the divergence term carries (heterosis_partition(), Chapter 5), and – the Step 6 question with divergence – how much of a DH line’s exact GCA against the other pool’s DH lines its own genetic value can see. For that last one, 10 DH lines are drawn from every founder, the full factorial between the two DH sets is made, and the GCA of each pool-A line is split into its additive and dominance parts with heterosis_gca_sca(); the identity behind that split was asserted in Step 6. The chunk also records, per draw, an ascertainment bookkeeping that the reading of GD_T will need: the fraction of sites monomorphic within each pool, the fraction fixed for opposite alleles in the two pools, and the part of GD_T those sites carry. Table 9.19 then tests the two GCA statistics between the extreme split times.

split_draw <- function(split, seed) {
  cfg <- utils::modifyList(rrs_cfg, list(split_gen = split, seed = seed))
  fp_s <- alphasimr_founder_pools(cfg)
  SP_s <- fp_s[["SP"]]
  pool <- rep(c("A", "B"), c(cfg[["n_pool_A"]], cfg[["n_pool_B"]]))
  geno <- pullSegSiteGeno(c(fp_s[["pop_A"]], fp_s[["pop_B"]]), simParam = SP_s)
  ct   <- ct_partition(geno, pool)
  pA_s <- colMeans(geno[pool == "A", ]) / 2
  pB_s <- colMeans(geno[pool == "B", ]) / 2
  d_s  <- SP_s$traits[[1]]@domEff
  a_s  <- SP_s$traits[[1]]@addEff
  hp   <- heterosis_partition(pA_s, pB_s, d_s)
  # Ascertainment: runMacs2() returns sites that segregate in the POOLED
  # sample, so a site fixed for different alleles in the two pools is kept
  # and, with equal pool sizes, sits at pooled frequency 1/2 -- the maximum
  # of 2 p q. GD_T = mean 2 pbar qbar exactly (census weights, equal sizes),
  # so it splits into the part those fixed differences carry and the rest.
  mono_A <- pA_s %in% c(0, 1)
  mono_B <- pB_s %in% c(0, 1)
  fixed  <- mono_A & mono_B & (pA_s != pB_s)
  pbar_s <- (pA_s + pB_s) / 2
  h_s    <- 2 * pbar_s * (1 - pbar_s)
  stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(ct[["diversity"]][["GD_T"]], mean(h_s))),
            isTRUE(all.equal(ct[["global"]][["F_ST"]],
                             ct[["diversity"]][["GD_BS"]] / ct[["diversity"]][["GD_T"]])))
  set.seed(seed)
  dh_A <- makeDH(fp_s[["pop_A"]], nDH = n_dh_gca, simParam = SP_s)
  dh_B <- makeDH(fp_s[["pop_B"]], nDH = n_dh_gca, simParam = SP_s)
  hyb  <- hybridCross(dh_A, dh_B, crossPlan = "testcross", simParam = SP_s)
  gca  <- tapply(gv(hyb)[, 1], hyb@mother, mean)[dh_A@id] - as.numeric(meanG(hyb))[1]
  U_s  <- pullQtlGeno(dh_A, simParam = SP_s) / 2
  hg   <- heterosis_gca_sca(U_s, pullQtlGeno(dh_B, simParam = SP_s) / 2, d_s)
  # GCA = additive part + dominance part (the Step 6 identity), so its
  # variance is var(add) + var(dom) + 2 cov(add, dom): three terms, not two.
  gca_add <- as.numeric(sweep(U_s, 2, colMeans(U_s)) %*% a_s)
  gca_dom <- as.numeric(hg[["gca_A"]])
  stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(as.numeric(gca), gca_add + gca_dom)))
  data.frame(split = split, seed = seed,
             F_ST  = ct[["global"]][["F_ST"]],
             GD_T  = ct[["diversity"]][["GD_T"]],
             GD_BS = ct[["diversity"]][["GD_BS"]],
             mono_A = mean(mono_A), mono_B = mean(mono_B),
             fixed_diff = mean(fixed),
             GD_T_fixed = sum(h_s[fixed]) / length(h_s),
             GD_T_other = sum(h_s[!fixed]) / length(h_s),
             maf_pooled = mean(pmin(pbar_s, 1 - pbar_s)),
             heterosis_divergence_share = hp[["divergence"]] / hp[["total"]],
             GCA_var = stats::var(gca),
             GCA_var_add = stats::var(gca_add),
             GCA_var_dom = stats::var(gca_dom),
             GCA_cov2 = 2 * stats::cov(gca_add, gca_dom),
             GCA_dominance_share = stats::var(gca_dom) / stats::var(gca),
             cor_perse_GCA = stats::cor(gv(dh_A)[, 1], gca))
}

Table 9.18 (c) is the bookkeeping behind that rise. Across the same range the fraction of sites monomorphic within pool A goes from 0.071 to 0.561 (pool B: 0.067 to 0.557), the fraction of sites fixed for opposite alleles in the two pools from 0 to 0.18, the part of GD_T those sites carry (GD_T_fixed) from 0 to 0.09, and the part carried by every other site (GD_T_other) falls from 0.31 to 0.24.

What is inferred from Table 9.18 (a) to Table 9.18 (c): runMacs2() returns the sites that segregate in the pooled sample of the two demes, so a site fixed for opposite alleles in the two pools is kept and, with equal pool sizes, sits at pooled frequency one half – the maximum of \(2\bar p \bar q\), which is exactly what GD_T averages over sites (asserted in the chunk). The rise in GD_T therefore comes entirely from sites fixed for opposite alleles, an ascertainment effect of how the founder sites are sampled, while diversity at every other site falls with divergence. At those other sites the reading is the one Chapter 5 gives for expected heterosis: drift moves diversity from within pools to between them and does not create it. How much of GD_T a marker panel shows under divergence therefore depends on how its sites were ascertained, which is a property of the panel and not of the pools.

Table 9.18 (d) and Table 9.18 (e) are the combining-ability side, mean and standard deviation, and Table 9.19 is the contrast between the two extreme split times, unpaired because the draws are independent. GCA is the sum of its additive and dominance parts (asserted in the chunk), so its variance is \(\mathrm{var}(\mathrm{add}) + \mathrm{var}(\mathrm{dom}) + 2\,\mathrm{cov}(\mathrm{add}, \mathrm{dom})\), three terms. What was measured, from split = 0 to split = 1000: GCA_var goes from 0.536 to 0.384, GCA_var_add from 0.503 to 0.3, GCA_var_dom from 0.05 to 0.074, and GCA_cov2 (\(2\,\mathrm{cov}\)) from -0.017 to 0.01, the last with between-draw standard deviations of 0.069 and 0.058; its largest \(|\text{mean}|/\text{s.e.}\) across splits is 2.36, against the two-sided 5 % point \(t_{0.975,\,19} =\) 2.09. The ratio GCA_dominance_share \(= \mathrm{var}(\mathrm{dom}) / \mathrm{var}(\mathrm{GCA})\) rises from 0.094 to 0.204 (t = 7.4). Which part drives it: holding the dominance part at its split = 0 value and moving only the additive part to its split = 1000 value gives a ratio of 0.149; holding the additive part and moving only the dominance part gives 0.132. And the per se-versus-GCA correlation falls from 0.954 to 0.894 (t = -7.1). Both correlations are below the 0.997 of Step 6’s undifferentiated halves, but that comparison crosses a change of founders, trait draw and line number, so the within-sweep contrast is the one that isolates split. The split = 0 row is the frequency-spectrum route of Step 6 without divergence: its pooled minor-allele frequency is 0.229, so the mean of \(|1 - 2\bar p_k|\) over sites, which equals \(1 - 2\,\)maf_pooled exactly, is 0.542, against 0.098 for Step 6’s two halves.

What is inferred: in this render the fall of the additive part of the GCA variance accounts for more of the rise in the ratio than the growth of the dominance part; an additive part that falls with divergence does so because the sites that fix within a pool stop contributing to it (at linkage equilibrium its variance over DH lines is \(\sum_k a_k^2\, p_{Ak} q_{Ak}\)), so the ratio can rise without the dominance part growing at all. The ratio is a partition of \(\mathrm{var}(\mathrm{GCA})\) only insofar as the covariance term is negligible. In this render that term is beyond the two-sided 5 % point at split = 10; with 20 draws, a term within that point is not shown to differ from zero, which is not a bound on its size. In magnitude, at split = 0 \(|2\,\mathrm{cov}|\) is 0.34 times GCA_var_dom, and dropping it changes the ratio of the split = 0 means from 0.093 to \(\mathrm{var}(\mathrm{dom}) / [\mathrm{var}(\mathrm{add}) + \mathrm{var}(\mathrm{dom})] =\) 0.09. “Share” is used only in that qualified sense, and each founder draw calls runMacs2(), whose coalescent set.seed() does not control, so that verdict is this render’s and may change with the next. Whether the dominance part itself grows with divergence is a separate question, and the algebra does not fix its sign: at linkage equilibrium its variance over DH lines is \(\sum_k d_k^2 (1 - 2 p_{Bk})^2\, p_{Ak} q_{Ak}\), and divergence pushes the weight \((1 - 2 p_{Bk})^2\) up at the same loci where it pushes \(p_{Ak} q_{Ak}\) down. Measured, GCA_var_dom changes by 0.025 from split = 0 to split = 1000 (t = 2.2, against the conservative point \(t_{0.975,\,19} =\) 2.09), so in this render that change is beyond that point; being computed from MaCS founders, it is this render’s verdict and not a property of the scheme. The ratio’s own contrast is beyond the same point in this render; where it is beyond it and positive, what it supports is that a larger share, in the qualified sense above, of \(\mathrm{var}(\mathrm{GCA})\) is invisible to a line’s own genetic value after the split, through whichever part the decomposition above identifies, and nothing here separates how much of the between-draw spread is the trait drawn with each founder set and how much is the DH sampling. The limit of the inference is the scale: 12 individuals per pool, 750 sites, 20 draws per split, and a split in coalescent generations that is not calibrated to any named heterotic-pool pair. No other scale was simulated, so whether any of these directions holds at another one is not claimed.

alphasimr_rrs_cycle() runs the same six steps for each pool, symmetrically (pool A tested on B, pool B tested on A), and every one is a primitive from above:

  1. Double haploids (Step 5). makeDH() draws 3 fully homozygous lines from each individual in the current pool – at book scale, 12 parents x 3 DH each = 36 DH lines per pool, per cycle. Because the pool is outbred, the DH lines from one parent differ from each other: this is the lottery of Step 4, drawn one generation earlier than in a line programme.
  2. Pick reciprocal testers. The first 2 individuals of the current opposite pool are used as testers – an arbitrary slice by index, not a random or merit-based draw, outbred individuals rather than inbred lines, and a different set each cycle because step 6 recreates the pool. This is the RRS-defining move: a line’s value is judged by its cross onto the other heterotic pool, not by itself.
  3. Testcross and phenotype (Step 6). hybridCross() forms the full factorial between every DH line and every tester – 36 DH x 2 testers = 72 testcross hybrids per pool – and setPheno() adds phenotyping error sized to the heritability set by SP$setVarE(h2 = ), at one location.
  4. GCA estimate (Step 6). Testcross phenotypes are grouped by DH-line parent (tapply(pheno[, 1], mother, mean)) and averaged across the 2 testers each line was crossed to. Only trait 1 is used for this ranking even though this chapter’s config simulates 2 traits per line; the second trait rides along in X/hybrids but plays no role in selection here. Step 7 is what it would take to use both.
  5. Keep the elites (Step 6). The 6 DH lines per pool with the highest mean testcross phenotype are kept as elite_A/elite_B.
  6. Recombine (Step 5). randCross() random-mates the elites back up to 12 individuals, founding the next cycle’s pool A (symmetrically for B).

elite_A/elite_B are what step 6 mates – and, in the last cycle, what the final commercial cross uses instead of being recombined again. The cycle’s summary mixes two different populations on purpose: mean_tc_A/mean_tc_B average over all DH lines tested that cycle (the population’s testcross performance before selection), while gd_A/gd_B are gene diversity of only the 6 surviving elites (the population after selection). That asymmetry is exactly what Figure 9.1 plots – response measured pre-selection, diversity measured post-selection.

9.8.3 The trajectory across cycles, replicated, against a paired control

alphasimr_pipeline() runs alphasimr_rrs_cycle() in a loop for 4 cycles, carrying pop_A/pop_B forward from one cycle’s output to the next’s input. Only the last cycle’s elite_A/elite_B go on to found the commercial hybrid pool; every earlier cycle’s elites are spent entirely on randCross()ing the next cycle’s pool.

One run of that loop is an anecdote, and it would be an anecdote about one founder draw. The loop is written out here instead, so that three things can be added to it: a paired no-selection control (random_elite = TRUE, which draws the elites at random and leaves every other operation – the DH lines, the testcrosses, the recombination – the same operations, so the two arms differ by selection in expectation; because the random pick calls sample() and the selected pick does not, the arms’ random streams part at the cycle-1 elite pick, and from there on their Mendelian sampling and phenotyping errors are independent realisations, which the paired t below counts as noise), the between-pool partition of the recombined pools each cycle, and the genetic value of the actual product, the elite factorial.

run_rrs <- function(fp, cfg, random_elite = FALSE, seed = 1) {
  set.seed(seed)
  SP_r  <- fp[["SP"]]
  pop_A <- fp[["pop_A"]]; pop_B <- fp[["pop_B"]]
  pool  <- rep(c("A", "B"), c(cfg[["n_pool_A"]], cfg[["n_pool_B"]]))
  pools_row <- function(pop_A, pop_B) {
    ct <- ct_partition(pullSegSiteGeno(c(pop_A, pop_B), simParam = SP_r), pool)
    data.frame(gd_pool_A = alphasimr_gd(pop_A, SP_r),
               gd_pool_B = alphasimr_gd(pop_B, SP_r),
               GD_BS = ct[["diversity"]][["GD_BS"]],
               F_ST  = ct[["global"]][["F_ST"]])
  }
  rows <- list(cbind(cycle = 0, mean_tc_A = NA, mean_tc_B = NA, gd_A = NA, gd_B = NA,
                     hybrid_meanG = NA, pools_row(pop_A, pop_B)))
  for (i in seq_len(cfg[["n_cycles"]])) {
    cy <- alphasimr_rrs_cycle(pop_A, pop_B, SP_r, cfg, random_elite = random_elite)
    pop_A <- cy[["pop_A"]]; pop_B <- cy[["pop_B"]]
    prod  <- hybridCross(cy[["elite_A"]], cy[["elite_B"]], crossPlan = "testcross",
                         simParam = SP_r)
    rows[[i + 1]] <- cbind(cycle = i, cy[["summary"]],
                           hybrid_meanG = as.numeric(meanG(prod))[1],   # trait 1
                           pools_row(pop_A, pop_B))
  }
  do.call(rbind, rows)
}

Pooling the two pools’ differences is not free noise reduction, and Table 9.22 (b) (pool_tab) measures what it buys. Averaging halves the variance only if the two pools’ paired differences are independent across founder draws. For the elite-line gene diversity they are correlated at 0.03, and the pooled standard deviation, 0.02, is close to the 0.02 it would be if they were independent: pooling does what it should there. For the testcross mean they are correlated at 0.85, and the pooled standard deviation is 1.02 against 0.75 if independent: pooling does not reduce that noise. The construction of the two statistics accounts for the correlation without any appeal to the founder draw: mean_tc_A is the mean of pool-A lines on testers from the current pool B and mean_tc_B the mean of pool-B lines on testers from the current pool A, so both are estimates of the same A x B hybrid level, and progress in either pool raises both (better lines in one statistic, better testers in the other). How much of the correlation is that and how much is the founder draw is not separated here. The gd_A/gd_B and gd_pool_A/gd_pool_B rows are per pool and show the asymmetry between two exchangeable pools that any one set of 48 draws produces; the mean_tc_A/mean_tc_B rows are not per-pool responses, for the reason just given. Table 9.23 says how much of the pools’ loss is selection: the control loses gene diversity too – 6 elites recombined into 12 individuals is a bottleneck whether or not the elites were chosen – and the selected arm’s mean loss exceeds the control’s, with the pooled t just stated as the evidence.

What is inferred: with real meiosis and a dominance trait behind it, the A x B hybrid level responds to reciprocal recurrent selection – both testcross means and the elite factorial’s genetic value measure it – and at this replication that response is beyond t_crit. How much of it each pool contributes is not identified by these statistics: each testcross mean carries the progress of both pools, so a response in pool A alone would raise mean_tc_A and mean_tc_B together. Whether it spends within-pool diversity faster than the same bottleneck under drift alone is what the pooled gene-diversity t decides, and in this render it does for the elite lines and does for the recombined pools. Per founder draw, the ratio of the mean elite-line selection component to its paired standard deviation is 0.4, so a single draw does not settle its sign; the 48-draw t of -2.9 against t_crit = 2.01 is what decides it. Where the sign holds, the control isolates that component from the drift part, which is the distinction Chapter 23 draws with the package’s own faster simulator over a longer trajectory and a grid of selection intensities, and Chapter 22 draws a third time in an inbred-line programme. The between-pool rows: the GD_BS difference is beyond t_crit; where it is, and positive, RRS is moving the pools apart faster than drift does, which is what a scheme selecting on the cross is expected to do. F_ST \(= GD_{BS} / GD_T\) moves with its denominator as well, and GD_T of the recombined pools is not recorded in Table 9.22 (a), so GD_BS is the row read for divergence. What limits the inference is the scale – 12 founders per pool, 4 cycles, 2 testers at one location, 48 draws – and the fact that the founders come from MaCS, so both the numbers in this paragraph and any verdict that rests on a t near t_crit may differ between renders.

9.9 Assumptions, and what this adds over Chapter 6

What is more realistic here. Actual recombination and linkage (a genetic map per chromosome, not independent markers), a trait with directional dominance so that testcross selection has something real to select on and heterosis and inbreeding depression have a value, outbred founder pools with a coalescent split behind them, and multi-generation recurrent selection with a paired control rather than one static pool.

What is declared, and where. Steps 2–6 use inbred founders from quickHaplo() with every site a QTL and dominance degrees drawn at meanDD = 0.4, varDD = 0.1; the “pools” of Steps 4 and 6 are two undifferentiated halves of one founder set. Step 7 uses a separate additive founder draw. Step 8 uses outbred founders from runMacs2(split = ), again with every site a QTL; its DH lines are fully inbred, its testers are outbred individuals, and every loss of diversity is measured against the same run’s founders (cycle 0) and against the paired no-selection control – not against a random-subset null of the same size, which is the reference Chapter 16’s alpha uses.

What is still simplified. Testers are the first 2 individuals of the current opposite pool, an arbitrary slice by index – not chosen at random or by merit, and not the elite inbred tester a real programme would use; because randCross() recreates each pool every cycle, the testers change from cycle to cycle, so successive cycles’ testcross means are not on a common tester and are comparable only through the pools’ genetic level; there is one phenotyping environment (setPheno()’s error variance, no genotype-by-environment term – Chapter 22 adds one); the population-split coalescent history is a stylised divergence process, not calibrated to a named heterotic-pool pair; doubled-haploid production is instantaneous, skipping the field seasons Step 5 counted; and selection acts on trait 1 only – the 2 traits this chapter’s config simulates are all carried through X/hybrids, but only the first ever enters the GCA ranking. The accuracy prediction of Step 6 is first order in the sample correlation – its finite-tester-pool and sample-variance corrections are applied, its second-order term is not – and the selfing trajectory of Step 5 follows 400 parallel lineages from one cross, checked at seven generations.

Scale. This chapter runs live at a book scale (12 + 12 founders, 750 markers, 4 cycles, 48 paired founder draws) – AlphaSimR is fast enough at this size that no caching is needed, unlike the marker-assisted backcrossing (MABC) scans in Chapter 8. Step 8’s numbers may change on re-execution and do change between sessions, because its founders come from MaCS (Step 1); a mean over draws moves by the order of its standard error (the between-draw standard deviation above over the square root of the number of draws), and so may a conclusion that rests on a t near t_crit, which is why the chapter writes each such inference conditionally on the render’s own t. A heavier, production-scale run is inst/scripts/run_alphasimr.R:

Rscript inst/scripts/run_alphasimr.R --test          # smoke run
Rscript inst/scripts/run_alphasimr.R --out=DIR        # full run

It runs alphasimr_pipeline() at a larger configuration, feeds the resulting hybrid pool through stage2_select(), and writes the cycle trajectory and selection metrics as CSVs.
