7  Simulating a hybrid breeding programme: AlphaSimR from the ground up

Chapter 4 built the {X, f, hybrids, fL} contract with a deliberately fast simulator: Balding-Nichols allele frequencies, independent markers, no recombination, no dominance. That is enough to exercise the diversity and selection machinery, but it is not how a hybrid breeding programme is actually run, and it is not how anyone would build one from scratch.

This chapter builds one. The crop in mind is maize, the canonical allogamous hybrid crop: two heterotic pools, inbred (doubled-haploid) lines drawn from each, lines judged by their testcrosses onto the other pool rather than by themselves, pools improved by reciprocal recurrent selection (RRS; Comstock et al. (1949)), and a commercial product that is a single cross between one line from each pool. Steps 1 to 7 are a step-by-step construction with AlphaSimR (Gaynor et al. (2021)), written out in the primitive calls – runMacs(), SimParam, addTraitAD(), makeCross(), hybridCross(), makeDH(), setPheno(), selectInd() – with a plant breeder’s reading of each argument and a measurement at each step. Step 8 assembles those primitives into RRS, replicates it against a paired no-selection control, and shows that the same stage-1 contract comes out the other end, so stage2_select() runs on it exactly as it does on the book’s own simulated data.

Why this machinery, and why it has no counterpart in a self-pollinated programme. In an autogamous (self-pollinated) crop – wheat, rice, soybean, common bean – recombination happens once, in the F1, and the programme then narrows a segregating population toward homozygosity by selfing or single-seed descent, selecting throughout on a line’s own (per se) phenotype, because a self-pollinated genotype breeds true and its own performance is what a farmer will grow: the released variety is very often the same homozygous line the breeder selected. Chapter 8 builds exactly that programme with the same AlphaSimR primitives – makeDH(), setPheno(), selectInd() – and is the chapter to read this one against, step by step. None of Steps 2, 4 and 6 below would have a job there: there is no dominance for per se selection to be blind to (an inbred line’s value is its additive merit alone – Step 3 makes this an identity, varD = 0 exactly on a homozygous panel), no tester to cross a candidate onto, no general or specific combining ability to estimate (Sprague and Tatum (1942)), and no second, deliberately divergent population to keep apart, because there is only one gene pool, not two heterotic ones held apart on purpose. What follows instead is specific to an allogamous, hybrid-product crop: the inbred line is not the product, the F1 is (Step 5); a line’s breeding value depends on which pool it will be crossed to, not on itself alone (Step 6’s tester dependence); and the trait improvement a programme has made is realised only in a cross remade every season, never in either parental line by itself.

The pedagogical source is the AlphaSimR course of Gorjanc and colleagues (Gorjanc et al. 2022): its vignettes on simulating DNA, simulating traits, the DNA lottery, single- and multi-trait selection, and a plant breeding programme. The course has no hybrid programme of its own; the wheat programme it does build is the subject of Chapter 8. This chapter takes the course’s primitives and assembles them into the hybrid case.

The order matters. Everything in Step 8 is a wrapper in R/13_alphasimr_bridge.R – rrs_config, alphasimr_founder_pools(), alphasimr_rrs_cycle(), alphasimr_make_hybrids(), alphasimr_to_stage1(), alphasimr_pipeline() – and a wrapper is only readable once you know what it is wrapping. AlphaSimR is a Suggests dependency, guarded exactly like quadprog and highs elsewhere in the package; this chapter and Chapter 8 are the only ones that need it.

7.1 Step 1 – Founder genomes: what a founder is, and which engine to use

Every AlphaSimR simulation starts with founder haplotypes. Before choosing an engine, it is worth seeing what one produces, at a size small enough to read. inbred = TRUE makes every founder fully homozygous, which is what a panel of inbred lines is.

Alleles are coded 0 and 1 – ancestral and derived under a coalescent engine, arbitrary labels under quickHaplo(). The two assertions fix the coding for the rest of the book. AlphaSimR’s dosage is 0/1/2; the contract of Chapter 4 codes the same information as 0/0.5/1, the within-individual frequency of the reference allele (AlphaSimR’s derived allele plays that role), and every bridge function divides by two on the way across (stage1_build() also detects and rescales 0/1/2 on its own). On an inbred individual the molecular self-coancestry of Chapter 1 is exactly 1, which is the second assertion.

There are two engines, and the choice is not cosmetic. runMacs() runs a backward-in-time coalescent (Chen et al. (2009)) under a species-specific demography – species = "MAIZE", "WHEAT", "CATTLE" – so the founders carry a realistic allele-frequency spectrum and realistic linkage disequilibrium. quickHaplo() samples 0s and 1s directly: instant, and with no history behind it.

Read the last two columns together. runMacs() is not reproducible from set.seed(), and R’s own random stream is untouched by that. runMacs() draws a seed for MaCS from R’s stream (one sample.int() per chromosome) and hands it to the binary. The fourth column, asserted in the chunk, says that after two calls from the same seed R’s .Random.seed is identical: the amount of R’s stream the call consumes is fixed, and every draw R makes afterwards is the same in both runs. Yet the third column says the haplotypes differ. What was measured, then, is that MaCS’s output is not a deterministic function of the seed it is handed. The only thing that moves between two runs is the founder haplotypes themselves – and everything computed from those founders. quickHaplo() is pure R sampling and is exactly reproducible.

(The seconds_per_call column is wall clock and will differ on your machine and between renders. runMacs() takes seconds; quickHaplo(), timed over 100 calls because one call is below the clock’s resolution, takes about 0.25 ms per call.)

WarningThe founders are not seeded, and everything downstream is

This is the single most expensive trap in AlphaSimR, because it fails silently: the code looks reproducible, runs without complaint, and returns different numbers. The non-reproducibility is documented in alphasimr_founder_pools()’s help page for runMacs2(); the table above shows it is not runMacs2-specific but true of MaCS generally.

Two consequences, both because the founders move and not because of anything R’s stream does:

  1. Found once, then branch. Any experiment comparing two schemes must draw the founders a single time and start both arms from that one object. Draw them twice and the two arms start from different founders, and the comparison measures the founders as much as the schemes. Chapter 20’ linked arm is built this way, and so is Step 8’s paired control below.
  2. Save what you cannot reseed. saveRDS(founderGenomes, ...) is the only way to get the same coalescent history back in a later session.

Everything after the founders – the crosses, the doubled haploids, the phenotyping error, the recombination – is controlled by set.seed(), given the founders. Reseeding downstream does not recover the founders and is not needed for anything else.

So what does quickHaplo() give up? Linkage disequilibrium among the founders. The honest way to measure that is against the sampling floor: with n individuals, two completely independent loci still show r^2 of about 1 / (n - 1) by chance alone.

ldstat <- function(fg) {
  SPx <- SimParam$new(fg)
  g <- pullSegSiteGeno(newPop(fg, simParam = SPx), simParam = SPx)
  g <- g[, apply(g, 2, stats::sd) > 0, drop = FALSE]
  chr <- sub("_.*$", "", colnames(g))           # "chr_site" column names
  r <- stats::cor(g)
  adj <- diag(r[-nrow(r), -1])                  # consecutive columns ...
  adj <- adj[chr[-length(chr)] == chr[-1]]      # ... on the same chromosome
  c(adjacent = mean(adj^2),                     # neighbouring sites, same chromosome
    overall  = mean(r[upper.tri(r)]^2),         # all pairs, mostly distant
    noise    = 1 / (nrow(g) - 1))               # what pure chance gives
}
set.seed(3); lq <- ldstat(quickHaplo(nInd = 100, nChr = 5, segSites = 200,
                                     inbred = TRUE))
set.seed(3); lm_ <- ldstat(runMacs(nInd = 100, nChr = 5, segSites = 200,
                                   inbred = TRUE, species = "MAIZE"))
fmt(rbind(quickHaplo = lq, runMacs = lm_))
adjacent overall noise
quickHaplo 0.0103 0.0101 0.0101
runMacs 0.1255 0.0120 0.0101

adjacent is the mean r^2 between neighbouring sites on the same chromosome (pairs that straddle a chromosome boundary are excluded); overall is over all pairs, most of them on different chromosomes. quickHaplo()’s adjacent-site r^2 sits on the noise floor: there is no founder LD at all, as designed. runMacs()’s is 12 times the floor in this render, and its overall r^2 is barely above it – so the LD it creates is local, decaying with map distance, which is what real LD does.

The runMacs row of that table is itself not reproducible, for the reason the row above it establishes; the quickHaplo row is, to the last digit.

What that does and does not imply for the rest of the chapter. R’s stream is unaffected by MaCS, so any step that draws its own quickHaplo() founders from a fresh set.seed() is exactly reproducible, and so would be a step that did not reseed: the runMacs() calls above leave the stream exactly where a fixed number of sample.int() calls leaves it. What is not reproducible is anything computed from MaCS founders – the runMacs rows of both tables here, and all of Step 8. The chapter still calls set.seed() immediately before each step’s own founder draw rather than once at the top, so that each step is reproducible on its own and not only as part of this chapter’s execution order. With that in place, two independent executions agree on every number in Steps 1 to 7 except the wall-clock seconds and the runMacs rows.

That measurement decides the policy this chapter follows:

  • quickHaplo(inbred = TRUE) for Steps 2–7 and all of Chapter 8. They teach the arithmetic of dominance, heterosis and response, where founder LD is not the point, and in exchange every number printed is reproducible from a clean clone. AlphaSimR still applies a real genetic map and real meiosis to every descendant, so linkage and the Bulmer effect of Chapter 20 still operate – only the founders’ history is stylised.
  • runMacs2(split = ) in Step 8. There the divergence between two heterotic pools is the object of study, and no amount of speed compensates for not having it. Every Step 8 result is therefore replicated over founder draws.

Founder LD does matter where marker-QTL association is the mechanism, which is genomic prediction – see Chapter 14, and note that the accuracies there are computed on a different simulator for this reason.

7.2 Step 2 – SimParam, and defining a trait with dominance

SimParam holds everything global: the genetic map, which sites are QTL, the trait effects, the error variances. It is an R6 object, so its methods are called with $, and a variable named SP is picked up automatically by every AlphaSimR function that needs it.

A hybrid programme exists because of dominance: without it a line’s cross performance is its own breeding value and there is nothing a testcross can see that the line per se could not (Chapter 10). So the trait defined here, and used through Step 6, carries a dominance term from the start. This is exactly the reason an autogamous programme has no equivalent of this step: there, dominance is invisible by construction (a released, self-pollinated variety is homozygous, so no heterozygote is ever evaluated or sold) and SP$addTraitA() – additive effects only – is the whole model, the case Chapter 8 runs throughout.

# Seeded here, not once at the top of the chapter: Step 1 draws from R's random
# stream (each runMacs() call consumes a fixed amount of it), so everything
# downstream would move with any edit or re-execution of Step 1 unless the seed
# is reset immediately before the draw that matters.
set.seed(101)
founders <- quickHaplo(nInd = 60, nChr = 10, segSites = 100, inbred = TRUE)
SP <- SimParam$new(founders)
SP$addTraitAD(nQtlPerChr = 100, mean = 10, var = 1,
              meanDD = rrs_cfg[["mean_dd"]], varDD = rrs_cfg[["var_dd"]])
SP$setVarE(h2 = 0.4)

7.2.1 The dominance model, as AlphaSimR writes it

For a diploid with QTL \(k = 1, \dots, m\), let \(x_{ik} \in \{0, 1, 2\}\) be the dosage of the derived allele of individual \(i\) at QTL \(k\) (the column of pullQtlGeno()). AlphaSimR’s TraitAD genetic value is

\[G_i \;=\; \mu_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{m} a_k\,(x_{ik} - 1) + \sum_{k=1}^{m} d_k\,\mathbb{1}[x_{ik} = 1]\]

with \(\mu_0\) an intercept, \(a_k \in \mathbb{R}\) the additive effect (half the difference between the two homozygotes, on the classical scale of Falconer and Mackay (1996), Ch. 7, which puts the origin at the midpoint of the two homozygotes and assigns them \(+a\) and \(-a\) and the heterozygote \(d\); no equation number is attributed here, the scale is stated in full) and \(d_k \in \mathbb{R}\) the dominance deviation, the departure of the heterozygote from that midpoint. It is the model of Chapter 10 with AlphaSimR’s coding, and the chunk checks it rather than trusts it:

tr <- SP$traits[[1]]
a  <- tr@addEff
d  <- tr@domEff
pop0 <- newPop(founders, simParam = SP)
x0   <- pullQtlGeno(pop0, simParam = SP)
gv_by_hand <- as.numeric(tr@intercept + (x0 - 1) %*% a + (x0 == 1) %*% d)
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(gv_by_hand, as.numeric(gv(pop0)))))

meanDD and varDD parameterise the dominance degree \(\delta_k = d_k / |a_k|\): AlphaSimR draws \(\delta_k \sim N(\texttt{meanDD}, \texttt{varDD})\) and sets \(d_k = \delta_k\,|a_k|\), so \(\delta_k = 0\) is additivity, \(\delta_k = 1\) complete dominance, \(\delta_k > 1\) overdominance, and a positive mean is directional dominance: on average the heterozygote sits above the homozygote midpoint, whichever allele is favourable. Directional dominance is what makes heterosis positive and inbreeding depression a loss on the trait scale, and it is the standing hypothesis for yield in maize.

c(n_qtl = length(a), meanDD_requested = rrs_cfg[["mean_dd"]],
  meanDD_realised = mean(d / abs(a)), varDD_requested = rrs_cfg[["var_dd"]],
  varDD_realised = stats::var(d / abs(a))) |> fmt(3)
#>            n_qtl meanDD_requested  meanDD_realised  varDD_requested 
#>         1000.000            0.400            0.378            0.100 
#>   varDD_realised 
#>            0.104

nQtlPerChr sets how polygenic the trait is. Setting it equal to segSites makes every segregating site a QTL, which is what this chapter does throughout – convenient, because then the marker matrix and the causal loci are the same set and there is no marker-versus-QTL gap to reason about. Real programmes have that gap; Chapter 9 is where the book takes it seriously.

mean fixes the mean genetic value of the founders. var fixes their additive variance (useVarA = TRUE, the default), not their total genetic variance – with inbred founders the two coincide, for a reason Step 3 shows. corA (Step 7) sets the genetic correlation between traits.

The environmental variance has two routes, and plant breeders use the second:

  • SP$setVarE(h2 = 0.4) fixes an error variance once, from the founders’ genetic variance (here 1.5), and every later setPheno() reuses it.
  • setPheno(varE = , reps = ) sets it per call. reps is the plant idiom for locations: varE is the plot-level error, and a genotype scored in reps plots has its error variance divided by reps, so the phenotype is an entry mean. That is how a staged programme raises selection accuracy from one stage to the next without ever changing the trait’s heritability – the mechanism Chapter 8 is built on, and the one Step 6 uses for testers.

7.3 Step 3 – The base population, and where the variance components live

newPop() turns founder haplotypes into individuals and, because a trait is now defined, computes their genetic values immediately.

pop <- setPheno(newPop(founders, simParam = SP), simParam = SP)
c(n = pop@nInd, meanG = as.numeric(meanG(pop)), varG = as.numeric(varG(pop)),
  varP = as.numeric(varP(pop)),
  h2_realised = as.numeric(varG(pop) / varP(pop))) |> fmt(3)
#>           n       meanG        varG        varP h2_realised 
#>      60.000      10.000       1.000       2.614       0.383

The realised heritability is near but not equal to the requested 0.4: setVarE() fixes a variance, and the ratio it produces in any finite sample is itself a random variable. This is a general and useful habit – check what the simulation realised, do not assume what you requested.

genParam() returns the full decomposition. Four of its fields are the ones a hybrid programme cares about, and they read differently on a panel of inbred lines and on a random-mating population made from them:

# Among fully homozygous lines the locus value is a (x - 1), so at linkage
# equilibrium varA = sum 4 p q a^2, while genicVarA = sum 2 p q alpha^2 with
# alpha = a + d (q - p): under dominance the LE ratio is close to, not
# exactly, 2. What varA carries above varA_LE is disequilibrium.
p_f      <- colMeans(x0) / 2                        # founder allele frequencies
varA_f   <- vtab[["varA"]][1]
genic_f  <- vtab[["genicVarA"]][1]
varA_LE  <- sum(4 * p_f * (1 - p_f) * a^2)
ratio_LE <- varA_LE / genic_f
varA_LD  <- varA_f - varA_LE
ratio_LD <- varA_LD / genic_f
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(as.numeric(bv(pop, simParam = SP)[, 1]),
                           as.numeric(sweep(x0, 2, 2 * p_f) %*% a))))
c(varA = varA_f, varA_LE = varA_LE, ratio_LE = ratio_LE,
  varA_LD = varA_LD, ratio_LD = ratio_LD) |> fmt(4)
#>     varA  varA_LE ratio_LE  varA_LD ratio_LD 
#>   1.0000   0.8495   1.9875   0.1505   0.3522

varA is the realised additive variance; genicVarA is what it would be if the loci were independent and in Hardy-Weinberg proportions. Their ratio is the whole subject of Chapter 20, and the package’s genic_var() computes the same quantity from a marker matrix directly.

The founders’ ratio of 2.34 has two parts, and the chunk separates them. Among fully homozygous lines the value at QTL \(k\) is \(a_k (x_{ik} - 1)\) with \(x_{ik} \in \{0, 2\}\), and AlphaSimR’s bv() on these founders is exactly \(\sum_k a_k (x_{ik} - 2 p_k)\) (asserted in the chunk). At linkage equilibrium its variance is varA_LE \(= \sum_k 4 p_k q_k a_k^2 =\) 0.8495, against a genic value of 0.4274: a ratio of 1.99, exact given the effects and the frequencies. That part is inbreeding – a homozygous population has per-locus variance 4pq where a Hardy-Weinberg one has 2pq. It is close to 2 and not exactly 2 because genicVarA is written with the Hardy-Weinberg average effect \(\alpha_k = a_k + d_k (q_k - p_k)\) defined below, whereas the homozygous lines carry \(a_k\); twice the genic value equals varA_LE only where \(d_k (q_k - p_k) = 0\) at every locus. The remainder, varA_LD = varA - varA_LE \(=\) 0.1505, or 0.35 in ratio units, is sampling (gametic-phase) disequilibrium among only 60 founders’ worth of gametes. Three rounds of random mating restore Hardy-Weinberg and pull the ratio to 1.06; what remains is that same disequilibrium. A ratio away from 1 in an outbred population is the gametic-phase disequilibrium Chapter 3 defines and Chapter 20 measures; a ratio away from 1 in an inbred one is mostly the homozygosity.

The dominance columns carry the hybrid lesson. varD among the inbred founders is exactly zero (the assertion above): a homozygous line has no heterozygous locus, so its dominance contribution is zero whatever \(d\) is, and no amount of per se evaluation of inbred lines can see dominance. This single identity is the operational reason an autogamous programme stops at per se selection and never needs a testcross: its released lines are exactly this “founders” row, varD = 0 for the whole life of the programme, so per se phenotype already is breeding value up to the additive/genic gap discussed below. After random mating varD appears. Two identities make that quantitative. With \(p_k\) the founders’ frequency of the derived allele at QTL \(k\) and \(q_k = 1 - p_k\), the population mean under Hardy-Weinberg proportions is (Falconer and Mackay 1996, Ch. 7, Eq. 7.2)

\[M_{HW} \;=\; \mu_0 + \sum_k \big[a_k\,(p_k - q_k) + 2\,p_k q_k\,d_k\big]\]

while the inbred panel at the same frequencies has only the first term. The difference, \(2\sum_k d_k p_k q_k\), is the inbreeding depression of Chapter 10 at \(F = 1\), which the package computes as inbreeding_depression(p, d, F_coef = 1). And the genic additive variance is \(\sum_k 2 p_k q_k \alpha_k^2\) with \(\alpha_k = a_k + d_k\,(q_k - p_k)\) the average effect of a gene substitution. Falconer and Mackay (1996) define \(\alpha\) in Ch. 7 and the single-locus additive variance \(2pq\alpha^2\) in Ch. 8; the equation numbers are not attributed here, so both identities are stated in this chapter’s notation: \(\alpha_k = a_k + d_k\,(q_k - p_k)\) and, summed over independent loci, \(\sum_k 2 p_k q_k \alpha_k^2\), which is the genic variance:

It is the same \(d\) that Step 4 turns into heterosis.

f1_sd is zero, asserted to machine precision. Two fully inbred parents are homozygous everywhere, so every gamete each one makes is identical, and every F1 is the same heterozygote. Two hundred F1 plants from a cross of two inbred lines are two hundred copies of one genotype. There is no lottery yet – and this uniformity is the first reason a single-cross hybrid is a saleable variety.

f1_mean sits well above better_parent. That is heterosis, and under the model of Step 2 it has an exact value: for inbred parents \(i\) and \(j\) with allele indicators \(u_{ik}, u_{jk} \in \{0, 1\}\) (their dosage halved), the F1 is heterozygous exactly where they differ, so

\[H_{ij} \;=\; G_{ij} - \tfrac{1}{2}(G_i + G_j) \;=\; \sum_k d_k\,\mathbb{1}[u_{ik} \neq u_{jk}]\]

The additive terms cancel from the mid-parent difference because an inbred parent’s additive contribution is exactly half the F1’s. heterosis_value(X, d) is this sum, with X the F1’s 0/0.5/1 genotype:

Falconer and Mackay (1996), Ch. 14, derive the heterosis of the F1 between two random-mating populations as \(H_{F1} = \sum_k d_k y_k^2\) with \(y_k = p_{Ak} - p_{Bk}\) (stated here in this chapter’s notation rather than by their equation number; it is twice the divergence term of heterosis_partition()). The two do not conflict; they use different mid-parents. Theirs carries each population’s own random-mating heterozygosity; an inbred line carries none, so the whole F1 heterozygosity is heterosis and the expression above keeps the shared term as well – Chapter 10 works the reconciliation through.

The lottery starts one meiosis later. dh_sd is the standard deviation among doubled haploids drawn from that F1, and it is substantial – this is the segregation that F2, DH and RIL populations exist to sample. It is also why Chapter 5 is a chapter: the number of DH lines you pull from a cross is the number of tickets you buy, and no downstream selection stage can recover a combination that was never sampled.

transgressive is the fraction of DH lines exceeding the better parent per se: 0.025, 0.01 and 0.32 for the three crosses. It depends strongly on the cross, and gap_in_dh_sd says why: the DH lines are centred on the mid-parent, so what matters is how far the better parent sits above it in units of the DH standard deviation – 1.95, 2.19 and 0.45. The cross with the smallest gap is the one with the most transgressive lines (asserted), about a third of them; the other two, whose better parent is two DH standard deviations above the mid-parent, give a few percent. Crossing two parents whose favourable alleles sit at different loci produces progeny beyond either of them – transgressive segregation, which Mackay et al. (2021) argue is the same phenomenon as heterosis and inbreeding depression viewed from a third angle – and how often depends on the gap between the parents relative to the segregation variance, not on a constant.

7.5 Step 5 – Selfing, doubled haploids, and why hybrid seed is remade every year

A plant breeder has to get from a heterozygous F1 to a homozygous line. There are two routes, and AlphaSimR implements both. Following the same cross through selfing generations also answers a question every hybrid grower has asked: what happens if the F1 seed is saved and sown again.

This is the one step where the allogamous, hybrid case and the autogamous case use literally the same primitive – makeDH() is exactly what Chapter 8 calls to take its wheat F1s straight to a homozygous line, one generation instead of the six or seven selfing generations traced below – and where they diverge again in what the resulting inbred line is for. In an autogamous programme that homozygous line is commonly the release: it breeds true, so what leaves the DH pipeline is what a farmer eventually grows. In this chapter’s programme the homozygous DH line is a parent, held inside a heterotic pool, testcrossed and recycled (Step 6, Step 8), and never sold on its own; the object a grower plants is the F1 these lines make together, which is the reason it cannot be saved.

Under selfing without selection, the fraction of the F1’s heterozygous loci still heterozygous in generation \(F_t\) is \((1/2)^{t-1}\) in expectation, so the expected mean of the selfed generation is

\[\mathbb{E}[M_{F_t}] \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}(G_i + G_j) + H_{ij}\,(1/2)^{t-1}\]

with \(H_{ij}\) the F1 heterosis of Step 4. At \(t = 2\) this is the classical result that the F2 retains half the F1 heterosis, \(H_{F2} = \tfrac{1}{2} H_{F1}\), which Falconer and Mackay (1996) derive in Ch. 14 for the F2 of a cross between two populations (stated here in this chapter’s notation rather than by their equation number); the form above extends it to later selfed generations by the same argument, since the expected heterozygosity at every locus is halved each generation.

Three columns, three lessons.

homozygosity: self() halves the remaining heterozygosity every generation – the familiar 1 - (1/2)^(t-1) approach to fixation, which never quite arrives. makeDH() gets there in one step, at 1.0 exactly, by doubling a haploid gamete.

meanG against expected: the F2 mean falls by half the heterosis, the F3 by three quarters, and the DH lines land on the mid-parent. Each row is 400 plants, one per F1 plant: self(nProgeny = 1) carries 400 parallel, independent selfing lineages forward, so within a row the 400 values are independent and sdG / sqrt(400) is the standard error of the row’s mean. The check is statistical: z is the observed minus expected mean in units of that standard error, and the chunk asserts every |z| < 4. Across rows the same 400 lineages are followed – each \(F_t\) plant is the selfed progeny of one \(F_{t-1}\) plant – so successive rows are correlated, and the table is 400 lineages checked at seven generations, not seven independent samples. Nothing about the parents changed; the loss is the heterozygosity being halved, times \(d\). This is why hybrid seed is remade from the two inbred parents every season and why a maize F2 crop yields visibly less than the F1 it was saved from.

sdG: the F1 has none and the F2 has plenty. The F2 is not a variety; it is a segregating population, and a uniform, high-yielding one cannot be recovered from it by anything but selection and re-fixation – which is the line development a breeding programme does, not something a grower can do.

The saving DH buys is seasons, and seasons are the currency of a breeding programme: six or seven selfing generations against one DH cycle. Step 8’s scheme uses makeDH() for precisely this reason, and the cost that never shows up in the simulation is that DH production is a laboratory operation with its own failure rate and its own genotype-dependent response.

The other operations a scheme needs are all one call each: randCross() for random mating within a pool, makeCross() for a specified crossing plan, hybridCross() for a full factorial or a testcross between two groups, and mergePops() or c() to combine populations.

7.6 Step 6 – Phenotyping and selection: per se, then on the testcross

Selection needs a criterion. setPheno() supplies the noisy one; selectInd() acts on it. This step does it twice: first on a line’s own phenotype, as the course does (Gorjanc et al. 2022), to check the breeder’s equation; then on the testcross, which is how a hybrid programme actually ranks lines.

Both forms of the breeder’s equation are here, each averaged over 20 independent draws of the phenotypes, the selection and the progeny (the population selected from is the same mixed every time, so varA is fixed and only S, the accuracy and the Mendelian sampling vary). R = S * h2 uses the selection differential directly, with h2 the narrow-sense heritability varA / varP of the population selected from; R = i * r * sigma_A decomposes it into intensity, accuracy and available variation – the form Chapter 3 builds the book’s objective side on. Because the trait has dominance, the accuracy r is the correlation of the phenotype with the breeding value (bv()), not with the total genetic value: only the additive part is transmitted. R_observed is the realised change in genetic mean between the selected parents’ random-mated progeny and the starting population, which is what the prediction is a prediction of.

gap_mean is the paired difference between the observed response and R_S_h2, with gap_sd its standard deviation over the draws. The mean gap is -0.5 percent of the prediction at q = 0.05 and 2.5 percent at q = 0.20; its standard error over the 20 draws is gap_sd / sqrt(20), and the largest gap in units of that standard error is 1.1. A single draw is another matter: R_observed_sd says that one realisation of the response scatters by around 11 percent of the prediction at q = 0.05, which is why the course’s one-off comparison (Gorjanc et al. 2022) “did not match perfectly” and why this one is replicated. The equation predicts the additive response to first order in the allele-frequency change; what it leaves out is second order in that change and the dominance contribution of the progeny’s departure from Hardy-Weinberg proportions, both small with 1000 progeny from 50 or more parents. The empirical intensity agrees with the normal-theory value to within 0.2 percent: with 1000 candidates and a criterion that is a sum over 1000 loci plus normal error, the two assumptions of sel_intensity() – an infinite population and a normal criterion – are nearly met.

7.6.2 Selection on the testcross: general combining ability

A maize breeder does not rank inbred lines on their own yield. A line is crossed to a tester from the opposite pool, the testcross hybrids are grown in yield trials, and the line is ranked on the mean of its testcrosses. This subsection is the one with no autogamous counterpart at all: a self-pollinated variety is judged, released and grown as itself, so selection there stops at the per se phenotype of Step 6’s first half. A hybrid parent is judged by a cross it will never be sold as, which is the entire reason general and specific combining ability exist as concepts. The quantity being estimated is the line’s general combining ability (GCA), the average performance of its crosses expressed as a deviation from the mean of all crosses (Sprague and Tatum (1942)); what a particular cross does beyond the sum of its two parents’ GCAs is the specific combining ability (SCA). Comstock, Robinson and Harvey’s reciprocal recurrent selection (Comstock et al. (1949)) is built on using the opposite pool as the tester so that selection captures both.

On the full factorial of Step 4 the GCA of every A line against pool B can be computed exactly, and under the model of Step 2 it has a closed form. With \(u_{ik}\) the line’s allele indicator and \(\bar G\) the factorial mean,

\[\mathrm{GCA}_i \;=\; \frac{1}{n_B}\sum_{j} G_{ij} - \bar G \;=\; \sum_k \alpha^{(B)}_k\,(u_{ik} - p_{Ak}), \qquad \alpha^{(B)}_k = a_k + d_k\,(1 - 2\,p_{Bk})\]

\(\alpha^{(B)}_k\) is the average effect of substituting the line’s allele into a pool-B background – the same \(\alpha = a + d(q - p)\) of Step 3, with the frequency of the tester pool, not the line’s own pool. That is the algebraic content of the sentence “combining ability is a property of a line against a tester”: change the tester pool and the weights change. Its dominance part, \(\sum_k d_k (1 - 2 p_{Bk})(u_{ik} - p_{Ak})\), is the gca_A of heterosis_gca_sca() (Section 10.10), and the interaction residual of the factorial is that function’s sca:

7.6.3 How many testers, how many locations

A programme cannot cross every candidate to every line of the other pool. It uses \(t\) testers and \(r\) locations, and the estimate of \(\mathrm{GCA}_i\) is the mean phenotype of the line’s \(t\) testcrosses, each an entry mean over \(r\) plots. Writing the testcross phenotype as \(\bar G + \mathrm{GCA}_i + \mathrm{GCA}_j + \mathrm{SCA}_{ij} + e_{ij}\) with \(e_{ij}\) the entry-mean error of variance \(\sigma^2_e / r\), and averaging over the \(t\) testers (whose own GCAs are common to every candidate and drop out of the ranking), the estimate is \(\widehat{\mathrm{GCA}}_i = \mathrm{GCA}_i + \bar s_i + \bar e_i\), with \(\bar s_i\) the mean of the line’s SCA with its \(t\) testers and \(\bar e_i\) the mean error. The statistic the chunk computes is a sample correlation between \(\widehat{\mathrm{GCA}}_i\) and \(\mathrm{GCA}_i\) over the \(n_A =\) 30 fixed candidate lines, with the \(t\) testers drawn without replacement from the \(n_B =\) 30 lines of pool B. To first order its expectation is

\[\rho(t, r) \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{v_g}{v_g + v_s + v_e}}, \qquad v_g = \frac{1}{n_A - 1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_A} \mathrm{GCA}_i^2, \quad v_s = \frac{\sigma^2_{SCA}}{t}\cdot\frac{n_A}{n_A - 1}\cdot\frac{n_B - t}{n_B - 1}, \quad v_e = \frac{\sigma^2_e}{t\,r}\]

where \(v_g\) is the sample variance (\(n_A - 1\) divisor) of the candidates’ true GCA, because the correlation is taken over exactly those \(n_A\) points; \(\sigma^2_{SCA} = \frac{1}{n_A n_B}\sum_{ij} \mathrm{SCA}_{ij}^2\) is the SCA variance on the factorial (computed above), \((n_B - t)/(n_B - 1)\) is the finite-population correction for \(t\) testers drawn without replacement from \(n_B\), \(n_A/(n_A - 1)\) turns the mean of the per-candidate variances of \(\bar s_i\) into the expected sample variance, and \(\sigma^2_e\) is SP$varE, the plot error of Step 2. The textbook form of this prediction replaces \(v_g\) by the population variance of GCA and \(v_s\) by \(\sigma^2_{SCA}/t\): it assumes an infinite tester pool and population variances. It is kept in the table as predicted_infinite, so the two can be compared. Neither corrects the second-order term of a sample correlation (the Jensen term); it is measured directly, not assumed away, in the Monte Carlo chunk below the accuracy grid, and left uncorrected because it is small at this \(n_A\). The simulation measures the thing itself: repeated draws of the tester set and of the field error, at every combination.

Accuracy rises with both, and the two arguments buy different things. In the scheme of Step 8 the testers are 2 individuals of the opposite pool and the trials have one location, which puts it in the low-accuracy corner of this table. gain_trait1 and gain_trait2 are the expected genetic change in each trait per unit of selection intensity on the index, G b / sqrt(b' P b), and aggregate is their a-weighted sum – which equals cor_with_H times the standard deviation of the aggregate genotype, asserted. Three readings, in the order they matter:

  1. Equal weights are not the Smith-Hazel solution. Smith-Hazel puts 0.75 units of weight on trait 2 for every unit on trait 1, not 1.00. The index is b = P^-1 G a (Smith (1936); Hazel (1943)), with no further structure, and b is proportional to a if and only if a is an eigenvector of P^-1 G. A sufficient condition is G = c P – every trait with the same heritability c and the genetic correlations equal to the phenotypic ones – which says nothing about the traits being equally variable or uncorrelated: index_smith_hazel()’s help page gives a correlated-trait pair (G = I, P = [[2, 0.5], [0.5, 2]]) for which equal weights are exact. Here the heritabilities are 0.47 and 0.07 and the genetic and phenotypic correlations -0.42 and -0.08, so G is nowhere near a multiple of P, and the table shows that (1, 1) is not an eigenvector of P^-1 G.
  2. The cost on the objective is modest here; the composition of the gain is not. Equal weighting loses 0.2 percent of the attainable correlation with the aggregate genotype. But it moves trait 1 by 0.011 per unit intensity where Smith-Hazel moves it by 0.044, 4.1 times as much; both indices put most of the aggregate into trait 2, whose variance is four times larger, and the weights decide how much trait 1 moves at all. An index that is nearly as good on the objective can still land the gain somewhere else.
  3. The restricted index holds trait 2 at exactly zero, at a cost of 15 percent of the aggregate. That is Kempthorne and Nordskog’s construction
    1. and, as Chapter 3 argues, the classical ancestor of this book’s alpha constraint – restrict a direction rather than price it.

7.8 Step 8 – The parts assembled: reciprocal recurrent selection

Everything above was one operation at a time. A breeding scheme is those operations in a loop, and the loop for two heterotic pools is reciprocal recurrent selection: each pool is improved using the other pool as tester. Doubled-haploid lines are drawn from pool A, testcrossed to pool B (and vice versa), the testcross hybrids are phenotyped, and the lines with the best mean testcross performance – the GCA estimate of Step 6 – are recombined to found the next cycle’s pool. Elite lines from both pools, at the end, are crossed directly to produce the commercial hybrids. It is exactly the asymmetry Chapter 19 argues for: selection happens on the hybrid, recycling happens within pool.

7.8.1 Founding two pools with a real coalescent history

This is the one place the chapter pays for runMacs2() rather than quickHaplo(). AlphaSimR::runMacs2(..., split = ) draws founder haplotypes from a population-split coalescent history: one ancestral population, then two demes of equal size that stopped exchanging migrants split generations ago, with the first half of the sampled individuals from one deme and the second half from the other. That is the realistic analogue of simulate_lines()’s Balding-Nichols draw in Chapter 4, and between-pool divergence is the entire subject of Chapter 2, so a stylised substitute would not do.

What split does. The bridge’s default is split_gen = 100. The sweep below draws 20 independent founder sets at each of four split times and measures three things on each: the Caballero & Toro partition of Chapter 2 over the two pools (ct_partition(), the general form of gd_partition(): the \(F\)-statistics of their Eq. 6 and the partition \(GD_T = GD_{WI} + GD_{BI} + GD_{BS}\) of their Eq. 7, from which \(F_{ST} = GD_{BS} / GD_T\) follows as an identity derived in Chapter 2, not stated in the paper), the share of expected heterosis that the divergence term carries (heterosis_partition(), Chapter 10), and – the Step 6 question with divergence – how much of a DH line’s exact GCA against the other pool’s DH lines its own genetic value can see. For that last one, 10 DH lines are drawn from every founder, the full factorial between the two DH sets is made, and the GCA of each pool-A line is split into its additive and dominance parts with heterosis_gca_sca(); the identity behind that split was asserted in Step 6. The chunk also records, per draw, an ascertainment bookkeeping that the reading of GD_T will need: the fraction of sites monomorphic within each pool, the fraction fixed for opposite alleles in the two pools, and the part of GD_T those sites carry.

split_draw <- function(split, seed) {
  cfg <- utils::modifyList(rrs_cfg, list(split_gen = split, seed = seed))
  fp_s <- alphasimr_founder_pools(cfg)
  SP_s <- fp_s[["SP"]]
  pool <- rep(c("A", "B"), c(cfg[["n_pool_A"]], cfg[["n_pool_B"]]))
  geno <- pullSegSiteGeno(c(fp_s[["pop_A"]], fp_s[["pop_B"]]), simParam = SP_s)
  ct   <- ct_partition(geno, pool)
  pA_s <- colMeans(geno[pool == "A", ]) / 2
  pB_s <- colMeans(geno[pool == "B", ]) / 2
  d_s  <- SP_s$traits[[1]]@domEff
  a_s  <- SP_s$traits[[1]]@addEff
  hp   <- heterosis_partition(pA_s, pB_s, d_s)
  # Ascertainment: runMacs2() returns sites that segregate in the POOLED
  # sample, so a site fixed for different alleles in the two pools is kept
  # and, with equal pool sizes, sits at pooled frequency 1/2 -- the maximum
  # of 2 p q. GD_T = mean 2 pbar qbar exactly (census weights, equal sizes),
  # so it splits into the part those fixed differences carry and the rest.
  mono_A <- pA_s %in% c(0, 1)
  mono_B <- pB_s %in% c(0, 1)
  fixed  <- mono_A & mono_B & (pA_s != pB_s)
  pbar_s <- (pA_s + pB_s) / 2
  h_s    <- 2 * pbar_s * (1 - pbar_s)
  stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(ct[["diversity"]][["GD_T"]], mean(h_s))))
  set.seed(seed)
  dh_A <- makeDH(fp_s[["pop_A"]], nDH = n_dh_gca, simParam = SP_s)
  dh_B <- makeDH(fp_s[["pop_B"]], nDH = n_dh_gca, simParam = SP_s)
  hyb  <- hybridCross(dh_A, dh_B, crossPlan = "testcross", simParam = SP_s)
  gca  <- tapply(gv(hyb)[, 1], hyb@mother, mean)[dh_A@id] - as.numeric(meanG(hyb))[1]
  U_s  <- pullQtlGeno(dh_A, simParam = SP_s) / 2
  hg   <- heterosis_gca_sca(U_s, pullQtlGeno(dh_B, simParam = SP_s) / 2, d_s)
  # GCA = additive part + dominance part (the Step 6 identity), so its
  # variance is var(add) + var(dom) + 2 cov(add, dom): three terms, not two.
  gca_add <- as.numeric(sweep(U_s, 2, colMeans(U_s)) %*% a_s)
  gca_dom <- as.numeric(hg[["gca_A"]])
  stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(as.numeric(gca), gca_add + gca_dom)))
  data.frame(split = split, seed = seed,
             F_ST  = ct[["global"]][["F_ST"]],
             GD_T  = ct[["diversity"]][["GD_T"]],
             GD_BS = ct[["diversity"]][["GD_BS"]],
             mono_A = mean(mono_A), mono_B = mean(mono_B),
             fixed_diff = mean(fixed),
             GD_T_fixed = sum(h_s[fixed]) / length(h_s),
             GD_T_other = sum(h_s[!fixed]) / length(h_s),
             maf_pooled = mean(pmin(pbar_s, 1 - pbar_s)),
             heterosis_divergence_share = hp[["divergence"]] / hp[["total"]],
             GCA_var = stats::var(gca),
             GCA_var_add = stats::var(gca_add),
             GCA_var_dom = stats::var(gca_dom),
             GCA_cov2 = 2 * stats::cov(gca_add, gca_dom),
             GCA_dominance_share = stats::var(gca_dom) / stats::var(gca),
             cor_perse_GCA = stats::cor(gv(dh_A)[, 1], gca))
}

Read the first two tables together: the first is the mean over the 20 draws, the second their standard deviation. What was measured: F_ST rises from 0.021 at split = 0 to 0.566 at split = 1000, and GD_BS from 0.007 to 0.188, each step many standard deviations wide; the divergence share of expected heterosis rises from 0.021 to 0.361. GD_T does not stay flat: it is 0.312 at split = 0, 0.297 at split = 100 and 0.331 at split = 1000, and the rise from split = 100 to split = 1000, 0.034, is 17 standard errors at this replication. The third table is the bookkeeping behind that rise. Across the same range the fraction of sites monomorphic within pool A goes from 0.066 to 0.561 (pool B: 0.067 to 0.563), the fraction of sites fixed for opposite alleles in the two pools from 0 to 0.187, the part of GD_T those sites carry (GD_T_fixed) from 0 to 0.093, and the part carried by every other site (GD_T_other) falls from 0.312 to 0.238.

What is inferred from those three tables: runMacs2() returns the sites that segregate in the pooled sample of the two demes, so a site fixed for opposite alleles in the two pools is kept and, with equal pool sizes, sits at pooled frequency one half – the maximum of \(2\bar p \bar q\), which is exactly what GD_T averages over sites (asserted in the chunk). The rise in GD_T therefore comes entirely from sites fixed for opposite alleles, an ascertainment effect of how the founder sites are sampled, while diversity at every other site falls with divergence. At those other sites the reading is the one Chapter 10 gives for expected heterosis: drift moves diversity from within pools to between them and does not create it. How much of GD_T a marker panel shows under divergence therefore depends on how its sites were ascertained, which is a property of the panel and not of the pools.

The fourth and fifth tables are the combining-ability side, mean and standard deviation, and the sixth is the contrast between the two extreme split times, unpaired because the draws are independent. GCA is the sum of its additive and dominance parts (asserted in the chunk), so its variance is \(\mathrm{var}(\mathrm{add}) + \mathrm{var}(\mathrm{dom}) + 2\,\mathrm{cov}(\mathrm{add}, \mathrm{dom})\), three terms. What was measured, from split = 0 to split = 1000: GCA_var goes from 0.56 to 0.293, GCA_var_add from 0.499 to 0.233, GCA_var_dom from 0.051 to 0.057, and GCA_cov2 (\(2\,\mathrm{cov}\)) from 0.01 to 0.003, the last with between-draw standard deviations of 0.078 and 0.043, so at every split it is within 1.4 standard errors of zero. The ratio GCA_dominance_share \(= \mathrm{var}(\mathrm{dom}) / \mathrm{var}(\mathrm{GCA})\) rises from 0.095 to 0.204 (t = 8.3). Which part drives it: holding the dominance part at its split = 0 value and moving only the additive part to its split = 1000 value gives a ratio of 0.174; holding the additive part and moving only the dominance part gives 0.1. And the per se-versus-GCA correlation falls from 0.955 to 0.896 (t = -8.3). Both correlations are below the 0.997 of Step 6’s undifferentiated halves, but that comparison crosses a change of founders, trait draw and line number, so the within-sweep contrast is the one that isolates split.

What is inferred: in this render the fall of the additive part of the GCA variance accounts for more of the rise in the ratio than the growth of the dominance part; the additive part falls with divergence because the sites that fix within a pool stop contributing to it, so the ratio can rise without the dominance part growing at all. The ratio is a partition of \(\mathrm{var}(\mathrm{GCA})\) only insofar as the covariance term is negligible; here that term is within 1.4 standard errors of zero at every split, which is the only sense in which “share” is used. As the pools separate, the dominance weights \((1 - 2 p_{Bk})\) of Step 6 move away from zero and a growing part of a line’s combining ability is invisible to its own genetic value; the evidence for that at this scale is the t of the contrast, and nothing here separates how much of the between-draw spread is the trait drawn with each founder set and how much is the DH sampling. The limit of the inference is the scale: 12 individuals per pool, 750 sites, 20 draws per split, and a split in coalescent generations that is not calibrated to any named heterotic-pool pair. The direction transfers; the numbers do not.

alphasimr_rrs_cycle() runs the same six steps for each pool, symmetrically (pool A tested on B, pool B tested on A), and every one is a primitive from above:

  1. Double haploids (Step 5). makeDH() draws 3 fully homozygous lines from each individual in the current pool – at book scale, 12 parents x 3 DH each = 36 DH lines per pool, per cycle. Because the pool is outbred, the DH lines from one parent differ from each other: this is the lottery of Step 4, drawn one generation earlier than in a line programme.
  2. Pick reciprocal testers. The first 2 individuals of the current opposite pool are used as testers – an arbitrary slice by index, not a random or merit-based draw, outbred individuals rather than inbred lines, and a different set each cycle because step 6 recreates the pool. This is the RRS-defining move: a line’s value is judged by its cross onto the other heterotic pool, not by itself.
  3. Testcross and phenotype (Step 6). hybridCross() forms the full factorial between every DH line and every tester – 36 DH x 2 testers = 72 testcross hybrids per pool – and setPheno() adds phenotyping error sized to the heritability set by SP$setVarE(h2 = ), at one location.
  4. GCA estimate (Step 6). Testcross phenotypes are grouped by DH-line parent (tapply(pheno[, 1], mother, mean)) and averaged across the 2 testers each line was crossed to. Only trait 1 is used for this ranking even though this chapter’s config simulates 2 traits per line; the second trait rides along in X/hybrids but plays no role in selection here. Step 7 is what it would take to use both.
  5. Keep the elites (Step 6). The 6 DH lines per pool with the highest mean testcross phenotype are kept as elite_A/elite_B.
  6. Recombine (Step 5). randCross() random-mates the elites back up to 12 individuals, founding the next cycle’s pool A (symmetrically for B).

elite_A/elite_B are what step 6 mates – and, in the last cycle, what the final commercial cross uses instead of being recombined again. The cycle’s summary mixes two different populations on purpose: mean_tc_A/mean_tc_B average over all DH lines tested that cycle (the population’s testcross performance before selection), while gd_A/gd_B are gene diversity of only the 6 surviving elites (the population after selection). That asymmetry is exactly what the next section plots – response measured pre-selection, diversity measured post-selection.

7.8.3 The trajectory across cycles, replicated, against a paired control

alphasimr_pipeline() runs alphasimr_rrs_cycle() in a loop for 4 cycles, carrying pop_A/pop_B forward from one cycle’s output to the next’s input. Only the last cycle’s elite_A/elite_B go on to found the commercial hybrid pool; every earlier cycle’s elites are spent entirely on randCross()ing the next cycle’s pool.

One run of that loop is an anecdote, and it would be an anecdote about one founder draw. The loop is written out here instead, so that three things can be added to it: a paired no-selection control (random_elite = TRUE, which draws the elites at random and leaves every other operation – the DH lines, the testcrosses, the recombination – identical, so the difference between the two arms is selection and nothing else), the between-pool partition of the recombined pools each cycle, and the genetic value of the actual product, the elite factorial.

run_rrs <- function(fp, cfg, random_elite = FALSE, seed = 1) {
  set.seed(seed)
  SP_r  <- fp[["SP"]]
  pop_A <- fp[["pop_A"]]; pop_B <- fp[["pop_B"]]
  pool  <- rep(c("A", "B"), c(cfg[["n_pool_A"]], cfg[["n_pool_B"]]))
  pools_row <- function(pop_A, pop_B) {
    ct <- ct_partition(pullSegSiteGeno(c(pop_A, pop_B), simParam = SP_r), pool)
    data.frame(gd_pool_A = alphasimr_gd(pop_A, SP_r),
               gd_pool_B = alphasimr_gd(pop_B, SP_r),
               GD_BS = ct[["diversity"]][["GD_BS"]],
               F_ST  = ct[["global"]][["F_ST"]])
  }
  rows <- list(cbind(cycle = 0, mean_tc_A = NA, mean_tc_B = NA, gd_A = NA, gd_B = NA,
                     hybrid_meanG = NA, pools_row(pop_A, pop_B)))
  for (i in seq_len(cfg[["n_cycles"]])) {
    cy <- alphasimr_rrs_cycle(pop_A, pop_B, SP_r, cfg, random_elite = random_elite)
    pop_A <- cy[["pop_A"]]; pop_B <- cy[["pop_B"]]
    prod  <- hybridCross(cy[["elite_A"]], cy[["elite_B"]], crossPlan = "testcross",
                         simParam = SP_r)
    rows[[i + 1]] <- cbind(cycle = i, cy[["summary"]],
                           hybrid_meanG = as.numeric(meanG(prod))[1],   # trait 1
                           pools_row(pop_A, pop_B))
  }
  do.call(rbind, rows)
}

Pooling the two pools’ differences is not free noise reduction, and pool_tab measures what it buys. Averaging halves the variance only if the two pools’ paired differences are independent across founder draws. For the elite-line gene diversity they are correlated at 0.2, and the pooled standard deviation, 0.019, is close to the 0.018 it would be if they were independent: pooling does what it should there. For the testcross mean they are correlated at 0.9, and the pooled standard deviation is 1.14 against 0.82 if independent: pooling does not reduce that noise, because the founder draw moves both pools’ response together. The per-pool rows are there to show the asymmetry between two exchangeable pools that any one set of 48 draws produces. The loss table says how much of the pools’ loss is selection: the control loses gene diversity too – 6 elites recombined into 12 individuals is a bottleneck whether or not the elites were chosen – and the selected arm’s mean loss exceeds the control’s, with the pooled t just stated as the evidence.

What is inferred: with real meiosis and a dominance trait behind it, reciprocal recurrent selection responds in both pools, and at this replication the response is beyond t_crit. Whether it spends within-pool diversity faster than the same bottleneck under drift alone is what the pooled gene-diversity t decides, and in this render it does for the elite lines and does for the recombined pools: the selection component of the loss is of the same order as its paired standard deviation at this scale, so the conclusion, not only the number, is what the t decides. Where the sign holds, the control isolates that component from the drift part, which is the distinction Chapter 19 draws with the package’s own faster simulator over a longer trajectory and a grid of selection intensities, and Chapter 8 draws a third time in an inbred-line programme. The between-pool rows: the GD_BS difference is beyond t_crit; where it is, and positive, RRS is moving the pools apart faster than drift does, which is what a scheme selecting on the cross is expected to do. F_ST rises partly because GD_T falls, so GD_BS is the row to read. What limits the inference is the scale – 12 founders per pool, 4 cycles, 2 testers at one location, 48 draws – and the fact that the founders come from MaCS, so both the numbers in this paragraph and any verdict that rests on a t near t_crit may differ between renders.

7.9 Assumptions, and what this adds over Chapter 4

What is more realistic here. Actual recombination and linkage (a genetic map per chromosome, not independent markers), a trait with directional dominance so that testcross selection has something real to select on and heterosis and inbreeding depression have a value, outbred founder pools with a coalescent split behind them, and multi-generation recurrent selection with a paired control rather than one static pool.

What is declared, and where. Steps 2–6 use inbred founders from quickHaplo() with every site a QTL and dominance degrees drawn at meanDD = 0.4, varDD = 0.1; the “pools” of Steps 4 and 6 are two undifferentiated halves of one founder set. Step 7 uses a separate additive founder draw. Step 8 uses outbred founders from runMacs2(split = ), again with every site a QTL; its DH lines are fully inbred, its testers are outbred individuals, and every loss of diversity is measured against the same run’s founders (cycle 0) and against the paired no-selection control – not against a random-subset null of the same size, which is the reference Chapter 16’s alpha uses.

What is still simplified. Testers are the first 2 individuals of the current opposite pool, an arbitrary slice by index – not chosen at random or by merit, and not the elite inbred tester a real programme would use; because randCross() recreates each pool every cycle, the testers change from cycle to cycle, so successive cycles’ testcross means are not on a common tester and are comparable only through the pools’ genetic level; there is one phenotyping environment (setPheno()’s error variance, no genotype-by-environment term – Chapter 8 adds one); the population-split coalescent history is a stylised divergence process, not calibrated to a named heterotic-pool pair; doubled-haploid production is instantaneous, skipping the field seasons Step 5 counted; and selection acts on trait 1 only – the 2 traits this chapter’s config simulates are all carried through X/hybrids, but only the first ever enters the GCA ranking. The accuracy prediction of Step 6 is first order in the sample correlation – its finite-tester-pool and sample-variance corrections are applied, its second-order term is not – and the selfing trajectory of Step 5 follows 400 parallel lineages from one cross, checked at seven generations.

Scale. This chapter runs live at a book scale (12 + 12 founders, 750 markers, 4 cycles, 48 paired founder draws) – AlphaSimR is fast enough at this size that no caching is needed, unlike the MABC scans in Chapter 6. Step 8’s numbers may change on re-execution and do change between sessions, because its founders come from MaCS (Step 1); when they move, they move by about the amounts the standard deviations above say, and so may a conclusion that rests on a t near t_crit: between sessions, the paired t for the selection component of gene-diversity erosion has been seen both above and below t_crit at 24 pairs, which is why the chapter runs 48 pairs and writes each such inference conditionally on the render’s own t. A heavier, production-scale run is inst/scripts/run_alphasimr.R:

Rscript inst/scripts/run_alphasimr.R --test          # smoke run, ~10 s
Rscript inst/scripts/run_alphasimr.R --out=DIR        # full run, ~2 min

It runs alphasimr_pipeline() at a larger configuration, feeds the resulting hybrid pool through stage2_select(), and writes the cycle trajectory and selection metrics as CSVs.

7.10 Where this connects

Chapter 4 and Step 8 build the same three-object contract from two different generative models – the fast one and the realistic one – which is the point of drawing that seam where the book does: everything past it, build_ctx() through stage2_select(), cannot tell which one it is looking at. Chapter 10 is the algebra Steps 3, 4 and 6 verify against AlphaSimR: inbreeding depression against genParam(), expected heterosis against a full factorial, and the GCA/SCA decomposition against the exact combining abilities. Chapter 3 supplies the breeder’s equation Step 6 checks and the index theory Step 7 uses; Chapter 20 is where Step 3’s varA-against-genicVarA gap becomes the subject rather than a diagnostic. Chapter 2 supplies the partition Step 8 measures across split and across cycles. Chapter 19 runs the same diversity-erosion argument at a longer horizon with the package’s own faster simulator; this chapter is its cross-check against an engine with real meiosis behind it, and Chapter 20 uses this chapter’s linked arm for the same reason.
