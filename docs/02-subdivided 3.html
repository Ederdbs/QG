2  Diversity in a subdivided population

A breeding programme is never one population. It is heterotic groups, or accessions, or landraces, or cycles – and the diversity of the whole is not the average of the diversity of the parts. Caballero and Toro (2002) give the partition that makes this precise, and it is the analytical backbone of everything that follows: the constraint in Chapter 18 is a special case of it, and the within/between asymmetry that makes plant hybrid breeding different from animal breeding is a statement about which term of it you are allowed to minimise.

The paper’s central claim is uncomfortable and worth stating first: a method that ranks accessions by distance alone can invert the correct conclusion. An accession can be the most distant because it is internally fixed, and removing it can raise the diversity of the pool. Section 2.7.1 reproduces that result numerically.

2.1 Notation

  • A metapopulation of \(n\) subpopulations; subpopulation \(i\) has \(N_i\) individuals, \(N_T = \sum_i N_i\). In this chapter \(n\) and \(N\) count subpopulations and individuals, not the selected and candidate hybrids of
  • A tilde, \(\tilde{x}\), is a weighted mean within subpopulations: \(\tilde{f} = \sum_i f_{ii} N_i / N_T\).
  • A bar, \(\bar{x}\), is a mean over all pairs in the metapopulation, between-subpopulation pairs included: \(\bar{f} = \sum_{i,j} f_{ij} N_i N_j / N_T^2\).
  • Always \(\bar{f} \le \tilde{f} \le \tilde{s}\). The differences between those three numbers are the partition of diversity.
  • Everything is computed per locus and then averaged over loci. Never average frequencies first.
WarningThe subtle point, and the one most often got wrong

Every coancestry here includes the pairs of an individual with itself – group coancestry in the sense of Cockerham (1967). That is what makes \(1 - f_{ii}\) exactly Nei’s gene diversity \(1 - \sum_k \tilde p_{k,i}^2\) (Nei 1973) – the plug-in form, without the small-sample correction \(2N_i/(2N_i - 1)\) – and it is what makes the algebra close. Drop the diagonal and none of the identities of this chapter hold.

2.2 The three coancestry blocks

2.2.1 \(f_{ij}\), between subpopulations

The probability that two alleles, one sampled at random in subpopulation \(i\) and one in \(j\), are identical. From markers, \(f_{ij} = \sum_k \tilde p_{k,i} \tilde p_{k,j}\).

Read it as redundancy between two materials. If you cross plants of \(i\) with plants of \(j\), the progeny has expected inbreeding \(F = f_{ij}\). It answers: what do I gain by recombining these two accessions?

On this molecular scale no fixed level of \(f_{ij}\) marks two materials as redundant: the reference is a base in which all alleles are distinct, so \(f_{ij}\) between unrelated lines is already high on a biallelic panel. The book’s simulated pools (book_cfg, Chapter 1) show it:

Show code 
ctx <- simulate_data(book_cfg)
fL  <- ctx[["fL"]]
inA <- ctx[["pool"]] == "A"
fAB <- fL[inA, !inA]
fWI <- c(fL[inA, inA][upper.tri(fL[inA, inA])],
         fL[!inA, !inA][upper.tri(fL[!inA, !inA])])
fl_rng <- c(between_min = min(fAB), between_max = max(fAB),
            between_mean = mean(fAB), within_min = min(fWI),
            within_max = max(fWI), within_mean = mean(fWI))

The coancestry between two inbred lines from opposite heterotic pools ranges from 0.608 to 0.663 (mean 0.638), and between two lines of the same pool from 0.668 to 0.719 (mean 0.693). What separates complementary from redundant material is therefore the contrast between the two distributions, measured on one panel, and not a threshold carried over from another panel. The ranges above come from one simulated draw at the teaching scale and are not a property of maize pools.

2.2.2 \(f_{ii}\), within a subpopulation

The case \(i = j\). From markers \(f_{ii} = \sum_k \tilde p_{k,i}^2\), the expected homozygosity, so

\[1 - f_{ii} \;=\; H_{e,i} \;=\; \text{Nei's gene diversity of accession } i. \tag{2.1}\]

A landrace accession regenerated twenty times with forty ears per cycle accumulates coancestry and watches \(f_{ii}\) rise silently. It is the cheapest alarm for genetic erosion inside a genebank – and it is precisely what a distance tree cannot see.

2.2.3 \(s_i\) and \(F_i\), self-coancestry and molecular inbreeding

\[s_i = \sum_k \sum_l \frac{p_{k,l,i}^2}{N_i}, \qquad F_i = 2 s_i - 1, \qquad 2(1 - s_i) = 1 - F_i = H_{o,i} \tag{2.2}\]

with \(p_{k,l,i}\) the frequency of allele \(k\) within individual \(l\) of subpopulation \(i\): 0, 0.5 or 1 for a diploid, that is, the 0/1/2 dosage divided by 2 (ct_partition() does exactly this). For a biallelic marker and the reference allele, \(p_{k,l,i}\) is the within-individual frequency that 1 writes \(x\); there \(x_{ik}\) indexes an individual and a marker, here \(k\) indexes an allele. On the 0/1/2 scale itself \(s_i\) would leave \([0.5, 1]\) and \(F_i\) would leave \([0, 1]\). \(H_{o,i}\) is the observed heterozygosity, the mean over individuals of the fraction of loci at which the individual is heterozygous. A heterozygote has self-coancestry 0.5 at the locus, so \(1 - s_i\) is half the observed heterozygosity, not all of it.

Warning\(F_i\) here is molecular, and is not \(F_{IS}\)

It is measured against a hypothetical base in which all alleles are distinct. It is not comparable across studies using different marker panels. For the question “is this population mating at random?”, use \(\alpha_i\) of Equation 2.3.

In soybean, rice and wheat, pure lines have \(s_i \to 1\), so \(F_i \to 1\) and \(H_o \to 0\). That is expected, not a symptom. It changes only where the diversity resides.

2.3 Two per-subpopulation metrics

\[\alpha_i = \frac{F_i - f_{ii}}{1 - f_{ii}}, \qquad G_i = \frac{s_i - f_{ii}}{1 - f_{ii}} = \frac{1 + \alpha_i}{2} \tag{2.3}\]

\(\alpha_i\) is the subpopulation’s analogue of \(F_{IS}\), not the diversity-loss constraint \(\alpha\) of Chapter 18, and \(G_i\) is not a genomic relationship matrix \(\mathbf{G}\). A positive \(\alpha_i\) means an excess of homozygotes (selfing, mating among relatives, a Wahlund effect from internal substructure, null alleles); negative means an excess of heterozygotes (dioecy, self-incompatibility, selection, or a freshly formed F1 or synthetic).

\(G_i\) is the proportion of the accession’s diversity that sits between individuals rather than inside them, and it is probably the most underused quantity in the paper. It is a mating-system meter (Table 2.1):

Operationally, \(1 - G_i\) is the fraction of an accession’s diversity stored inside each plant, and it decides the sampling design. With \(G_i \to 1\) (selfers), one individual captures almost nothing and you need many plants per accession. With \(G_i \approx 0.5\) (maize), a few plants already capture half.

2.4 Distances, and what they actually are

Nei’s minimum distance between subpopulations (Toro and Caballero 2005, sec. 3a) is

\[D_{ij} = \frac{f_{ii} + f_{jj}}{2} - f_{ij} = \sum_k \tfrac{1}{2}(\tilde p_{k,i} - \tilde p_{k,j})^2 . \tag{2.4}\]

Note what that is: half the sum of squared allele-frequency differences – a variance component, not an evolutionary distance calibrated in time. It is the piece that enters \(F_{ST}\), and it is legitimate. The error in a distance-only method is not using \(D_{ij}\); it is using only \(D_{ij}\).

TipNo double sum over individuals is needed

Between individuals \(l\) and \(l'\) the distance is \(d_{ll'} = (s_l + s_{l'})/2 - f_{ll'}\), with \(s_l\) and \(f_{ll'}\) the self-coancestry and coancestry of single individuals (Equation 2.2 with \(N_i = 1\)); at a biallelic locus it equals \((p_l - p_{l'})^2\), \(p_l\) the within-individual frequency, and over loci it is averaged. The mean of \(d_{ll'}\) over all pairs of an individual of \(i\) and an individual of \(j\), \(\bar d_{ij}\), computed literally as a double sum over all pairs of individuals, costs \(O(N_i N_j m)\). The identity \(\bar d_{ij} = (s_i + s_j)/2 - f_{ij}\) is exact and needs only the subpopulation frequencies and self-coancestries, at cost \(O(N_T m + n^2 m)\). \(\bar d_{ij}\) is not the \(D_{ij}\) of Equation 2.4: its diagonal \(\bar d_{ii} = s_i - f_{ii}\) is not zero, and Nei’s distance is recovered as \(D_{ij} = \bar d_{ij} - (\bar d_{ii} + \bar d_{jj})/2\). Both identities follow by substituting the definitions of \(d_{ll'}\), \(s_i\) (Equation 2.2) and \(f_{ij}\). No timing at genebank scale was run for this chapter.

2.5 The equation that matters most in practice

\[\bar{f} = \sum_{i} \frac{N_i}{N_T} \Big[\, \underbrace{f_{ii}}_{\text{internal coancestry}} - \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_j D_{ij} N_j / N_T}_{\text{mean distance to the others}} \Big] \tag{2.5}\]

In Equation 2.5, each accession’s contribution to global coancestry – that is, to the loss of diversity in the pool – has two terms of opposite sign:

  • Term 1 (+). The more internally fixed an accession is, the more it raises global coancestry: it dilutes the pool with copies of the same allele.
  • Term 2 (−). The more distant it is from the others, the more it reduces global coancestry.

A distance-only method sees term 2 alone. That is the whole mechanism of the inverted conclusions.

2.6 F statistics and the partition

\[F_{IS} = \frac{\tilde F - \tilde f}{1 - \tilde f}, \qquad F_{ST} = \frac{\tilde f - \bar f}{1 - \bar f} = \frac{\bar D}{1 - \bar f}, \qquad F_{IT} = \frac{\tilde F - \bar f}{1 - \bar f} \tag{2.6}\]

with \((1 - F_{IT}) = (1 - F_{IS})(1 - F_{ST})\). These are Wright’s fixation indices (Wright 1951) written in coancestries; the form \(F_{ST} = \bar D/(1 - \bar f)\) is due to Cockerham (1969), as restated by Toro and Caballero (2005, sec. 3a).

The partition itself, of gene diversity (\(GD\)) into the terms named in Table 2.2, is the heart of Caballero and Toro (2002):

\[(1 - \bar f) = \underbrace{(1 - \tilde s)}_{GD_{WI}} + \underbrace{(\tilde s - \tilde f)}_{GD_{BI}} + \underbrace{(\tilde f - \bar f)}_{GD_{BS}} \tag{2.7}\]

The four components satisfy the identities

\[\frac{GD_{WI}}{GD_T} = (1-G)(1-F_{ST}), \quad \frac{GD_{BI}}{GD_T} = G(1-F_{ST}), \quad \frac{GD_{WS}}{GD_T} = 1-F_{ST}, \quad \frac{GD_{BS}}{GD_T} = F_{ST}, \tag{2.8}\]

where \(G = (\tilde s - \tilde f)/(1 - \tilde f)\) is the metapopulation analogue of \(G_i\) (Equation 2.3). Since \(s_i - f_{ii} = G_i (1 - f_{ii})\), \(G = \sum_i N_i (1 - f_{ii}) G_i / \sum_i N_i (1 - f_{ii})\): a mean of the \(G_i\) weighted by \(N_i (1 - f_{ii})\), not the census mean \(\sum_i (N_i/N_T)\, G_i\). The two coincide when all \(G_i\) are equal or all \(f_{ii}\) are equal, and the first two identities then hold with either; when both the \(G_i\) and the \(f_{ii}\) differ the two means generally differ, and the first two identities fail with the census mean (the chunk below exhibits one such case). They follow from \(1 - F_{ST} = (1 - \tilde f)/(1 - \bar f)\).

Every identity of this chapter so far needs individual genotypes, so the chunk below draws a toy metapopulation – two random-mating subpopulations and one of inbred lines, 400 biallelic loci – runs ct_partition() on it, and asserts each identity, the distance identities against a literal double sum over pairs of individuals:

Show code

  matrix(stats::rbinom(N_toy[1] * m_toy, 2, rep(p_toy[1, ], each = N_toy[1])), N_toy[1]),

  matrix(2 * stats::rbinom(N_toy[3] * m_toy, 1, rep(p_toy[3, ], each = N_toy[3])), N_toy[3]))

2.6.1 Why the partition changes the strategy by species

  • Outcrossers (maize, sunflower, forages, forest trees): \(GD_{WI}\) is large. The diversity is literally inside the plants. Conserving means maintaining heterozygosity – regenerate with many individuals and balanced contributions.
  • Selfers (soybean, wheat, rice, beans) and doubled-haploid or line collections: \(GD_{WI} \approx 0\). All diversity is \(GD_{BI} + GD_{BS}\). Conserving means maintaining the number of distinct lines; heterozygosity is not a target. Regenerating with few plants per line is acceptable; losing lines is not.
  • Clonal crops (potato, sugarcane, fruit trees): \(GD_{WI}\) is high and frozen – it does not decay by drift while the clone exists. The \(N_e\) algebra does not apply without adaptation.

This is the reason a hybrid maize programme cannot manage diversity with a single pool-wide number: it needs \(GD_{WS}\) and \(GD_{BS}\) reported separately. Under drift alone in two closed pools of equal weight, \(f_{AB}\) is unchanged in expectation while \(f_{AA}\) and \(f_{BB}\) rise, so an expected rise \(\Delta\) in \(\tilde f\) raises \(\bar f\) by \(\Delta/2\): \(GD_{WS}\) falls by \(\Delta\), \(GD_{BS}\) rises by \(\Delta/2\), and \(GD_T\) falls by only \(\Delta/2\). A single total understates the within-pool loss; Section 13.7 shows what the between-pool gain is worth. Selection is not covered by this argument.

2.7 The worked example, reproduced

The metapopulation of Caballero and Toro (2002): four subpopulations of \(N\) = 12, 4, 8, 6, three loci with 5, 3 and 3 alleles. Its published coancestry matrix, transcribed from the paper:

Show code 
f <- matrix(c(0.3391, 0.3021, 0.2535, 0.3229,
              0.3021, 0.8750, 0.3073, 0.3889,
              0.2535, 0.3073, 0.4766, 0.2483,
              0.3229, 0.3889, 0.2483, 0.3704), 4, 4, byrow = TRUE,
            dimnames = list(paste0("Sub", 1:4), paste0("Sub", 1:4)))
N <- c(12, 4, 8, 6)
w <- N / sum(N)

Note subpopulation 2: \(f_{22} = 0.875\), so \(H_e = 0.125\). It is nearly fixed internally. Now the global quantities, with the paper’s published values beside them (Table 2.3):

Show code 
f_bar <- drop(w %*% f %*% w)
f_til <- sum(w * diag(f))
s_til <- 0.7056                       # published; needs individual genotypes

computed  <- c(f_bar = f_bar, f_til = f_til, GD_T = 1 - f_bar,
               GD_WI = 1 - s_til, GD_BI = s_til - f_til, GD_BS = f_til - f_bar,
               F_ST = (f_til - f_bar) / (1 - f_bar))
published <- c(f_bar = 0.3256, f_til = 0.4535, GD_T = 0.6744, GD_WI = 0.2944,
               GD_BI = 0.2521, GD_BS = 0.1279, F_ST = 0.1897)
# rows computed from f alone must match to half a unit of the 4th decimal
from_f <- c("f_bar", "f_til", "GD_T", "GD_BS", "F_ST")
stopifnot(max(abs(computed[from_f] - published[from_f])) < 5e-5)
# the published partition must close: three 4-dp roundings, at most 1.5e-4
stopifnot(abs(sum(published[c("GD_WI", "GD_BI", "GD_BS")]) -
              published[["GD_T"]]) < 1.5e-4)
ct_glob <- data.frame(quantity = names(computed),
                      computed = round(unname(computed), 4),
                      published = unname(published))

The five values computed from \(\mathbf{f}\) alone (\(\bar f\), \(\tilde f\), \(GD_T\), \(GD_{BS}\), \(F_{ST}\)) reproduce the published ones within half a unit of the fourth decimal, and the chunk asserts it. The chunk also asserts that the published \(GD_{WI}\), \(GD_{BI}\) and \(GD_{BS}\) sum to the published \(GD_T\), a check of the paper’s internal consistency. The computed \(GD_{WI}\) and \(GD_{BI}\) are not a check: they are derived from the published \(\tilde s\), which needs individual genotypes this chapter does not have, so they agree by construction. Note that \(GD_{BS}\) is only 19 % of the total: the between-subpopulation divergence – the only thing a distance method measures – accounts for less than a fifth of the diversity in this metapopulation.

2.7.1 Removing an accession can increase diversity

Show code 
res <- list(f = f, by_subpop = data.frame(N = N))
lg  <- cbind(ct_loss_gain(res),
             delta_pct_equal = ct_loss_gain(res, "equal")$delta_pct)
D_nei   <- outer(diag(f), diag(f), "+") / 2 - f
lg$dist <- drop(D_nei %*% w)               # mean distance to the pool
stopifnot(which.max(lg$dist) == 2)

The number to look at is subpopulation 2. Its mean distance to the pool, \(\sum_j D_{2j} N_j/N_T\) (last column of Table 2.4), is the largest of the four, so a method that ranks accessions by distance alone – the Weitzman approach discussed by Toro and Caballero (2005, sec. 3d) is one – ranks it first. The coancestry analysis says removing it raises pool diversity by 1.8 % (census weights) or 6.5 % (equal weights).

It is distant because it is fixed. Alleles fixed in a sample of four individuals are not a genetic resource; they are the result of drift. The same pattern appears in the reanalysis of eleven European pig breeds in Caballero and Toro (2002), as published in corrected form by Toro and Caballero (2005, Table 4): the breed with the largest Weitzman loss (FRBA) raises gene diversity by 0.67 % when removed. That value is quoted from the table, not recomputed here.

NoteWhat this does and does not license

For a genebank: a rare accession with few plants and high homozygosity – the archetypal “unique” accession on a distance tree – may be contributing negatively to the pool. That does not mean discard it. It means the correct priority may be to regenerate it at larger \(N\), not to replicate it into a core collection. Storing seed is cheap; extinction is irreversible.

2.7.2 Optimal contributions

Maximising \(GD_T = 1 - \sum_{i,j} f_{ij} c_i c_j\) subject to \(c_i \ge 0\) and \(\sum_i c_i = 1\) is a quadratic programme:

Show code 
oc <- optimal_contributions(f)
stopifnot(max(abs(oc[["c_i"]] - c(0.522, 0, 0.270, 0.208))) < 5e-4)

Subpopulation 2 receives weight zero (computed \(\mathbf{c} = (0.522,\, 0.000,\, 0.270,\, 0.208)\)), and total diversity rises from 0.6744 to 0.6874, a gain of 1.9 %. Rounded to three decimals the solution equals the published \(\mathbf{c} = (0.522,\, 0,\, 0.270,\, 0.208)\) of Caballero and Toro (2002); the chunk asserts it.

Three direct uses in plant breeding:

  • Base population for recurrent selection. \(c_i\) gives the seed proportion from each source that maximises the neutral-marker \(GD_T\) of the base. That equals maximising its additive (genic) variance only under additive gene action, effects of equal size at every locus, and markers whose frequencies represent those of the quantitative trait loci; Chapter 3 shows the two differ by the squared-effect weighting.
  • Core collection. Instead of clustering heuristics plus proportional sampling, solve the quadratic programme directly with operational constraints (\(c_i \le\) multiplication capacity, \(c_i \ge\) a policy minimum for accessions of cultural importance).
  • Optimal contribution selection. Meuwissen (1997) maximises \(\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{u}\) subject to a bound on the group coancestry \(\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{c}\), with \(\mathbf{u}\) the vector of breeding values; its Lagrangian form is \(\max \mathbf{c}'\mathbf{u} - \lambda\,\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{c}\), \(\lambda \ge 0\). This chapter is the case \(\lambda \to \infty\), pure diversity. A breeder operates in the middle of the curve – which is Chapter 18.

2.8 Equal contributions and effective size

Minimising \(\bar f\) maximises the status number \(N_s = 1/(2\bar f)\) (Lindgren et al. 1997) by definition. \(N_s\) is a static function of the current coancestry; the effective size \(N_e\) is defined from the rate at which inbreeding or coancestry rises across generations, so the two are not the same quantity, and lowering \(\bar f\) today does not by itself fix \(N_e\).

What links contributions to \(N_e\) is the asymptotic metapopulation effective size of Caballero and Toro (2002), in the form restated by Toro and Caballero (2005, sec. 4a):

\[N_e \approx \frac{2Nn}{(V_W + V_B + 1)(1 - F_{ST})}, \tag{2.9}\]

with \(n\) subpopulations of equal size \(N\), and \(V_W\) and \(V_B\) the variances of the long-term contributions of individuals within and between subpopulations. It assumes random mating within subpopulations and a size and structure constant over generations, and it holds only after enough generations for \(N_e\) to reach its asymptote. With random (Poisson) contributions within subpopulations and equal contributions of the subpopulations (\(V_W = 1\), \(V_B = 0\)) Equation 2.9 gives \(Nn/(1 - F_{ST})\); equalising contributions within subpopulations (\(V_W = 0\), \(V_B = 0\)) gives \(2Nn/(1 - F_{ST})\), double.

Show code 
ne_fm  <- function(N, Vk) 4 * N / (2 + Vk)           # Falconer & Mackay, ch. 4
ne_ct  <- function(N, VW) 2 * N / (VW + 1)            # @eq-ne-meta, n = 1, F_ST = 0
N0     <- 100
stopifnot(all.equal(ne_fm(N0, c(2, 1, 0)), ne_ct(N0, c(2, 1, 0) / 2)))
ne_gain <- c(seed_only = ne_fm(N0, 1) / ne_fm(N0, 2),
             both      = ne_fm(N0, 0) / ne_fm(N0, 2))
stopifnot(all.equal(ne_gain[["seed_only"]], 4 / 3), all.equal(ne_gain[["both"]], 2))

The doubling assumes that each individual’s contribution is equalised as both female and male parent, which needs controlled pollination. In the single-population case Equation 2.9 reduces to \(2N/(V_W + 1)\), which is \(N_e = 4N/(2 + V_k)\) (Falconer and Mackay 1996, ch. 4) with \(V_W = V_k/2\), \(V_k\) the variance of the number of gametes an individual contributes. Harvesting the same number of seeds from every plant of an open-pollinated population equalises only the maternal contribution; with a maternal variance of 0 and a Poisson paternal variance of 1, \(V_k = 1\) and \(N_e = 4N/3\), a gain of 1.333 over Poisson contributions, not 2.

TipEqual contributions of both parents double \(N_e\)

Under controlled pollination, equal numbers of seeds from every plant as female and as male parent, and the same number of plants from every family, double the effective size (\(V_W = 0\) against \(V_W = 1\)). Equal seed numbers from open-pollinated plants alone give a factor of 4/3. Neither needs genotyping.

It is an intervention in germplasm management. The opposite – bulk harvest from a plot, which in practice samples the most productive plants disproportionately – inflates \(V_W\) and collapses \(N_e\).

2.9 The honest caveats

A breeder should take these seriously.

Criteria based on within-population variation favour the largest populations, which are often the commercial and least endangered ones (Toro and Caballero 2005, sec. 3e). A \(\bar f\) criterion can push a genebank toward abundant elite material – the opposite of the conservation objective. Beyond that:

  • Neutral markers do not measure adaptive value, disease resistance, stress adaptation or cultural value. A fixed accession may carry the only resistance allele to an emerging rust.
  • Allelic richness is more sensitive to bottlenecks than heterozygosity and is often the more relevant criterion for pre-breeding. The argument that minimising \(\bar f\) maximises allelic richness in the long run (via the effective number of alleles being, by definition, the inverse of mean coancestry; Toro and Caballero (2005, sec. 2)) is valid asymptotically but does not protect rare alleles in the short term. This is why allelic richness survives the redundancy analysis in Chapter 15 as the one genuinely independent axis.
  • Weighting between-group variation more heavily is arbitrary. A more defensible fix is to optimise \(\mathbf{c}\) with per-accession minimum constraints, as Toro and Caballero (2005, sec. 3e, Table 5) do for five Iberian pig strains.

Rule of thumb: use \(\bar f\) to decide proportions and regeneration effort; never use \(\bar f\) alone to decide discard.

2.10 A correction published by the authors

Toro and Caballero (2005, Table 4, footnote a) republish the pig-breed reanalysis of Caballero and Toro (2002) and mark one entry (breed DESH) as correcting a mistake in the 2002 paper. Any value from that reanalysis, including the 0.67 % quoted in Section 2.7.1, should be read from the 2005 table. The same review restates the effective-size result of Equation 2.9: equalising contributions within subpopulations doubles \(N_e\) (Toro and Caballero 2005, sec. 4a). Only the 2005 review was accessible when this chapter was checked; values attributed to the 2002 paper without a 2005 counterpart (the worked example’s matrix, \(\tilde s\) and contributions) are transcribed from it and were not re-checked against its full text.

2.11 What manages this quantity, and where

This chapter is the book’s keystone, and it is easy to read it as a self-contained treatment of Caballero and Toro (2002). The partition it derives (Equation 2.7) is not background: it is the object every later chapter operates on. Three consequences follow, and each is a chapter.

A single pool-wide statistic cannot express “conserve within, hold between”. That is why Chapter 14 reports the partition rather than only \(GD_T\), and why Chapter 16 makes stage S1 a separate monitoring stage instead of folding pool conservation into the hybrid-selection constraint.

The between term is not a bonus to be maximised. Section 13.7 shows that under neutral drift every unit of \(GD_{BS}\) gained is paid for out of the shared term, so pushing the pools apart buys nothing. The partition tells you where diversity is, not which direction to move it.

The within term is the one with a future. \(GD_{BI}\) is the raw material of next cycle’s response, which Chapter 3 makes precise: it is the same sum over the same loci as genic variance, weighted equally instead of by squared effect. That is the entire reason a diversity constraint is worth writing down, and Chapter 23 is where the equivalence is pushed until it breaks.
