22  A complete plant breeding programme, and what it costs in diversity

The seventh consequence for the pipeline drawn in Chapter 21 is to report the diversity cost per cycle explicitly whenever cycles are shortened. This chapter measures that cost in a whole programme, but as a stock: the change in gene diversity after 15 years, for arms whose cycle lengths in years it also states. It does not report a rate per cycle, because the cycles of the programme overlap (Section 22.7).

Chapter 9, built back in the Simulation part, assembled AlphaSimR’s primitives into a hybrid scheme: two heterotic pools, reciprocal testers, a commercial cross at the end. This chapter assembles the same primitives into the other kind of programme – an inbred-line pipeline, where the product is a variety rather than a hybrid, and where the organising problem is not which cross to sell but how many genotypes to carry through how many stages of field testing.

The scheme is the staged wheat programme of Gaynor et al. (2017), as reduced for the AlphaSimR course vignette (Gorjanc et al. (2022), week 5 C) and reduced further here (the configuration, and how it differs from both, is stated in Section 22.1). It is a stylised inbred-line programme, not a survey of programmes in use. It is worth building because it exposes something the hybrid chapter cannot: the trade between the number of genotypes tested and the precision with which each is measured. A breeder has a fixed number of plots. Spending them on more entries scored once or fewer entries scored with eight replicates (the course calls replicates “locations”; they are not locations here, see Section 22.1.1) is the same budget spent two ways, and the staged trial is the standard compromise. This chapter sets that trade up but does not measure it: it never varies the number of entries against the replication at a fixed number of plots. The Mating and testing design part measures allocation of plots between crosses and locations, for hybrids, in Chapter 18, Chapter 19 and Chapter 20; none of those chapters is a measurement on this inbred-line programme.

Then the chapter does what the course vignette does not (it reports the genetic mean and variance only): it puts the book’s diversity machinery over the result, and asks what the faster programme spends to be faster.

22.1 The scheme

The configuration. It is the author’s own reduction, not the course’s and not Gaynor et al.’s. Gaynor et al. (Table 1 and the Methods, pp. 2375-2377) start from 50 founders and run 100 crosses a year, 100 doubled-haploid (DH) lines per cross, 10,000 headrows, 500 lines in the preliminary trial, 50 in the advanced trial and 10 in the elite trial. The course vignette reduces that to 30 founders on 21 chromosomes, 30 crosses, 50 DH lines per cross (1500 headrows), at most 10 lines per cross into the preliminary trial and 300, 25 and 5 entries in the three trials, with 50 causal sites (quantitative trait loci, QTL) per chromosome. The values below (sch) reduce it again; Figure 22.1 draws the stages.

Show code 
sch <- list(
  n_founders = 40, n_chr = 10, n_qtl = 60,   # founder genomes
  n_neutral = 0,                             # neutral sites per chromosome (0 = every site is a QTL)
  n_crosses  = 20, n_dh = 20, fam_max = 5,   # crossing and DH production
  n_pyt = 60, n_ayt = 12, n_eyt = 4,         # entries per yield trial
  rep_hdrw = 1/4, rep_pyt = 1, rep_ayt = 4, rep_eyt = 8,   # replicates per stage
  var_e = 0.4, gs_boost = 3, n_burn = 7, n_years = 15,
  gs_from = 6                                # first year a genomic arm switches on
)
n_hdrw <- sch$n_crosses * sch$n_dh           # every DH line is scored in the headrows
fx <- function(x, d = 2) sprintf("%.*f", d, x)   # fixed decimals for inline numbers
n_probe <- 48                                # seeds of the neutral-locus probe

Seven stages (Figure 22.1), and the logic connecting them is a single idea: at every step, entries fall and replication rises. 400 doubled-haploid lines are scored once, cheaply, in unreplicated headrows; 60 survive into a preliminary yield trial (PYT) with 1 replicate; 12 into an advanced trial (AYT) with 4; 4 into an elite trial (EYT) with 8. The number of entries falls by factors of 6.7, 5.0 and 3.0 over the three steps, not by a constant factor.

rep_hdrw = 1/4 is the course’s device for a stage less precise than a single plot: headrows are scored visually on a short row, which carries less information than one replicated plot, so the plot-error variance is multiplied by four. fam_max caps how many DH lines any one cross may contribute to the preliminary trial – selectWithinFam() from Chapter 9’s Step 6, holding the genetic base open against a truncation that would otherwise take the whole trial from the two best crosses.

22.1.1 What reps does, and what it does not

reps in setPheno() is the argument Chapter 9’s Step 2 flagged as the plant idiom, and the course calls its values “locations”. It divides the plot-error variance varE by the number of replicates and nothing else. The genotype-by-environment (GxE) term enters a phenotype differently. In AlphaSimR 2.1.0 the trait of addTraitAG(varGxE = ) gives each entry a deviate \(g_i\) of variance varGxE, and a setPheno() call multiplies every \(g_i\) by one standard-normal draw \(z\) made for that call and shared by all entries of the call; reps does not average it. The chunk checks that structure by its consequence, in two parts. With varE = 0, the departures of the phenotypes from the genetic values over four calls (two with reps = 1, two with reps = 8) are one vector scaled four times, so the matrix has rank one: one shared deviate per call. That check is blind to a rescaling of each call, so it cannot say whether reps divides the GxE term; the second part measures it directly, as the mean squared departure over 2000 calls at reps = 1 and at reps = 8, which would fall eight-fold at reps = 8 if reps averaged GxE. The chunk then measures the accuracy of an entry mean (Table 22.1), \(r = \text{cor}(\text{genetic value}, \text{phenotype})\) over the 40 founders, as the number of replicates grows, with and without the GxE term.

Show code 
set.seed(7)
fg_acc <- quickHaplo(nInd = 40, nChr = 10, segSites = 60, inbred = TRUE)
mk <- function(vg) {
  SP <- SimParam$new(fg_acc)
  SP$addTraitAG(nQtlPerChr = 60, mean = 4, var = 0.1, varGxE = vg)
  list(SP = SP, pop = newPop(fg_acc, simParam = SP))
}
# GxE enters as g_i * z, one z per call: varE = 0 leaves a rank-one residual matrix
m <- mk(0.2)
res <- sapply(c(1, 1, 8, 8), function(r)
  setPheno(m$pop, varE = 0, reps = r, onlyPheno = TRUE, simParam = m$SP)[, 1] -
    gv(m$pop)[, 1])
sv <- svd(res)[["d"]]
stopifnot(sv[2] < 1e-8 * sv[1])
set.seed(5)
msq <- sapply(c(1, 8), function(r) mean(replicate(2000,
  (setPheno(m$pop, varE = 0, reps = r, onlyPheno = TRUE, simParam = m$SP)[, 1] -
     gv(m$pop)[, 1])^2)))
stopifnot(msq[2] / msq[1] > 0.5)          # eight-fold division would give 0.125
acc <- function(vg, r, seed = 11) {
  m <- mk(vg); set.seed(seed)
  a <- replicate(300, cor(gv(m$pop)[, 1],
                          setPheno(m$pop, varE = sch$var_e, reps = r,
                                   onlyPheno = TRUE, simParam = m$SP)[, 1]))
  c(mean(a), stats::sd(a))
}
reps_grid <- c(0.25, 1, 3, 4, 8)
tab_acc <- t(sapply(reps_grid, function(r) c(acc(0.2, r), acc(0, r))))
dimnames(tab_acc) <- list(reps = reps_grid,
  c("GxE mean", "GxE sd", "no GxE mean", "no GxE sd"))
varG_found <- stats::var(gv(m$pop)[, 1])
fmt(tab_acc, 3)

The mean squared departure of the phenotype from the genetic value, with varE = 0, is 0.202 at reps = 1 and 0.200 at reps = 8, against varGxE = 0.2: reps does not divide the GxE term (it would give 0.025 at 8 if it did). The founders’ additive variance is 0.103 (var = 0.1), so the GxE variance of 0.2 is about twice it. Eight replicates raise the accuracy of an entry mean from 0.40 to 0.63 with the GxE term, against 0.45 to 0.82 without it, and the standard deviation across calls stays at 0.18 where the GxE term is present against 0.04 where it is not. Selection accuracy at the last stage is higher because the plot-error variance has been divided by eight, not because the trait became more heritable; but it is bounded by a GxE term that replication does not reduce and that changes from call to call. What the chapter exposes is therefore the trade between entries and plot-error precision under one shared, random environmental shift per setPheno() call, and not between entries and independent locations. The printed acc_pyt of the runs below is cor(gv, phenotype) of the PYT at one replicate (the row 1 of Table 22.1, one call per year), whichever arm is run.

22.2 Why a breeding programme needs a burn-in

The loop below advances every stage by one year, simultaneously. That only means something if all seven stages are already occupied, by cohorts of different ages: this year’s elite trial was crossed five years before this year’s crossing block (the cohort in the DH stage was crossed one year before it, the headrows two, the PYT three, the AYT four, the EYT five). Starting the loop from an empty programme would compare a year-1 elite trial that does not exist against a year-1 headrow that does.

The fix is the staggered fill in the course’s vignette, and it is the most confusing seven lines in it:

for (year in 1:7) {
  F1 <- randCross(Parents, n_crosses)
  if (year < 7) DH   <- makeDH(F1, n_dh)
  if (year < 6) HDRW <- setPheno(DH, reps = rep_hdrw)
  if (year < 5) PYT  <- ...
  if (year < 4) AYT  <- ...
  if (year < 3) EYT  <- ...
}

Read it as a triangle rather than a loop. On the first pass every stage runs, so every stage holds a cohort. On the third pass the elite trial is skipped (it runs on the first two only), so the cohort sitting in it is older than the one behind it; the advanced trial is skipped from the fourth pass, the preliminary trial from the fifth, the headrows from the sixth and the DH production from the seventh. By the seventh pass only the crossing block still runs. What comes out is a programme in which each stage holds a cohort of the right age relative to its neighbours – a steady state, which is the only state in which a per-year genetic gain is a meaningful number. (The function below writes the thresholds as offsets of n_burn, which gives the same thresholds, 7 down to 3, at n_burn = 7.)

22.3 One programme, fifteen years

The whole scheme is one function. It is written out rather than packaged because the loop is the thing being taught, and because a reader adapting it to their own programme will edit the loop, not call it. Its argument arm selects one of five programmes, all sharing the burn-in and the stage sizes of sch:

  • "ps", the phenotypic programme: selection on phenotype throughout; parents are all n_eyt + n_ayt lines of the EYT and the AYT, so there is no truncation at the parents’ step.
  • "l1", lever 1 alone: from year gs_from, the PYT-to-AYT step uses a surrogate genomic value; parents as in "ps".
  • "l2", lever 2 alone: from year gs_from, parents are the best n_eyt + n_ayt of the n_eyt + n_ayt + n_pyt lines of EYT, AYT and PYT by the surrogate genomic value (raw phenotype for the EYT and AYT lines, the surrogate for the PYT lines); the PYT-to-AYT step is phenotypic.
  • "gs", both levers.
  • "ctl", a control for lever 2: parents are the best n_eyt + n_ayt of the same n_eyt + n_ayt + n_pyt lines, truncated by plain phenotype, with no surrogate anywhere.

The surrogate genomic value is a phenotype of the same line simulated at gs_boost times the PYT replication, in a separate setPheno() call; its accuracy is the row 3 of Table 22.1 (0.54 with GxE, 0.66 without), and it is a phenotypic correlation, not a prediction accuracy fitted from markers. In "l2" and "ctl" the candidates come from different cohorts and different setPheno() calls, so the selection criterion compares lines measured with unequal precision and under different GxE deviates; this is part of what the arms do, and the control inherits it.

Show code 
gd_of <- function(pop, SP, loci = c("qtl", "neutral")) {
  loci <- match.arg(loci)
  g <- pullSegSiteGeno(pop, simParam = SP)
  is_q <- colnames(g) %in% colnames(pullQtlGeno(pop, simParam = SP))
  1 - mean(molecular_coancestry(
    g[, if (loci == "qtl") is_q else !is_q, drop = FALSE] / 2))
}

run_programme <- function(cfg, seed, arm = c("ps", "l1", "l2", "gs", "ctl")) {
  arm <- match.arg(arm)
  lever1 <- arm %in% c("l1", "gs")   # surrogate genomic value for PYT -> AYT
  lever2 <- arm %in% c("l2", "gs")   # parents from c(EYT, AYT, PYT)
  stopifnot(cfg$n_burn >= 7)
  # founders: re-drawn per arm from the same seed (quickHaplo() is deterministic)
  set.seed(seed)
  fg <- quickHaplo(nInd = cfg$n_founders, nChr = cfg$n_chr,
                   segSites = cfg$n_qtl + cfg$n_neutral, inbred = TRUE)
  SP <- SimParam$new(fg)
  SP$addTraitAG(nQtlPerChr = cfg$n_qtl, mean = 4, var = 0.1, varGxE = 0.2)
  ph <- function(p, r) setPheno(p, varE = cfg$var_e, reps = r, simParam = SP)

  # --- burn-in: fill every stage with a cohort of the right age ---
  Parents <- newPop(fg, simParam = SP)
  gd_founders <- gd_of(Parents, SP)
  nb <- cfg$n_burn
  for (year in 1:nb) {
    F1 <- randCross(Parents, cfg$n_crosses, simParam = SP)
    if (year < nb)     DH   <- makeDH(F1, cfg$n_dh, simParam = SP)
    if (year < nb - 1) HDRW <- ph(DH, cfg$rep_hdrw)
    if (year < nb - 2) {
      PYT <- selectWithinFam(HDRW, nInd = cfg$fam_max, use = "pheno", simParam = SP)
      PYT <- ph(selectInd(PYT, nInd = cfg$n_pyt, use = "pheno", simParam = SP),
                cfg$rep_pyt)
    }
    if (year < nb - 3)
      AYT <- ph(selectInd(PYT, nInd = cfg$n_ayt, use = "pheno", simParam = SP),
                cfg$rep_ayt)
    if (year < nb - 4)
      EYT <- ph(selectInd(AYT, nInd = cfg$n_eyt, use = "pheno", simParam = SP),
                cfg$rep_eyt)
  }

  # --- advancing years, last stage first so each reads the previous cohort ---
  out <- data.frame(year = 1:cfg$n_years, meanG = NA_real_, varG = NA_real_,
                    acc_pyt = NA_real_, gd_pyt = NA_real_, gd_parents = NA_real_,
                    gd_pyt_neutral = NA_real_, par_eyt = NA_integer_,
                    par_ayt = NA_integer_, par_pyt = NA_integer_)
  for (year in 1:cfg$n_years) {
    on <- year >= cfg$gs_from          # the switch of the arm is on from this year

    # (the released variety, the best EYT entry, is not tracked: the chapter
    #  measures the pipeline, not the released line)
    EYT <- ph(selectInd(AYT, nInd = cfg$n_eyt, use = "pheno", simParam = SP),
              cfg$rep_eyt)

    if (lever1 && on) {  # lever 1: a more accurate criterion at the PYT stage
      PYT@ebv <- setPheno(PYT, varE = cfg$var_e,
                          reps = cfg$rep_pyt * cfg$gs_boost,
                          onlyPheno = TRUE, simParam = SP)
      AYT <- ph(selectInd(PYT, nInd = cfg$n_ayt, use = "ebv", simParam = SP),
                cfg$rep_ayt)
    } else {
      AYT <- ph(selectInd(PYT, nInd = cfg$n_ayt, use = "pheno", simParam = SP),
                cfg$rep_ayt)
    }

    PYT <- selectWithinFam(HDRW, nInd = cfg$fam_max, use = "pheno", simParam = SP)
    PYT <- ph(selectInd(PYT, nInd = cfg$n_pyt, use = "pheno", simParam = SP),
              cfg$rep_pyt)
    HDRW <- ph(DH, cfg$rep_hdrw)
    DH   <- makeDH(F1, cfg$n_dh, simParam = SP)

    if (lever2 && on) {  # lever 2: recycle parents a year earlier, from the PYT
      PYT@ebv <- setPheno(PYT, varE = cfg$var_e,
                          reps = cfg$rep_pyt * cfg$gs_boost,
                          onlyPheno = TRUE, simParam = SP)
      EYT@ebv <- pheno(EYT); AYT@ebv <- pheno(AYT)
      Parents <- selectInd(c(EYT, AYT, PYT), nInd = cfg$n_eyt + cfg$n_ayt,
                           use = "ebv", simParam = SP)
    } else if (arm == "ctl" && on) {   # control: the same wider set, plain phenotype
      Parents <- selectInd(c(EYT, AYT, PYT), nInd = cfg$n_eyt + cfg$n_ayt,
                           use = "pheno", simParam = SP)
    } else {
      Parents <- c(EYT, AYT)
    }
    F1 <- randCross(Parents, cfg$n_crosses, simParam = SP)

    out$meanG[year]      <- meanG(PYT)
    out$varG[year]       <- varG(PYT)
    out$acc_pyt[year]    <- stats::cor(PYT@gv[, 1], PYT@pheno[, 1])
    out$gd_pyt[year]     <- gd_of(PYT, SP)
    out$gd_parents[year] <- gd_of(Parents, SP)
    if (cfg$n_neutral > 0) out$gd_pyt_neutral[year] <- gd_of(PYT, SP, "neutral")
    out$par_eyt[year] <- sum(Parents@id %in% EYT@id)
    out$par_ayt[year] <- sum(Parents@id %in% AYT@id)
    out$par_pyt[year] <- sum(Parents@id %in% PYT@id)
  }
  out$gd_founders <- gd_founders
  out
}

Two details are worth pausing on.

The advancing loop runs backwards. The elite trial is filled before the advanced trial is refilled, which is before the preliminary trial is refilled. Written in the other order, EYT would be selected from an AYT that had already been overwritten with this year’s cohort, and the programme would advance two stages per year without anyone noticing. Ordering is the whole correctness argument of a staged simulation.

Progress is reported at the PYT. It is the first stage with a yield phenotype and the last with enough entries for a variance to mean anything – meanG on four elite lines is noise. This is the course’s choice and it is the right one.

The phenotypic arm on seed 1 is the first run (Table 22.2).

Genetic mean rises, genetic variance falls, and gene diversity of the preliminary trial falls with it. Nothing here is surprising; what matters is that all three are now being measured on the same run.

ImportantThese numbers are reproducible

The founders come from quickHaplo(), which is seeded by set.seed(), so run_programme(sch, seed = 1) returns the same data frame every time – unlike Chapter 9’s Step 8, whose runMacs2() founders are not reproducible. That is what makes the paired comparison below legitimate: the phenotypic and genomic arms are run from the same seed, so they share founders, and every difference between them is attributable to the scheme.

This is a documented exception to rule 1 of Chapter 9’s Step 1 (found the founders once and branch every arm off that object): each arm here re-draws its founders from the same seed. It is safe only because quickHaplo() is plain R sampling; the design chunk below asserts, for every seed, that years 1 to 5 (burn-in and the years before the arms differ) are identical across the five arms, which they could not be if the founders differed.

22.4 Genomic selection: two levers, separated

Genomic selection is usually described as buying accuracy. In a staged programme it can buy two things, and the arms above have a switch for each:

  1. Accuracy earlier in the cycle (lever 1). A prediction is available on a line that has been genotyped but barely phenotyped, so the advance from preliminary to advanced trial can be made on better information than one replicate of yield.
  2. A shorter generation interval (lever 2). Parents can be recycled from the preliminary trial instead of waiting for the elite trial. Define the cycle length \(T\) of a parent as the years from the crossing that produced its cohort to the crossing that uses it. By the loop above a cross in year \(t\) gives DH lines in year \(t+1\), headrows in \(t+2\), the PYT in \(t+3\), the AYT in \(t+4\) and the EYT in \(t+5\), and the parents are chosen in the year their cohort is in the trial: \(T = 3\), \(4\), \(5\) years for a parent taken from the PYT, the AYT and the EYT.

The breeder’s equation of Chapter 4, \(R = i\,r\,\sigma_A\) (Equation 4.1), is the response to one round of selection: \(i\) the intensity, \(r\) the accuracy and \(\sigma_A\) the additive standard deviation. A response per year is that response divided by the years \(T\) a round takes. That division is the chapter’s own bookkeeping, not an equation of Chapter 4, and it assumes discrete rounds of a common length (assumption A4 of Chapter 10), which this overlapping programme, with parents of three different lengths, only approximates. It is used below to read a direction, not to predict a number.

Two statements in the literature are about these levers, and neither transfers as a ratio. Gaynor et al. (2017) report, in their simulations, that a genomic programme that changes accuracy only gained 1.21 (95 % confidence interval, CI, 1.18-1.25) to 1.39 times the gain of the conventional programme, and the two-part programmes, which also shorten the cycle, 2.36 to 2.47 times (p. 2379; the ranges span their genotype-by-year variance settings, 0 and 10); they write that in the two-part programmes “the shortening of breeding cycle time was the major driver of increased genetic gain” (p. 2381). Their genomic programmes are cost-matched (fewer DH lines per cross to pay for genotyping, Table 1), whereas the genomic arms here are free. And Meuwissen et al. (2001) state in their abstract that marker-based selection could substantially increase the rate of genetic gain, especially if combined with reproductive techniques that shorten the generation interval (the full text was not consulted for this chapter). That the second lever is the larger is thus reported for one simulated design; what follows measures it for this one.

This chapter mimics the prediction rather than fitting one: ebv is filled with a phenotype simulated at gs_boost times the replication, which is a stand-in for a genomic prediction of the corresponding accuracy. Chapter 14 is where the book actually fits prediction models and measures what accuracy is attainable; the honest statement here is that this chapter assumes an accuracy (gs_boost = 3, the row 3 of Table 22.1) and studies its consequences, rather than earning it.

The same seed with both levers on (Table 22.3):

Show code 
gs1 <- run_programme(sch, seed = 1, arm = "gs")
knitr::kable(fmt(gs1[c(1, 5, 10, 15), cols1]), col.names = names1, row.names = FALSE)

Years 1 to 5 are identical to the phenotypic arm, digit for digit (asserted for every seed in Section 22.5), because the genomic switches only turn on in year 6. The arms share a history, so the comparison is paired rather than between independent runs; it is a pairing of two arms from one history, not the random-selection control of Chapter 24, which is a different design.

22.5 The comparison, replicated

One run of one seed is an anecdote. Five arms are cheap enough to replicate. The design runs all five arms on 120 seeds (600 programme runs, several minutes to execute); the seeds are read in five batches of 24, because 24 seeds is a size a single experiment of this kind can easily have and the batches show how far such an experiment moves.

Show code 
arms <- c(ps = "phenotypic", l1 = "lever 1 alone", l2 = "lever 2 alone",
          gs = "both levers", ctl = "control (phenotype-only truncation)")
seeds   <- 1:120
n_batch <- 24
batch   <- rep(seq_len(length(seeds) / n_batch), each = n_batch)
stopifnot(length(seeds) == 120, n_batch == 24, n_probe == 48)  # constants typed in captions
runs <- lapply(setNames(names(arms), names(arms)), function(a)
  lapply(seeds, function(s) run_programme(sch, s, a)))

# The pairing rests on identical history: years 1-5 equal across arms, every seed
for (a in names(arms)[-1]) for (i in seq_along(seeds))
  stopifnot(identical(runs[[a]][[i]][1:5, ], runs[["ps"]][[i]][1:5, ]))
stopifnot(identical(ps1, runs[["ps"]][[1]]), identical(gs1, runs[["gs"]][[1]]))

last <- sch$n_years
rate <- function(d)                       # slope of PYT genetic mean on year
  unname(stats::coef(stats::lm(meanG ~ year,
                               data = d[d$year >= sch$gs_from, ]))[2])
per_seed <- function(f) sapply(runs, function(r) vapply(r, f, numeric(1)))

Gain. The phenotypic arm gains 0.0575 (0.0013) per year (mean and s.e.). Relative to it, lever 1 alone adds 0.0086 (t = 7.2), lever 2 alone 0.0267 (t = 21.3) and both levers 0.0294 (t = 21.1); the ratio of the mean rates, both levers over phenotypic, is 1.51 (95 % bootstrap interval over seeds, 1.46 to 1.56). The two single effects add to 0.0353, more than the joint 0.0294, and adding lever 1 to lever 2 gives 0.0027 (t = 2.0). In this simulation, therefore, lever 2 contributes the larger part of the gain and lever 1 adds little once lever 2 is on. That is one configuration, with an assumed surrogate accuracy; it does not show that the second lever is normally the larger. Nor is it a statement about lever 1 in general: the PYT genetic mean that measures the gain is a lagged readout of a selection made one stage later, so lever 1 shows in it only through the parents.

The control recycles parents from the same wider set, but by plain phenotype. It gains 0.0184 per year over the phenotypic arm (t = 15.8), which is 69 % of lever 2’s gain, and differs from lever 2 alone by -0.0083 (t = -6.4). The mean cycle lengths \(T\) of Table 22.4 are 4.25 years for the phenotypic arm, 3.34 for lever 2 alone and 3.26 for the control. If \(i\,r\,\sigma_A\) per round were unchanged, the gain per year would scale as \(1/T\), by the ratio 1.27 for lever 2 alone and 1.30 for the control; the observed ratios of mean gains are 1.46 (95 % bootstrap interval over seeds, 1.42 to 1.51) and 1.32 (1.28 to 1.36). Lever 2’s ratio exceeds the ratio of cycle lengths by 0.19 (95 % interval, one resample of seeds for gains and cycle lengths, 0.14 to 0.24), an interval that excludes zero; the control’s exceeds it by 0.017 (-0.025 to 0.059), an interval that includes zero. The control’s ratio is therefore of the size of the shortening of the cycle, within this resolution; lever 2’s is larger. This is consistent with the surrogate genomic value in the parent choice adding to the accuracy \(r\) or the intensity \(i\) beyond what the shorter cycle gives, but neither \(i\) nor \(r\) is measured per round here, the rates are averages over a transient, and \(T\) is a mean of three parent sources; it is a reading, not a decomposition. The premise itself is a reference point, not a description of either arm: lever 2 and the control also change the intensity at the parent step, which takes the best 16 of 76 lines instead of all 16.

The year-15 loss of PYT diversity that goes with lever 2 is thus reproduced, within the resolution of 120 seeds (the standard error of the control-minus-lever-2 difference in the PYT is 0.0040), by truncating the same wider candidate set on plain phenotype. It does not need a genomic value. What it goes with is a different parent step: the phenotypic arm takes all 16 lines of the EYT and the AYT as parents, with no truncation, whereas the lever-2 arms and the control take the best 16 of 76, and 11.7 of them (lever 2) or 12.7 (control) come from the PYT. Lever 1 alone, whose parents are chosen as in the phenotypic arm, lowers the diversity of the parent set but its effect on the PYT is not resolved at this number of seeds; that is a statement about resolution, not about absence.

What was not measured is why. A natural hypothesis is that parents truncated from a wider set, and taken from a preliminary trial in which full sibs have not yet been separated by later trials, are more closely related to each other, and that a shorter interval accumulates coancestry faster per unit of time. This chapter does not test it: it records the gene diversity of the parent set, not the coancestry among the chosen parents, nor their family structure. Gaynor et al. (2017) give a different mechanism for the loss of genetic variance under genomic selection: the Bulmer effect of the increased selection accuracy in early stages, with effective population size, selection intensity, accuracy and the number of cycles per year as the factors of later changes (p. 2383). They name optimal contribution selection, with introgression, as an established remedy for the decline of genetic variance (p. 2382). That concerns variance, not molecular gene diversity, and supports the direction, not this mechanism.

WarningGene diversity with the diagonal is not comparable across set sizes

These three levels are not on one scale: for fully homozygous lines the diagonal of \(f\) is 1, so that

\[ \theta = \frac{1}{n} + \Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{n}\Bigr)\theta_{\text{off}}, \qquad GD = \Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{n}\Bigr)GD_{\text{off}}, \tag{22.1}\]

where \(\theta_{\text{off}}\) is the mean of \(f_{ij}\) over pairs of distinct lines and \(GD_{\text{off}} = 1 - \theta_{\text{off}}\) is the gene diversity between distinct lines (Equation 22.1 is checked in the chunk above on a random matrix of homozygous lines). \(GD_{\text{off}}\) does not carry the weight \(1/n\), which differs among \(n\) = 40 (founders), 60 (PYT) and 16 (parents). On that scale the founders are at 0.5000, the year-1 PYT at 0.4761 and the year-1 parent set at 0.4731. A part of the diversity of the founders has therefore gone before the first row of the table, by 0.027 for the parents on the \(GD_{\text{off}}\) scale, not by the 0.044 that the levels above suggest, 0.017 of which is the difference in diagonal weight between \(n\) = 40 and \(n\) = 16.

These numbers are not the quantity the book calls \(\alpha\). The \(\alpha\) of Equation 11.15 (Section 11.9) is \((GD_{\text{ref}} - GD_S)/GD_{\text{ref}}\), with \(GD_{\text{ref}}\) the mean gene diversity of random subsets of the same size as the selected set, so that \(\alpha = 0\) is a random sample and \(\alpha < 0\) is possible. The quantity in Table 22.8 is the cumulative relative change over fifteen years of one population against its own earlier value (year 1, or the founders): there is no reference distribution of random subsets, it includes whatever drift and burn-in do to a set of that size, and it can be compared with an \(\alpha\) budget of Chapter 15 or Chapter 16 only as a different kind of number. I call it the relative loss against year 1 (or against the founders). Against year 1 the numerator and the denominator are gene diversities of sets of the same size, so the factor \((1 - 1/n)\) of the diagonal (Equation 22.1) cancels exactly and the ranking and the levels of those columns do not depend on it. Against the founders they are sets of different size (\(n\) = 60 or 16 over 40), and the columns of Table 22.8 are therefore computed on \(GD_{\text{off}}\). Left on the \(GD\) with the diagonal, the loss of the phenotypic arm against the founders would have been 0.249 (PYT) and 0.254 (parents) instead of 0.256 and 0.224, and that of the both-levers arm 0.313 instead of 0.285 for the parents: the difference is a sample-size artefact, not loss.

status_number() on a set of lines returns \(N_s = 1/(2\theta)\). At biallelic loci the group coancestry satisfies \(\theta \in [1/2, 1]\), so \(N_s \in [1/2, 1]\) for every set of lines, whatever its size (Section 11.3, Chapter 7): \(N_s\) is a monotone rescaling of \(\theta\), not a head count. 22.6.1 Selection or bottleneck?

All 600 segregating sites of the design are QTL (the chunk above asserts it: segSites equals nQtlPerChr). The gene diversity of the ledger is therefore measured at causal loci, which selection acts on directly, and no neutral locus is measured in the main design. The chapter also has no no-selection arm, so the loss in Table 22.8 cannot be split into what selection did and what a bottleneck of 16 parents and 20 crosses a year would have done anyway. (Chapter 9’s Step 8 builds a paired no-selection control for the hybrid programme; this chapter has none.) One probe bears on the question. It re-runs the phenotypic and the both-levers arms on 48 seeds with 60 neutral sites added per chromosome beside the QTL (n_neutral = 60, so segSites = 120 and the founders differ from the main design), and measures the relative loss against year 1 at the QTL loci and at the neutral loci separately (Table 22.9).

At neutral loci the phenotypic arm loses 97 % (95 % bootstrap interval over seeds, 95-99 %) of what it loses at the QTL loci and the both-levers arm 95 % (95 % bootstrap interval over seeds, 93-96 %). The within-seed excess of the QTL loss over the neutral loss is resolved in both arms (Table 22.9: t = 2.9 for the phenotypic arm and 6.4 for the both-levers arm, beyond the critical value 2.01), and larger in the faster arm (t of the difference of the two excesses = 2.7). So the loss is mostly, not entirely, shared with neutral loci: it is consistent with a loss that is mainly a bottleneck effect plus a smaller part, resolved here, that is specific to the loci selection acts on or to what is linked to them. It does not separate the two: neutral loci here lie on the same ten chromosomes as the QTL, and loci linked to selected ones lose diversity with them, so the neutral loss is not a drift-only measure; and the difference between the arms is present at neutral loci too (0.036 against 0.044 at the QTL loci, in the relative loss), so the extra loss of the faster arm is not confined to the causal loci either. A random-parent arm would separate selection from bottleneck; it is not built here.

What would an \(\alpha\) budget mean here? Not a constraint on which hybrids to sell, as in Chapter 16, but a constraint on which parents to recycle: the same optimal-contribution problem of Chapter 15, applied to the c(EYT, AYT, PYT) candidate set that the lever-2 arms select from. The candidate set is small, the coancestry matrix is cheap, and the choice being constrained is annual. Chapter 15 and Chapter 16 were developed and tested on hybrid candidates, Chapter 23 follows the trajectories of constrained and unconstrained hybrid programmes forward, and Chapter 24 tests whether the cumulative gain can be forecast from the rate of inbreeding; none of this is tested on the inbred-line programme of this chapter, and the application to line parents is a proposal.

22.7 Assumptions

What this models. Staged testing with replication rising across stages, genotype-by-environment interaction as a variance component (SP$addTraitAG(varGxE = ), here 0.2 against a founder additive variance of 0.1, entering as one shared deviate per setPheno() call, as in Section 22.1.1), doubled-haploid line development, within-family caps, a steady-state programme reached through a burn-in, one additive trait with no dominance (assumption A7 of Chapter 10), and two genomic-selection levers with a switch each, plus a phenotype-only control for the second.

What it does not. Genomic prediction is mimicked by adding replication, not fitted – so the accuracy is assumed rather than earned (its value, under the chapter’s GxE, is in Table 22.1), and the training population, its size, and its relationship to the selection candidates play no role (Chapter 14 is where they do). There is one trait, so the index questions of Chapter 9’s Step 7 do not arise. Locations differ only through plot error and a shared GxE deviate per call: there is no correlated environment structure, no year effect, and no location-by-year term, so a “replicate” here is an independent draw of plot error only and not an independent location (the GxE deviate is common to all entries of a call and is not averaged by reps). Doubled-haploid production is instantaneous and never fails. Founders come from quickHaplo(), so they carry no linkage disequilibrium – Chapter 9’s Step 1 measures what that costs and argues it is the right trade for a chapter about response rather than about marker-QTL association. Every segregating site is a QTL, so the marker panel is the causal set and gene diversity is measured at causal loci; this is the limit Chapter 10 (assumption A5) states. There is no no-selection or random-parent arm, so selection and the bottleneck are not separated (the probe above is suggestive only). The base against which loss is reported is the programme’s year 1 (or the founders), not a frozen allele-frequency base (assumption A6) and not a random-subset reference.

Against the assumptions of Chapter 10. The chapter keeps A2 (fully inbred lines, by DH) and A7 (no dominance). It leaves A1 (an allogamous crop and an F1 hybrid product: the product here is a line) and A4 (discrete cycles: the cycles overlap, a stage holds cohorts of five different ages, and the parents of one cycle come from three stages, so the cycle length \(T\) is a mean of 3, 4 and 5 years and a per-cycle rate is not computed). The results of Chapter 10 and of the hybrid chapters do not transfer to this chapter by that route, nor this chapter’s to theirs.

Rate window. The gain per year is the slope over years 6 to 15, which includes the years before a parent chosen by a genomic arm in year 6 reaches the PYT (three years for a PYT parent): it averages a transient, with a lag that differs between arms.

Scale. 40 founders, 600 loci, 15 years, 120 paired seeds per arm, all live at render. Gaynor et al. (2017)’s programme starts from 50 founders, against 40 here, and is larger in every other dimension of the chunk above (crosses, DH lines per cross, headrows, entries of the three trials, QTN) by factors from 2.5 (EYT entries) to 35 (QTN). The configuration is the author’s own reduction (see Section 22.1). The qualitative results here are the ones this simulation shows at this scale; they have not been measured at another, and the absolute rates are not comparable to a real wheat programme. Gaynor et al.’s genomic programmes are cost-matched and the arms here are not, so ratios of gain are not comparable with theirs either.

22.8 Where this connects

Chapter 9 supplies every primitive this chapter uses and builds them into the hybrid scheme instead; the two chapters are the same toolkit pointed at the two kinds of programme. Chapter 4 states the breeder’s equation for one round of selection; the per-year form used here is the division by the cycle length stated above. Chapter 14 is where an accuracy of the kind this chapter assumes is measured, on a trait generated from the markers the models see; there it is finite because of training size, noise and the rank bound, not because of a marker-versus-QTL gap. This book does not measure that gap, and Chapter 10 (assumption A5) states its collapse as a limit. How many crosses, entries and locations to plant for a fixed number of plots is the subject of Chapter 18, Chapter 19 and Chapter 20, for hybrids.

The diversity result belongs to the book’s spine. Chapter 23 shows the same tension – faster response, faster erosion – with the package’s own simulator over a longer horizon, for the hybrid programme; Chapter 24 tests whether the cumulative gain can be forecast from the rate of erosion (three budgets tested; the 15-cycle gain is reached for one of them, in the five-draw mean) and what the frequency-based metrics cannot see; Chapter 15 is the machinery for spending the budget deliberately rather than discovering afterwards what was spent.

Reproduce this chapter

All the code of this chapter, in order and with the explanation kept as comments, is one script: 04d-breeding-programme.R. Download it, install hybdiv, and run it top to bottom in an empty directory; cached results are fetched on first use.
