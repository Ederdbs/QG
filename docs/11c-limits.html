19  The limit, and what the metrics cannot see

Chapter 18 ran the programme forward and found the crossover: a constrained programme trails an unconstrained one for about eight cycles and leads thereafter. That is a measurement. This chapter asks the two questions a measurement leaves open – could it have been predicted, and is the quantity being managed the quantity that actually matters.

The answers are yes-with-a-condition, and no-not-entirely.

19.1 Robertson’s limit

Chapter 3 wrote down the response to one cycle of selection. Robertson (1960) wrote down the sum of all of them. Selection fixes alleles; a finite population fixes some of the favourable ones in the wrong direction; and the total advance before everything is fixed is approximately

\[R_{\text{total}} \;\approx\; 2 N_e R_1\]

with a half-life of about \(1.4 N_e\) generations. The result is the theoretical ancestor of every optimal-contribution scheme in this book: it is why \(N_e\) is the thing to manage, and it says exactly what a unit of \(N_e\) is worth in units of cumulative gain.

Both inputs are already in Chapter 18’ trajectory. \(R_1\) is the first cycle’s response, and \(N_e\) comes from the rate of inbreeding, exactly as Chapter 18 computed it:

rob <- do.call(rbind, lapply(unique(traj$budget), function(b) {
  d  <- aggregate(cbind(index, F_drift) ~ cycle, traj[traj$budget == b, ], mean)
  f  <- lm(log(1 - F_drift) ~ cycle, d[-1, ])
  Ne <- 1 / (-2 * coef(f)[["cycle"]])
  R1 <- d$index[2] - d$index[1]
  data.frame(budget = b, R2 = summary(f)$r.squared, Ne = Ne, R1 = R1,
             limit = 2 * Ne * R1, half_life = 1.4 * Ne,
             observed_15 = d$index[16] - d$index[1],
             predicted_15 = 2 * Ne * R1 * (1 - (1 - 1 / (2 * Ne))^15))
}))
fmt(rob, 3)

Read the last two columns against each other, and then read both against the first.

19.2 The \(R^2\) column decides whether the theory applies

For the tightest budget the prediction is 10.03 and the observation is 9.98. Robertson’s theory, published in 1960 and derived for a single locus under idealised drift, predicts fifteen cycles of a genomic hybrid programme to better than one percent.

For the unconstrained programme it predicts 15.69 against an observed 7.81 – too high by about half.

The theory did not fail arbitrarily, and Chapter 18 already said why. Robertson assumes a constant rate of inbreeding, and the regression of \(\log(1-F)\) on cycle is where that assumption is testable. Its \(R^2\) is 0.998 for the tightest budget and 0.853 for the unconstrained run, whose drift decelerates because there is progressively less left to lose. That low \(R^2\) is not a nuisance. It is the theory declining to apply, in advance, and in a column you can read before you trust the number.

ImportantDo not quote a Robertson limit without its \(R^2\)

\(N_e\) here is a fitted quantity, and Chapter 18 showed the fit is meaningless for a saturating series – its fitted \(\Delta F\) comes out lowest for the run that is actually losing diversity fastest, which is backwards. Feeding that \(N_e\) into \(2 N_e R_1\) propagates the error into a number that looks like a long-term plan.

Report the \(R^2\) next to every limit, and treat a limit computed from a fit below about 0.99 as an illustration rather than a projection.

That yields an argument for the constraint that this book did not previously have. Chapter 18 justified \(\alpha\) by where the trajectories cross. The table above adds: the constrained programme is the one whose future can be computed. An unconstrained programme is not merely worse after cycle 8, it is unforecastable, because the theory that would forecast it needs a constant \(\Delta F\) that it does not have.

19.3 What the metrics cannot see

Now the second question. Every diversity metric in this book – \(\theta\), \(GD\), \(N_s\), allelic richness, F_hom, F_drift – is a function of allele frequencies one locus at a time. Chapter 3 showed that additive variance is not:

\[\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta) \;=\; V_{\text{genic}} \;+\; 2\sum_{k<l}\beta_k\beta_l\operatorname{Cov}(x_k, x_l)\]

Directional selection drives that covariance term negative (Bulmer (1971)): it couples loci with like effects in repulsion, hiding genic variance from the next generation without moving a single allele frequency. No metric in the catalogue can detect it.

Chapter 18 assumed the effect away, in assumption C1: loci are unlinked, so no linkage disequilibrium builds up and there is no Bulmer effect on the genic side. That is worth testing rather than assuming, because Bulmer’s 1971 treatment covers unlinked loci explicitly – free recombination halves the disequilibrium each generation, but selection regenerates it every generation, and the equilibrium is not zero.

19.3.1 The experiment

The gap \(\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta) - V_{\text{genic}}\) is not on its own a measure of the Bulmer effect. A finite founder base leaves disequilibrium in the very first cycle, and two divergent pools add more. So the comparison is against a control: an arm identical in every respect – same founders, same seeds, same recycling, same population sizes – in which the hybrids are chosen at random instead of on the index.

gapc <- aggregate(gap ~ regime + cycle, vr, mean)
w <- reshape(gapc, idvar = "cycle", timevar = "regime", direction = "wide")
names(w) <- sub("gap.", "", names(w))
w$selection_effect <- w$unconstrained - w$random
fmt(head(w[, c("cycle", "random", "unconstrained", "constrained",
               "selection_effect")], 7), 4)

Cycle 0 is identical across all three arms, to the digit. Nothing has been selected yet, so the control is doing its job.

From cycle 1 the arms separate. Paired within seed and cycle:

p  <- reshape(aggregate(gap ~ regime + seed + cycle, vr, mean),
              idvar = c("seed", "cycle"), timevar = "regime", direction = "wide")
p  <- p[p$cycle > 0, ]
dd <- p$gap.unconstrained - p$gap.random
c(mean_effect = mean(dd), se = sd(dd) / sqrt(length(dd)),
  t = mean(dd) / (sd(dd) / sqrt(length(dd))), n_pairs = length(dd))
#> mean_effect          se           t     n_pairs 
#> -0.08568088  0.02108980 -4.06266819 96.00000000
WarningAssumption C1 of Chapter 18 is too strong, and is restated here

Selection builds negative disequilibrium among unlinked loci, at \(t = -4.06\) over 96 paired observations. Unlinked loci do not prevent the Bulmer effect; they make it decay fast.

What C1 should say is that with free recombination the disequilibrium is halved every generation, so it reaches a low equilibrium and does not accumulate across cycles the way frequency change does. That is what makes Chapter 18’ frequency trajectories trustworthy, and it is the correct reason – not the absence of the effect.

The effect is largest in the early cycles and decays toward zero late, which is the expected shape: it is proportional to the genic variance still available to be hidden, and by cycle 12 there is much less of it.

19.3.2 With real linkage

The same question with real meiosis behind it. Chapter 7’s reciprocal recurrent selection runs on a coalescent founder history with a genetic map, and AlphaSimR reports varA (which carries the disequilibrium) and genicVarA (which does not), so this arm needs no variance code of its own.

lk$rel <- ((lk$varA_A - lk$genicVarA_A) + (lk$varA_B - lk$genicVarA_B)) /
          (lk$genicVarA_A + lk$genicVarA_B)
pl  <- reshape(lk[, c("regime", "seed", "cycle", "rel")],
               idvar = c("seed", "cycle"), timevar = "regime", direction = "wide")
pl  <- pl[pl$cycle > 0, ]
ddl <- pl$rel.selected - pl$rel.random
c(mean_effect = mean(ddl), se = sd(ddl) / sqrt(length(ddl)),
  t = mean(ddl) / (sd(ddl) / sqrt(length(ddl))), n_pairs = length(ddl))
#> mean_effect          se           t     n_pairs 
#> -0.16493928  0.04672916 -3.52968674 32.00000000

Same sign, larger in magnitude, and measured with fewer replicates so the evidence is weaker. Linkage does what linkage should: it slows the decay, so the disequilibrium selection creates persists longer than it does under free recombination.

NoteA reproducibility trap in the linked arm

alphasimr_founder_pools() is not reproducible from cfg$seed. AlphaSimR::runMacs2() hands the coalescent to MaCS, which seeds itself outside R’s RNG, so two calls with the same configuration return different founder haplotypes. Everything downstream of the founders is seeded normally.

The consequence for an experiment like this one is severe: founding the pools separately for each arm would have compared two different base populations and charged the difference to selection. book/scripts/regenerate.R founds once per replicate and branches both arms off that single result, which is why cycle 0 of the table above matches exactly.

19.4 The two costs of selection

The chapter’s result stated once.

Selection pays for gain twice. It builds negative covariance between loci, which is a real loss of usable variance, and recombination repays it – halved every generation under free recombination, more slowly under linkage. And it changes allele frequencies, moving them toward fixation, and nothing repays that short of mutation or migration.

The first cost is invisible to every metric in this book and is largely self-correcting. The second is what every metric in this book measures, and is permanent.

That is the justification for constraining the second and ignoring the first, and it is a stronger statement than “the constraint is what we can compute”. It is worth being explicit that this ordering is a consequence of the two decay rates, not an assumption: if the disequilibrium did not decay – tight linkage, few chromosomes, short cycle times – the ranking would change, and a constraint written on allele frequencies would be managing the smaller of the two bills.

19.5 Where this connects

Chapter 3 supplies the variance decomposition and the breeder’s equation this chapter runs forward. Chapter 18 supplies the trajectories, and has one assumption restated as a result of the test above. Chapter 7 supplies the linked arm, which is the only place in the book where a genetic map, rather than an assumption about one, decides how fast disequilibrium decays.

Chapter 20’s checklist gains one line from this chapter: report the \(R^2\) of the \(\log(1-F)\) regression next to any projection built on \(N_e\), and treat a projection from a poor fit as an illustration.
