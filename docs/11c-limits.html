24  The limit, and what the metrics cannot see

Chapter 23 ran the programme forward and found a crossover: the \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.02\) programme trails an unconstrained one for 8.4 cycles on average (five founder draws, crossovers from 5 to 12) and leads thereafter; the \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.005\) programme crosses at a mean of 11.4 (9 to 15). That is a simulation result under the assumptions of Chapter 23, not a measurement on a breeding programme. This chapter asks the two questions it leaves open: could the cumulative gain have been forecast from the rate of inbreeding, and is the quantity the constraint manages the only quantity that limits the response?

On these simulations the answers are, in short: a forecast built on Robertson’s result, with a rate-based \(N_e\) fitted on the same 15 cycles it is compared with, reaches the observed five-draw mean 15-cycle gain for the tightest budget (\(\alpha_{\max} = 0.005\)) and not for the other two arms; along the way it is low over the first cycles at that budget (Table 24.4): the end-point agreement coincides with a low first-cycle response and a response that decays faster than \(\Delta F\), and is not a match of the trajectory. For a single founder draw the forecast is wrong by tens of per cent even at the end point. As for the second question, the constraint does not act on the covariance between loci that selection builds, which no one-locus diversity metric can see, and that covariance lowers the realised variance below the genic variance in these runs; whether it lowers the response was not computed here.

24.1 What this chapter reads, and what it assumes

Three cached files, which differ in what a reader can regenerate:

  • cycles_trajectory.csv and cycles_crossover.csv: three budgets (unconstrained, \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.02\), \(0.005\)) \(\times\) five founder draws (seeds 1 to 5) \(\times\) cycles 0 to 15, produced by run_cycles() through book/scripts/regenerate.R cycles. Reproducible from this repository; the same file Chapter 23 reads.
  • limits_variance.csv: three regimes (random, unconstrained, \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.02\)) \(\times\) eight seeds \(\times\) cycles 0 to 12, produced by regenerate.R limits. Reproducible from this repository: a chunk below re-runs seed 1 in two arms and asserts the match.
  • limits_linked.csv: an AlphaSimR arm, four replicates \(\times\) two arms \(\times\) cycles 0 to 8, produced by regenerate.R limits. Treated as not reproducible from this repository: its founders come from runMacs2() (a coalescent run by MaCS), they were not saved, and whether a re-run under the same seed returns them was not measured (callout in the linked-arm section). It is a cached result of one run of the producer.

The unlinked arm is run_cycles() at the reduced scale of book_cfg: 25 + 25 inbred lines, \(m = 2000\) biallelic markers coded 0/0.5/1 on the hybrids, 300 QTL with known \(N(0,1)\) effects, \(F_{ST} = 0.15\) between pools, the complete \(25 \times 25\) factorial of 625 hybrids as the candidate set, and 60 hybrids selected per cycle. The selector is sel_greedy_budget() (truncation when unconstrained), the budget \(\alpha_{\max}\) is applied per cycle against the random-subset reference of that cycle, cycles are discrete, lines are recycled within pool as doubled haploids, and loci are unlinked. The selection index is the true genetic value, known without error and additive: the h2 that run_cycles() passes to simulate_pools() only perturbs a noisy index the loop does not use. Nothing in this chapter varies \(F_{ST}\), \(N\), \(n_{sel}\) or the number of markers, so none of its conclusions is verified at another scale. The index is expressed in standard deviations of the cycle-0 hybrid index. The linked arm is described where it is used.

Three things this index leaves out matter to what the chapter can claim, and other chapters treat them: the crossing plan (the complete factorial is assumed; Chapter 18, Chapter 19 and Chapter 20 treat which crosses and which locations), estimated rather than known marker effects (Chapter 14), and the choice of selector (Chapter 17).

24.2 Robertson’s limit

Chapter 4 wrote down the response to one round of selection. Robertson (1960) summed the responses over generations in a population of constant effective size \(N_e\). Selection fixes alleles; a finite population fixes some of the favourable ones in the wrong direction. For additive genes and low values of \(N_e i\) (with \(i\) the selection intensity) his summary states the total advance as \(2 N_e\) times the gain of the first generation (for a quantitative trait he credits this to Dempster, 1955), says it approaches \(4 N_e\) times that gain for larger values of \(N_e s\) (\(s\) the selective advantage of a gene), and bounds the half-life of the process by \(1.4 N_e\) generations (Robertson (1960), summary items 3 and 4, p. 234; text p. 237 and p. 241). In the symbols of this chapter:

\[R_{\text{total}} \;\approx\; 2 N_e R_1, \qquad t_{1/2} \;\le\; 1.4\, N_e \tag{24.1}\]

with \(R_{\text{total}}\) the total advance (index units), \(R_1\) the gain of the first generation (index units), \(N_e > 0\) the effective size, and \(t_{1/2}\) the half-life in generations. The first relation is an equality only for low \(N_e i\); the second is an upper bound, so “about \(1.4 N_e\)” is not what the source says. Reading this as the reason \(N_e\) is the thing to manage, or as the ancestor of the optimal-contribution schemes of this book, is this book’s interpretation: the paper does not discuss optimal contributions.

The form tested below is the chapter’s own use of the result, not a transcription of an equation of the paper. If the response of each generation is the previous one multiplied by \((1 - \Delta F)\), with \(\Delta F = 1 / (2 N_e)\), the cumulative gain after \(t\) generations is

\[G_t \;=\; R_1 \sum_{j=0}^{t-1} (1 - \Delta F)^j \;=\; 2 N_e R_1 \left[ 1 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{2 N_e}\right)^{t} \right] \tag{24.2}\]

which tends to \(2 N_e R_1\) as \(t \to \infty\). Here a generation is a cycle: cycles are discrete and non-overlapping (Chapter 10, assumption A4).

24.2.1 What is fitted, and what it is not

\(R_1\) is the mean index of the 60 hybrids selected in cycle 1 minus that of cycle 0. It is neither the genetic selection differential of Chapter 4 nor the change of the population mean that Robertson’s \(R_1\) refers to; it is the change of the mean of the selected set between two successive cycles. \(G_t\) is the same difference between cycle \(t\) and cycle 0, and \(G_{15}\) the observed 15-cycle gain.

\(N_e\) is rate-based: \(N_e = 1 / (2 \Delta F)\) with \(\Delta F = 1 - e^{\text{slope}}\), the slope of the regression of \(\log(1 - F_{drift})\) on cycle over cycles 1 to 15 of the five-draw mean series (Section 10.7.4; cycle 0 is the displacement of the selection from the base before any recycling, not a generation of drift). This is a quantity that the book declines to report for managed populations (Section 12.6, Chapter 25): \(F_{drift}\) of a managed arm is computed on the merged frequencies of the 60 selected hybrids against the frozen base \(p_0\), a level different from the drift equation of Equation 1.4, and a saturating series makes the slope an average over a curve. The contradiction is not resolved here. \(N_e\) is used below as the fitted parameter of Equation 24.2, and what the comparison tests is whether a geometric decay of the response at the fitted rate of \(F_{drift}\) describes the observed gain, not Robertson’s theorem for a population of effective size \(N_e\). For the same estimator applied to a neutral arm (equal usage, no selection), see Chapter 23; this chapter does not recompute it.

Show code 
# Fit of one series (cycle, index, F_drift); t_end = last cycle used in the fit
rob_fit <- function(d, t_end = 15) {
  d  <- d[order(d$cycle), ]
  f  <- lm(log(1 - F_drift) ~ cycle, d[d$cycle %in% 1:t_end, ])
  dF <- 1 - exp(coef(f)[["cycle"]]); Ne <- 1 / (2 * dF)
  R1 <- d$index[2] - d$index[1]
  c(R2 = summary(f)$r.squared, dF = dF, Ne = Ne, R1 = R1,
    G15_obs = d$index[d$cycle == 15] - d$index[1],
    G15_pred = 2 * Ne * R1 * (1 - (1 - 1 / (2 * Ne))^15))
}
bud   <- c("unconstrained", "0.020", "0.005")
smean <- function(b, seeds = 1:5)         # mean series over founder draws
  aggregate(cbind(index, F_drift) ~ cycle,
            do.call(rbind, lapply(seeds, function(s)
              traj[traj$budget == b & traj$seed == s, ])), mean)
rob <- data.frame(budget = bud, t(sapply(bud, function(b) rob_fit(smean(b)))),
                  row.names = NULL)
rob$ratio <- rob$G15_pred / rob$G15_obs
rb  <- function(col, b) rob[[col]][rob$budget == b]

# identity: the geometric sum equals the closed form of @eq-limits-cumul
Ne0 <- rob$Ne[1]; dF0 <- 1 / (2 * Ne0)
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(sum((1 - dF0)^(0:14)),
                           2 * Ne0 * (1 - (1 - 1 / (2 * Ne0))^15))))
Show code 
knitr::kable(fmt(rob, 3), row.names = FALSE)

Result (Table 24.1). For the tightest budget the predicted 15-cycle gain is 10.05 against an observed 9.98, a ratio of 1.008. For the unconstrained programme it is 15.73 against 7.81, a ratio of 2.01: the prediction is about twice the observation. For \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.02\) it is 14.44 against 10.43 (ratio 1.38). The \(R^2\) values are 0.998, 0.853 and 0.993 for the budgets \(0.005\), unconstrained and \(0.02\) in that order.

24.2.2 The agreement at \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.005\) is a mean over five draws

A ratio of the five-draw means hides the spread between founder draws. The same fit applied to each draw’s own trajectory, and a bootstrap over the five draws (2000 resamples of the draws with replacement, the series averaged and refitted in each, set.seed(1) before each budget):

Result (Table 24.2). For \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.005\) the ratio of the means is 1.008, the per-draw ratios run from 0.58 to 1.52 and the bootstrap 90 % interval of the ratio of the means is 0.8 to 1.23. For the unconstrained programme the per-draw ratios run from 1.48 to 2.72, for \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.02\) from 1.05 to 1.66.

Interpretation, and what limits it. The agreement at the tightest budget is an agreement of a five-draw mean, within a bootstrap interval that five draws do not narrow; a single founder draw is predicted with errors of up to 52 % at that budget. \(R_1\) is one difference between two cycles, and per draw it ranges from 0.43 to 1.16 at \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.005\); a forecast proportional to it inherits that spread. The comparison is in sample (\(N_e\) and \(G_{15}\) come from the same 15 cycles), the unit of replication is the founder draw (\(n = 5\)), and the three budgets share the same five draws, so the three rows of Table 24.1 are not independent.

24.2.3 The \(R^2\) of the \(\log(1-F)\) fit does not tell which forecasts fail

Robertson’s formula needs a constant \(\Delta F\), and the regression of \(\log(1 - F_{drift})\) on cycle is where that is testable; the unconstrained programme has the lowest \(R^2\) and the worst ratio (a possible reason, not tested here, is that its drift decelerates because there is progressively less left to lose). But \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.02\) has an \(R^2\) of 0.993 and is still over-predicted by 38 %. Two further checks, both on the chapter’s own runs:

Show code 
# N_e fitted on cycles 1-5 only (R_1 is still that of cycle 1)
short <- data.frame(budget = bud,
                    t(sapply(bud, function(b) rob_fit(smean(b), t_end = 5))),
                    row.names = NULL)
short$ratio <- short$G15_pred / short$G15_obs
# Per-cycle response R_t observed, against what geometric decay at dF would give
Rt <- sapply(bud, function(b) diff(smean(b)$index))        # 15 x 3: R_1 ... R_15
rt <- data.frame(budget = bud, R_1 = Rt[1, ], R_15 = Rt[15, ],
                 R_15_geometric = Rt[1, ] * (1 - rob$dF)^14, row.names = NULL)
sh  <- function(col, b) short[[col]][short$budget == b]
rtv <- function(col, b) rt[[col]][rt$budget == b]
Show code 
knitr::kable(fmt(rt, 2), row.names = FALSE)

Result. Fitting \(N_e\) on cycles 1 to 5 only and keeping the observed \(R_1\), the ratio is 1.34 for \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.02\) (fit \(R^2\) 0.9996), 0.99 for \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.005\) and 1.56 for the unconstrained programme: the miss at \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.02\) survives a fit with an \(R^2\) close to one. Over the 15 (budget, draw) fits, 7 have \(R^2 \ge 0.99\) and 4 of those are off by more than 20 % (with the threshold of 0.995 that Section 10.7.4 calibrated at its own scale, 4 fits pass, of which 1 is off by more than 20 %). Table 24.3 shows where the miss sits: at \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.02\) the per-cycle gain falls from 1.22 to 0.34, where decay at \(\Delta F\) would leave 0.74; in the unconstrained programme it falls to 0.02, against 0.85.

Interpretation, and what limits it. The \(\log(1 - F)\) regression tests the constancy of \(\Delta F\); Equation 24.2 also requires the response to decay at that same rate, which the regression cannot see. In these runs the observed \(R_{15}\) is below the value of decay at \(\Delta F\) in all three arms (Table 24.3). One hypothesis, not tested here, is that the genic variance is being used up faster than allele frequencies drift (the unconstrained arm’s genic variance falls from 1.03 at cycle 0 to 0.02 at cycle 12, below). Whatever the mechanism, \(R^2\) is not a screen that separates good from bad forecasts here: Section 10.7.4 reads \(R^2 < 0.995\) as a non-constant rate at its own scale (for the same threshold applied to the arms of Chapter 23, see that chapter), and this chapter finds a high \(R^2\) with a poor forecast. The \(R^2\) is worth reporting as a diagnostic of the constancy of \(\Delta F\), and it validates nothing more.

24.2.4 Along the trajectory, not only at cycle 15

Table 24.1 compares one number per budget, \(G_{15}\). The same fit implies a predicted cumulative gain at every cycle, which can be set against the five-draw mean:

Result (Table 24.4). At \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.005\) the predicted cumulative gain is 0.81 to 0.88 of the observed over cycles 2 to 5, 0.92 to 0.99 over cycles 6 to 14, and 1.01 at cycle 15; it is not above 1 at any cycle before 15. The observed per-cycle gain (Table 24.3) starts at 0.80 in cycle 1, below 1.01 in cycle 2 and 1.08 in cycle 3, whereas the form of Equation 24.2 has its largest response at cycle 1; the observed \(R_{15}\) is 0.41 against 0.55 under decay at \(\Delta F\). At \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.02\) and for the unconstrained programme the ratio is above 1 from cycle 2 on (asserted in the chunk), and in all three arms the observed \(R_{15}\) is below its geometric value (also asserted).

Interpretation, and what limits it. At \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.005\) the 15-cycle agreement is not an agreement of the trajectory: the forecast is low while the first cycles accumulate and reaches the observation at the end, and the two features of the series just listed, a first response below the following ones and a response that decays faster than \(\Delta F\), are consistent with one error offsetting the other at cycle 15. That offsetting is an inference from three numbers, not a decomposition. It rests on the five-draw mean and is in sample; the per-draw trajectories were not examined cycle by cycle.

24.2.5 The asymptote is not tested

Robertson’s limit is the asymptote of Equation 24.2, and the horizon of 15 cycles does not reach it for two of the three arms.

Result. The implied limit \(2 N_e R_1\) is 44.3 for the unconstrained programme, whose observed 15-cycle gain is 7.81 and whose last-cycle gain is 0.02: the series has plateaued at 18 % of the implied limit, so the limit is over-predicted by a factor of 5.7. For \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.02\) and \(0.005\) the observed gains are 30 % and 33 % of the implied limit, and the bound \(1.4 N_e\) on the half-life is 19.9 and 26.8 cycles, beyond the 15 observed. Interpretation. Where the limit could be reached it was not; where it could not, it was not tested. Table 24.1 tests a short-horizon forecast built on the geometric form, and says nothing about the total advance or the half-life.

24.2.6 Is \(N_e i\) low?

Robertson’s \(2 N_e\) is for low \(N_e i\) and approaches \(4 N_e\) otherwise. A rough per-locus indicator for this simulation: with the selection intensity \(i\) of truncating 60 of 625 and the substitution effect \(\beta_k/2\) of a line allele on the hybrid index (hybrids are coded 0/0.5/1), \(N_e\, i\, (\beta_k/2)/\sigma_P\), with \(\sigma_P\) the standard deviation of the cycle-0 hybrid index (seed 1) and \(N_e\) the mean of the fitted values of Table 24.1:

Show code 
sim1  <- simulate_pools(n_A = 25, n_B = 25, m = 2000, seed = 1)
i_int <- dnorm(qnorm(1 - 60 / 625)) / (60 / 625)       # normal-theory intensity
sg    <- sd(as.vector(sim1[["X"]] %*% sim1[["beta"]]))
a_k   <- abs(sim1[["beta"]][sim1[["beta"]] != 0]) / 2
Nis   <- mean(rob$Ne) * i_int * a_k / sg

Result. \(i =\) 1.77; over the 300 QTL the indicator has median 2.32 and ranges from 0.003 to 10.1. Interpretation, and what limits it. The indicator is not small for most loci, so they are not clearly in the low-\(N_e i\) regime for which \(2 N_e R_1\) is derived; the passages of Robertson (1960) used here give no numerical threshold between “low” and “larger”, and the mapping of the indicator to his \(s\) is approximate. The condition is therefore unchecked, and the agreement of the 15-cycle end point at \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.005\) is a property of this simulation, not evidence that the theorem applies to it.

ImportantA projection from a fitted \(N_e\) is not validated by its \(R^2\)

\(N_e\) here is a rate fitted on a managed trajectory (see above), and it is fed into a cumulative-gain formula. Report with any such projection the per-draw spread, the per-cycle gain next to the rate of inbreeding, and the \(R^2\) of the \(\log(1 - F)\) fit as a diagnostic of the constancy of \(\Delta F\) only: an \(R^2\) of 0.993 accompanied a forecast 38 % too high.

What the forecast does and does not show for the constraint, then. It does not show that a constrained programme is forecastable: for \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.005\) the five-draw mean 15-cycle gain is reached within the spread of five draws, in sample and not along the trajectory (Table 24.4); for \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.02\) it is not reached, and the unconstrained programme, whose \(\Delta F\) is not constant, is not forecast by this form. Chapter 23 reports where the trajectories cross; this chapter does not use that crossing to choose a budget, and adds no argument for the constraint from forecastability.

24.3 What the metrics cannot see

Now the second question. The one-locus diversity metrics of this book – \(\theta\), \(GD\), \(N_s\), allelic richness, \(F_{hom}\) and \(F_{drift}\) – are functions of allele frequencies one locus at a time; eff_dim() is the exception among the catalogue’s metrics (a multilocus quantity that weights loci by nothing, not by effect; Chapter 4). Additive variance is not a one-locus quantity: the realised variance differs from the genic variance by the covariance term of Equation 4.3. Directional selection drives that term negative (Bulmer (1971): negative gametic-phase disequilibrium among trait loci, which lowers the additive variance below its genic value), without moving a single allele frequency. The primary text of Bulmer was not read for this book and what is attributed to it is reported, not checked (Chapter 4); his result is for an infinitesimal number of loci, and the simulations below have finitely many.

Chapter 23 lists as its assumption C1 that loci are unlinked. Whether that excludes a selection-built covariance term is worth testing rather than assuming. Recombination between unlinked loci reduces disequilibrium by half per round of meiosis (a standard result, not checked here against Bulmer’s text), and selection recreates it each cycle; what the balance is here is a measurement.

24.3.1 The experiment

The gap \(\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta) - V_{\text{genic}}\) is not on its own a measure of the Bulmer effect. A finite founder base leaves a non-zero gap in the very first cycle. Over the complete factorial the covariance among loci is that of the founder lines of each pool, \([\operatorname{Cov}_A + \operatorname{Cov}_B]/4\) up to the \(n - 1\) divisor, with expectation zero and no between-pool term (Chapter 4); two divergent pools do not add to it. So the comparison is against a control: an arm with the same founders and the same seed in which the hybrids are chosen at random instead of on the index. After cycle 0 the arms differ in the selection and in the random draws of recycling (the random selector consumes the random stream), so the control is valid in expectation, not draw for draw. The gap is reported in two units: on the index scale, and relative to the genic variance, \((\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta) - V_{\text{genic}}) / V_{\text{genic}}\).

Show code 
vr$rel <- vr$gap / vr$var_genic              # gap in units of genic variance

w$rel_unc <- w$rel.unconstrained - w$rel.random
w$rel_con <- w$rel.constrained  - w$rel.random

                 gap_random = cm("gap")[, "random"],
                 gap_unconstrained = cm("gap")[, "unconstrained"],
                 gap_constrained = cm("gap")[, "constrained"],
                 Vgenic_random = cm("var_genic")[, "random"],

Interpretation, and what limits it. Selection builds negative disequilibrium among unlinked loci in this simulation: the effect is negative in 6 of 8 seeds on the index scale and in 8 of 8 relative to genic variance. The seed-level tests carry the existence of the effect; the percentages of Table 24.7 carry its size, at eight seeds. What eight seeds do not resolve: the 6 % of the random arm at cycle 5 is within about one standard error of zero, and over cycles 1 to 8 the random arm shows no loss (1.00, s.e. 0.03), so the size of the loss in the control is not distinguished from none; the paired contrast of the constrained arm with the random arm at cycle 5 is not distinguished from zero either, so the difference between the percentages of those two arms at that cycle is not established (the constrained-minus-random effect over cycles 1 to 8 of Table 24.6, which averages eight cycles, is). The shrinking of the absolute gap in later cycles is consistent with the genic variance being used up, since the covariance term scales with the variance that remains (a reading, not tested separately).

On persistence: no trend of the relative effect is detected over cycles 1 to 8, but the slope has a 95 % interval that includes values at which the effect would reach zero near cycle 8.7 (from its mean at the middle of the window, cycle 4.5), so “no trend detected” is not evidence of no decay. What supports persistence is the late-window effect above (cycles 6 to 8), which is negative in the seeds counted above, and is itself post hoc. Late cycles of the relative measure beyond cycle 8 are ill-conditioned (the unconstrained genic variance is near zero by cycle 12) and are not interpreted. The early (1-4) against late (9-12) split on the index scale is exploratory (entered after the eight seeds had been seen); the twenty draws below were drawn after it was defined. These are eight seeds at one scale (625 candidates, 300 QTL, \(F_{ST} = 0.15\)); how the size of the effect moves with \(h^2\), \(N\), \(n_{sel}\) or the number of loci was not measured.

WarningUnlinked loci do not remove the covariance term

The measurement above shows that, with free recombination between loci, selection still builds a negative covariance term that the genic variance does not contain (Table 24.6). Assumption C1 of Chapter 23, that loci are unlinked, therefore cannot be read as the absence of that term. Its replacement is a statement about the size of the term, measured above at eight seeds. Whether the term decays to a negligible equilibrium at longer horizons is not shown either way: no decay is detected over cycles 1 to 8, and the data cannot exclude one (the slope interval above).

alphasimr_founder_pools() draws the founder haplotypes with runMacs2(), which hands the coalescent to MaCS. Whether a second call of runMacs2() under the same seed returns the same founders was not measured in this chapter; Chapter 9 compares one pair of runMacs() calls per render and does not call runMacs2(). 24.4 The two costs of selection

Result. In the unlinked arm the realised variance is, as a mean over eight seeds and cycles 1 to 8, 32 % below the genic variance for the unconstrained programme (s.e. 3 points) and 21 % for \(\alpha_{\max} = 0.02\) (5), against 0 % for random selection (3); no trend of the relative effect is detected over those cycles, with the interval of the slope given above. In the same simulation allele-frequency change is permanent: these runs have no mutation, migration or germplasm injection (inject is not used), and a fixed allele stays fixed.

Interpretation, and what limits it. Selection pays for gain in two currencies. It builds negative covariance between loci, a loss of usable variance that is charged every cycle while selection continues and that recombination reduces (here, at a measured balance that is not small); and it changes allele frequencies toward fixation, which only mutation or migration repays. The constraint of this book manages the second and does not see the first. Whether that is the right choice needs the two bills in a common unit (for example, response per cycle), and the chapter does not compute that: its two measurements are the unit-free ratio of the first and the short-horizon forecast of the second (Table 24.1). The ordering of the two bills is therefore not established here, and neither is whether the variance deficit lowers the response: no response was computed from it. The size of the covariance term may change with the genome (linkage, number of chromosomes, cycle time); that is a hypothesis with no source and no measurement in this chapter, and the four-replicate linked arm does not resolve it.

24.5 Where this connects

Chapter 4 supplies the variance decomposition (Equation 4.3) and the breeder’s equation this chapter runs forward. Chapter 23 supplies the trajectories and the assumption C1 tested above. Chapter 9 supplies the linked arm and the rules for founding pools that it follows. Three things are assumed that other chapters treat: the crossing plan behind the 625 hybrids (Chapter 18, Chapter 19, Chapter 20), the estimation of marker effects (Chapter 14), and the selector (Chapter 17). Two points of this chapter belong with the across-cycles items of Chapter 25: a projection from a fitted rate is not validated by the \(R^2\) of the \(\log(1 - F)\) regression, and the covariance term is outside every one-locus metric.
