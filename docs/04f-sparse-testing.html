19  Sparse testing: which hybrids go to which locations

A hybrid programme’s first field stage concentrates most of its selection pressure where it has the least information. It typically has thousands of testcrosses, seed for one or two plots of each, a handful of locations, and almost no replication. Chapter 18 compared which crosses to make from two pools – connected sparse factorials against tester designs, analysed by BLUP, with results that are conditional on the design and field size it states – but held the field fixed and folded genotype x location into plot error. This chapter takes the crosses as given and asks the next question: which locations should each one be planted in?

Sparse testing is the strategy that the six primary studies of Table 19.2 examine, on different axes and with different conclusions about overlap (Section 19.6). Not every hybrid is grown at every location. Different subsets go to different environments, and the missing hybrid x environment cells are predicted, not discarded, with a relationship matrix connecting what the field left unconnected. In Chapter 18 the sparsity was in the female x male grid. Here it is in the hybrid x environment grid. Real programmes face both at once, the joint problem Section 18.9 lists as its second gap.

The chapter covers the quantitative basis, the history and a taxonomy of the designs, the validation schemes used to judge them, and the empirical record across crops, including its main controversy. It then measures the question with a simulation that the primary literature had not run. The results it defends:

  1. In the one cell examined, a sparse allocation reached within 5 % of the accuracy of the complete design at a fraction of its plots; the margin is thin. At 400 hybrids, ten locations and \(h^2 = 0.4\) the cheapest allocation within 5 % used 20 % of the plots. It passes the 95 % line by 0.0033 accuracy units, which is 0.5 standard errors of the difference, and it is the cheapest passing cell among 12 candidates, chosen on the same noisy means that are then reported. At \(h^2 = 0.2\) the cheapest passing cell uses 50 % of the plots. “Wider at higher heritability” rests on these two cells.
  2. At a fixed number of plots, spreading them over more locations raised accuracy. Spreading two plots per hybrid over ten locations instead of two raised accuracy by 0.06 (s.e. 0.009 of the unpaired difference, \(z = 6.4\)) at \(h^2 = 0.4\) and 0.09 (s.e. 0.026, \(z = 3.3\)) at \(h^2 = 0.2\), with no extra plots (cached REML study, one configuration: see Scope below). The step from six to ten locations is not resolved (\(z = 0.8\) and 0.1).
  3. Parentage allocation exceeded random allocation by a small margin that does not survive a multiplicity correction, and not on the operational metric. Parentage minus random was +0.0051 on accuracy over all hybrids, +0.0086 on never-planted hybrids and +0.0077 on missing cells of planted hybrids, but +0.0046 \(\pm\) 0.0069 on the coincidence with the true top 5 %. The smallest of the 22 p-values of the family is 0.0031, above the Bonferroni threshold 0.0023. A subset (\(k \le 2\)) with a larger difference is exploratory.
  4. Whether parent coverage, rather than shared hybrids, connects a sparse trial is a hypothesis that these data do not decide. In the one cell examined, the parentage design (no hybrid shared between some location pairs, both pools in every location) exceeded random allocation by +0.0057 (\(z = 0.74\), unresolved), while the optimised blocks, with the highest hybrid overlap and nearly the same parent coverage, did not. No arm varies coverage with overlap fixed.
  5. In the cached REML grid the dispersion slope of the BLUP stayed near 1 where REML converged; the variance components were estimated less well, the SCA variance with an upward median bias at small \(k\) whose cause these data do not separate. Where every fit converged (\(k \ge 2\)) there was no systematic dispersion bias. At one location per hybrid REML converged in 42.6 % of fits, and the median relative bias over scenarios was +18.8 % for plot \(h^2\), -2.0 % for the female GCA variance and +220 % for the SCA variance (interquartile range of the scenarios +56 to +386 %; no complete design exists at \(k = 1\) to compare with). In the complete design the SCA bias is positive at both heritabilities (+40 % and +21 %), resolved at the uncorrected 5 % level at one of them and at neither after Bonferroni correction over the 12 tests of the validation table.
  6. In the cached two-stage scenario, covering more candidates in stage 1 gave a larger realised gain at the same total plots, but with four replicates nothing survives a multiplicity correction. The paired difference at matched budgets is 0.33 to 0.36 genetic SD (\(t\) = 3.0 to 4.1, 3 df) from 100 to 200, and 0.36 to 0.48 (\(t\) = 1.7 to 2.0, not resolved) from 200 to 400.

Scope. Every simulated result below refers to one configuration: a 20 x 20 female x male factorial of which 80 % of the hybrids are planted at random (each line is a parent of about 16 planted crosses), 400 hybrids, at most ten locations, one plot per cell, and the calibration of sparse_vc(). It is a dense factorial, in which parents are shared by construction. The sparse factorials of Chapter 18 (a few crosses per line out of 200 candidates per pool) and the 2,000-lines-per-pool pipeline of Figure 19.1 are different regimes, and none of the results was measured there. The cached study is one study, and the contrasts and bias figures quoted from it cannot be recomputed in this repository.

19.1 The allocation problem of stage 1

19.1.1 The economics of the first stage

Stage 1 is where a plot costs least and a decision is worth most. Early-stage testing typically evaluates a large number of candidates at one or a few experimental sites (Werner, Covarrubias-Pazaran, et al. 2025). Two shifts are taken here as premises of the chapter, not as results measured in it or taken from a cited source:

  • Genotyping is no longer the bottleneck. Phenotyping is. The cost of a marker datum is taken to be small against the cost of a yield plot.
  • Doubled haploids reversed the constraint. A programme can now make more candidates than it can evaluate. Allocating plots, not creating variation, is then the limiting factor.

What is left is the classical trade-off. More genotypes at fewer locations buys selection intensity and costs accuracy. Fewer genotypes at more locations does the opposite. At stage 1 the scarce resource is the plot, so the question is not how much to test but how to spread what can already be tested (Li et al. 2026).

19.1.2 The breeder’s equation as the decision criterion

The gain per unit time,

\[ \Delta G_t = \frac{i \, \rho \, \sigma_A}{t}, \]

has four terms. \(\rho\) is the accuracy of the selection criterion – the \(r\) of Chapter 4; this Part keeps \(r\) for plots, as Chapter 18 does. \(\sigma_A\) is the additive standard deviation of Chapter 4 (written \(\sigma_g\) in Chapter 18). The accuracy reported by the simulation below is a correlation with the value of a hybrid, defined in Section 19.11.3; it is not the GCA accuracy of Chapter 18, which is a correlation over lines. A trial design moves at least three of the terms together:

  • intensity \(i\), through the number of candidates;
  • accuracy \(\rho\), through plots, locations, and how well the locations sample the target population of environments (TPE);
  • cycle length \(t\), when parents can be recycled from stage 1.

What a gain measured on a research station is worth depends on the genetic correlation between the selection environment and the TPE (Atlin et al. 2000, 2001). No design is better in the abstract. A design is better according to whichever term limits that programme (Werner, Zaman-Allah, et al. 2025; Riedelsheimer and Melchinger 2013).

19.2 Foundations

19.2.1 The hybrid model: GCA, SCA, and what sparsity threatens

The value of the single cross between female \(i\) and male \(j\) – a pool-A and a pool-B line in the notation of Chapter 18 – splits as

\[ g_{ij} = \mathrm{GCA}^f_i + \mathrm{GCA}^m_j + \mathrm{SCA}_{ij} \]

This split is classically attributed to Sprague and Tatum (1942) (not checked at page level here). Section 5.11 derives it from allele frequencies. Its consequence for this chapter is that a line’s GCA includes dominance effects weighted by the opposite pool’s frequencies. A GCA is therefore specific to the tester pool, not a property of the line alone (González-Diéguez et al. 2021; Legarra et al. 2023). The ratio \(\sigma^2_s/\sigma^2_g\) of Chapter 18 (symbols defined below) is a design parameter of the simulation. In the dominance-only model of Section 5.11 the corresponding quantity grows with the mean degree of dominance and with how close the pools are; Chapter 18 notes that this is a different statistic from its own ratio, under narrower assumptions, so the statement is not transferred to the ratio used here. In simulated maize the ratio of González-Diéguez et al. (2026), in that paper’s own definition, was 0.05, 0.23 and 0.50 at cycle 1 for mean dominance degrees of 0.1, 0.5 and 0.9.

GCA is expected to survive sparsity better than SCA, because it is mostly additive and is averaged over many crosses. This is an expectation, not a result of this chapter. The one supporting number is from Li et al. (2026), who recovered GCA from a prediction-completed 50 % trial with correlations of 0.94 to 0.97 against the complete data, while phenotypic prediction accuracy over the same scenarios was 0.43 to 0.59. The chapter has no SCA comparator for that correlation, and its own simulation measures the bias of the SCA variance, not the robustness of SCA prediction (see its limitations). If stage 1 is used to recycle parents, GCA is what it has to estimate; whether GCA is the part of the model most robust to missing cells, and therefore the reason sparse testing works in hybrids, is not tested here.

TipQuick glossary
  • GCA: a line’s average performance in crosses with the opposite pool.
  • SCA: the deviation of a particular cross from the sum of its parents’ GCAs.
  • Mid-parent heterosis: the hybrid’s advantage over the mean of its parents (Chapter 5).
  • Heterotic pool: a group of lines bred to cross well with another group.
  • Tester: a line of the opposite pool used to evaluate candidates.
  • TPE: the target population of environments the product is sold into.

19.2.2 Hybrid relationship matrices

With female and male relationship matrices \(\mathbf{A}_f\) and \(\mathbf{A}_m\), the three hybrid terms are distributed as

\[ \mathrm{GCA}^f \sim N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf A_f\sigma^2_{f}), \quad \mathrm{GCA}^m \sim N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf A_m\sigma^2_{m}), \quad \mathrm{SCA} \sim N(\mathbf 0, (\mathbf A_f\otimes\mathbf A_m)\sigma^2_{s}) \]

(Bernardo 1994, 1996; Technow et al. 2012). Here \(\sigma^2_f\) and \(\sigma^2_m\) are the GCA variances of the female (pool-A) and male (pool-B) lines. They are the \(\sigma^2_{gA}\) and \(\sigma^2_{gB}\) of Chapter 18, whose \(\sigma^2_g\) is their mean, \(\sigma^2_g = (\sigma^2_f + \sigma^2_m)/2\); \(\sigma^2_s\) is the SCA variance of Chapter 18, also written \(\sigma^2_{SCA}\) in this chapter. The effects \(\mathrm{GCA}^f_i\), \(\mathrm{GCA}^m_j\) and \(\mathrm{SCA}_{ij}\) are the \(g^A_i\), \(g^B_j\) and \(s_{ij}\) of Chapter 18. Two sums are used below and each is defined here once. The genetic variance of a hybrid’s value in the target population of environments is \(\sigma^2_G = \sigma^2_f + \sigma^2_m + \sigma^2_s\) (2.25 in sparse_vc(), where it is Vg); it is not the \(\sigma^2_g\) of Chapter 18. Each of the three terms interacts with location, with variances \(\sigma^2_{f\ell}\), \(\sigma^2_{m\ell}\) and \(\sigma^2_{s\ell}\) (s2fl, s2ml, s2sl; the last is also written \(\sigma^2_{SCA\times L}\) below), and \(\sigma^2_{GE} = \sigma^2_{f\ell} + \sigma^2_{m\ell} + \sigma^2_{s\ell}\) (1.125 in sparse_vc(), where it is VgxE). \(\sigma^2_e\) is the plot error.

The practical point is that the hybrid itself never needs genotyping: its covariance is a Kronecker product of the parental matrices. Replacing pedigree \(\mathbf A\) by marker \(\mathbf G\) (VanRaden 2008) gives the genomic version. Chapter 14 fits a single additive GBLUP over all hybrids, with no pool split, no SCA kernel and no Kronecker product, so the kernels of this section are not fitted there. The live code of this chapter uses the pedigree matrices of relmat_halfsib(), and the cached genomic layer comes from the external study. There are two ways to write the additive and dominance effects: the “biological” form and the “statistical” form, which is orthogonal at the population’s frequencies. Pool-specific marker effects are a third option (Vitezica et al. 2013; González-Diéguez et al. 2021). All of them lead to the same covariance structure.

The relationship matrix is what makes a sparse design analysable. It is the only link between cells that the field did not link. A hybrid with no plot gets a BLUP only because its parents are related to parents that do have plots. Without it, a sparse design yields comparisons that are not fair.

19.2.3 Multi-environment trials and mixed models

How many parameters the between-environment covariance spends ranges widely:

  • identity, diagonal, or compound symmetry;
  • heterogeneous AR(1);
  • unstructured;
  • factor-analytic (Smith et al. 2001; Smith et al. 2005; Kelly et al. 2007; Smith and Cullis 2018).

Factor-analytic models carry most of the information of the unstructured matrix at a fraction of its parameters, and extend directly to genomic relationships (Oakey et al. 2016; Tolhurst et al. 2019; Burgueño et al. 2012). Reaction-norm and marker x environment models are cheaper alternatives (Jarquin et al. 2013; Lopez-Cruz et al. 2015). Only crossover G x E changes selection decisions. A scale effect does not.

In a sparse design the between-environment covariance stops being a statistical detail and becomes part of the design. It decides how much one environment can tell us about another. Two-stage analyses that carry the first-stage error forward are now as efficient as one-stage ones (Piepho et al. 2012; Endelman 2023). Unreplicated trials still need spatial correction and checks (Robbins et al. 2012; Cullis et al. 2006).

19.3 What sparse testing is, and where it came from

19.3.1 Definition and genealogy

Definition. A trial is sparse when not every genotype is evaluated in every environment and the missing cells are predicted rather than discarded. The literature’s environment is a location in a year; the simulations of this Part have one season, so their environments are locations, and the two words are used in those two senses. The idea developed in four steps:

  1. Incomplete blocks and lattices. These already accepted partial evaluation within a location.
  2. Partially replicated designs (p-rep) and augmented p-rep. These replaced replication of each entry with replicated checks and partial replication (Cullis et al. 2006; Williams et al. 2011).
  3. The genomic relationship replacing replication (Endelman et al. 2014). With markers, the replicated unit is no longer the genotype but the haplotype, shared among relatives.
  4. Genomic sparse testing across environments, formalised (Jarquin et al. 2020). The design was later framed explicitly as a balanced incomplete block design in which the block is the environment (Montesinos-López et al. 2023).

Farm-as-incomplete-block is the limiting case. Each farmer’s field is an incomplete block holding a handful of entries, and the TPE is sampled through hundreds of them (Werner, Zaman-Allah, et al. 2025; Werner, Covarrubias-Pazaran, et al. 2025; Atlin et al. 2001).

Sparse testing is not a new method. It is incomplete-block design applied at the scale of many environments, made workable by genomic relationships acting as the connecting mechanism. The classical theory of incomplete blocks therefore carries over.

19.3.2 A taxonomy of sparse designs

The designs can be classified along three independent axes.

Axis of sparsity.

  • Environments: the classical case.
  • Testers: sparse testcrossing (Section 19.8).
  • Female x male combinations: the incomplete factorials of Section 18.4.
  • Any combination of these.

Overlap structure.

  • full overlap, with a common core in every environment;
  • no overlap;
  • partial overlap, with a fixed core plus entries unique to each environment;
  • unrestricted random allocation;
  • restricted random allocation, for example keeping full-sibs out of the same location (Werner, Covarrubias-Pazaran, et al. 2025);
  • allocation optimised against a formal criterion (Isidro y Sanchez and Akdemir 2021; Fernandez-Gonzalez et al. 2023).

Replicated unit. Genotype, family, or haplotype.

WarningA naming collision to watch for

Li et al. (2026) use “CV1” to “CV5” as names of allocation strategies, not of the classical validation schemes of Section 19.4. Their “CV1” is the full-overlap design: 44 of the 264 genotypes are evaluated in all six environments. Their introduction states that CV1 “is used to predict entirely new genotypes”, and in their Methods only those 44 genotypes carry records, so for CV1 the prediction task is the “new genotype” one of Burgueño et al. (2012). Their CV2 and CV3 retain at least one record per genotype (Methods, Section 2.4, and the legend of their Fig. 1), so the cells predicted are unobserved cells of observed genotypes. For CV4 and CV5 the text read does not state that every genotype is observed, and the correspondence is not asserted here. What the label collision does is more modest: the same labels name a design in one literature and a validation task in the other, and reading the two together without noticing this produces wrong comparisons.

A second collision is inside this book, and it has three parts. Section 14.4 uses CV2 / CV1 / CV0 for hybrids with both / one / neither parent seen in training – the T2 / T1 / T0 of Section 18.6. Here, as in Burgueño et al. (2012), the labels classify genotype x environment cells. A hybrid that is CV1 here (never planted) is CV2 in Section 14.4 when both its parents are planted in other crosses, CV1 there when one parent is not, and CV0 there when neither is. Section 14.4’s CV1 (one parent unseen) therefore has no counterpart label in this chapter, and this chapter’s CV0 (a new environment) shares only the label with that chapter’s CV0. In this chapter’s simulation every parent is planted (a 20 x 20 factorial with 20 % of the hybrids dropped at random), so every CV1 hybrid here is a T2 / CV2 hybrid in those terms. That is a property of the simulated design, not of the definition, which also admits T1 and T0 hybrids.

19.4 Validation schemes: what each one answers

The classical schemes are defined by two questions. Has the genotype been observed anywhere? Has the environment been observed for anyone? (Burgueño et al. 2012; Jarquin et al. 2020)

  • CV2: the genotype is observed in some environments and missing in others. The task is to complete the table.
  • CV1: the genotype is new, never observed anywhere. The task is to predict an unseen candidate.
  • CV0: the environment is new (next year, say) and the genotypes are known. The task is to extrapolate to an untested environment.
  • CV00: both the genotype and the environment are new. This is the hardest case, and the most realistic for long-run decisions.

The schemes pose different prediction tasks, so accuracies obtained under different schemes are not comparable like for like. In the cached cells of this chapter acc_cv1 exceeds acc_cv2 in 114 of the 165 scenario cells where both exist (median difference +0.026): neither is above the other in general, and the two also differ in target (see the estimands in Section 19.11.3). The correlation is not the only metric:

  • top-\(x\) % coincidence: top 20 % by a Smith-Hazel index in Atanda et al. (2022), top 50 % in Li et al. (2026);
  • mean squared error (Lubanga et al. 2025);
  • Kendall’s \(\tau\) (Hild Aono and Chawade 2026);
  • PEV and reliability, and the dispersion slope \(b_1\) of truth on prediction.

At stage 1 the operationally relevant number is the rate of wrongly discarded superior candidates. The correlation is only a proxy for it.

Returns diminish beyond about 50 % coverage: going from 50 % to 67 % cost 33 % more phenotyping and returned 3.5 to 5.4 % accuracy. Top-50 % coincidence was above 61.9 % in all 25 strategies.
  • Wheat (Atanda et al. 2022). Going from 0 to 50 % common lines raised predictive ability by 42 % to 989 %, depending on the environment. Plot \(h^2\) was 0.18 in the worst environment. Genetic correlations between environments in the same year ranged from \(-0.04\) to \(0.67\).
  • Cassava (Lubanga et al. 2025). With 20 % of records per environment, predictive ability was moderate (maxima of 0.53 for dry matter and 0.47 for fresh root yield). Phenotyping cost could be cut by up to 80 %.
  • Potato (Hild Aono and Chawade 2026). For pairs of environments at 50 % completeness, \(r^2\) was 0.995 to 0.997 with an image-derived environment kernel, against 0.612 to 0.798 with the genomic kernel. Section Section 19.10 covers the caveat.

The common denominator is the genetic correlation between environments, and the size and diversity of the training set. Together they explain most of the spread. In Atanda et al. (2022), the environment with low heritability and weak correlations stayed poorly predicted under every design. In Li et al. (2026), plant height was the trait with the strongest between-environment correlations (0.36 to 0.67) and the best predicted. Grain yield had no strong correlations and was predicted worst. The record on modelling G x E explicitly is mixed. Lubanga et al. (2025) report higher predictive ability and lower MSE for the G x E model across their sparse designs, with 20 % of records per environment; in Li et al. (2026) the reaction-norm model, which has a main genomic effect and an environment-specific deviation, had the highest accuracy of the four models compared (Table 2, S1 and CV1, and, by their text, averaged by region group over the 25 strategies); in Hild Aono and Chawade (2026) the G x E term gave results comparable to the model without it at 66 % of the data per environment, and performance fell when it was included at higher proportions of missing data, which the authors attribute to too few individuals to estimate it. The studies differ in crop, data, kernels and sparsity, and none compares the same models across the same range, so the chapter draws no rule from them. Cutting phenotyping by 50 to 80 % without losing accuracy is feasible. The exact cut depends on the programme’s own between-environment correlations, and does not transfer between programmes.

19.6 The minimum-overlap controversy

The literature disagrees openly on how much overlap is needed:

  • Atanda et al. (2022) conclude that “a substantial overlap between lines across environments should be used to achieve optimal prediction accuracy”.
  • Crespo-Herrera et al. (2021) suggest 30 to 50 common lines.
  • Persa et al. (2023) report a gain with as few as 10.
  • Lubanga et al. (2025) find that more overlap made prediction worse: predictive ability fell and MSE rose monotonically with the number of overlapping genotypes, and the fully non-overlapping design was the best.
  • Li et al. (2026) sit in between. Full overlap was the worst design, and the four partial or non-overlapping designs tied.
  • Werner, Covarrubias-Pazaran, et al. (2025) and González-Diéguez et al. (2026) do without overlap altogether. All of their connectivity comes from relationships.

A proposed reconciliation, offered here as a hypothesis. The designs are not comparable because they confound overlap with the size and diversity of the training set:

  • In Lubanga et al. (2025) the training set per environment is fixed in size. More overlap therefore means fewer distinct genotypes observed.
  • In Atanda et al. (2022) more overlap means a larger calibration set per environment, growing from 46-47 lines to as many as 165.

A second factor is family structure. Families of 1 to 9 individuals (Atanda et al. 2022) give the relationship matrix little to work with, so physical overlap has to carry the connection. Families of 20 to 50 (Werner, Covarrubias-Pazaran, et al. 2025; González-Diéguez et al. 2026) let the relationship matrix carry all of it.

The first half of the hypothesis can be examined with the package, but only in part (see below). The experiment uses four locations and a training set fixed at 80 hybrids per location out of 400. A fraction of the 80 is a core common to all four locations, and the rest are unique to each location. Family size runs from unrelated lines to half-sib families of 10 (regenerate.R sparse, part c; 30 replicates per point; known components; plot \(h^2 = 0.4\)).

With the training size held fixed, accuracy falls as overlap rises: it is 0.023 to 0.059 lower under full overlap than under none, 1.6 to 4.7 standard errors of the difference depending on family size. The drop exceeds two standard errors for family size 1 and 10 (1 is unrelated lines) and not for family size 4 (1.6 standard errors), whose curve has the same direction. Every hybrid moved into the common core is one fewer distinct hybrid observed (top axis), so in this design overlap and the number of distinct hybrids move together by construction, and no arm varies one with the other fixed. The fall in accuracy is the pattern Lubanga et al. (2025) reported and the one the confound hypothesis predicts, but this experiment cannot attribute it to the fall in distinct hybrids rather than to overlap as such.

The second half of the hypothesis is not supported at this scale. The family-size curves cross, and they differ by at most 2.2 standard errors at any one overlap: of the 15 pairs compared (every pair of family sizes at every overlap, with no correction for multiple comparisons), 1 exceeded two standard errors and 0 exceeded three. A possible reason, not tested here, is structural. In this factorial every line is a parent of 20 hybrids, of which on average 16 (no overlap) to 4 (full overlap) are observed, so the factorial may carry much of the connectivity before any relationship among lines is added, and half-sib families would add relatively little on top of that. In a line-breeding programme like Atanda et al. (2022), each entry is its own genotype and no parent links them, so family structure should matter far more. This experiment cannot test that case. The experiment also holds the training size fixed, so it does not reproduce Atanda et al. (2022)’s design, in which more overlap also meant a larger calibration set and the two effects pull in opposite directions.

What to do with this. The right question is not “how many common genotypes?”. It is “how much effective connectivity does the design have, counting both physical overlap and relationships?”. The experiment above did not resolve an effect of relationships between lines at this scale, so it does not show that physical overlap and relationships substitute for each other. Atanda et al. (2022) found both the genetic correlation between environments and the genomic relationship of lines across them among the drivers of sparse-testing accuracy. In a factorial as dense as the simulated one, shared parents may already supply much of the relationship side (a hypothesis, not tested here).

19.7 Connectivity and checks

Connectivity is not the same as overlap. Two designs with the same number of common genotypes can be connected very differently, depending on how those genotypes are distributed. Hybrids add a dimension. There is female x male connectivity, the subject of Section 18.2.3. There is genotype x location connectivity. And there is female x male x location, for which no metric is agreed (Section 19.13); Section 20.4 gives a necessary screen and a counter-example to sufficiency, and leaves the metric open. The available tools are:

  • the rank of the incidence matrix;
  • the PEV of pairwise contrasts, and the reliability derived from it;
  • the connected components of the genotype-environment graph.

Checks do three jobs:

  • anchor connectivity;
  • estimate within-environment error in unreplicated trials, as in the augmented design of Li et al. (2026);
  • control spatial trend (Cullis et al. 2006; Williams et al. 2011).

Restricted randomisation is a cheap connectivity tool. Werner, Covarrubias-Pazaran, et al. (2025) kept full-sibs apart within a location, but did not measure the restriction against unrestricted randomisation. Section 19.11.3 does measure a structured layout against unrestricted randomisation.

Connectivity can be checked before planting. Compute it at the planning stage, rather than finding out from a model that does not converge.

TipBox 4 – Computing the connectivity of a design before planting it

  2. Check the connected components of the bipartite graph. Every location should share hybrids, or at least parents, with every other.

  5. Compare candidate designs on the same metric.

19.8 Sparse testcrossing and the number of testers

The central idea of González-Diéguez et al. (2026) is to treat the tester as an “environment”. Each line is crossed to one tester, but full-sibs go to different testers. What gets evaluated against several testers is therefore the haplotype, not the genotype. This removes a real logistical constraint. The more accurate alternative, an incomplete factorial among candidates (Section 18.4), needs hand pollination at scale and synchronised flowering between pools.

The advantage is largest in early cycles, while the pools are still close. That does not confine the method to emerging programmes. González-Diéguez et al. (2026) report a minimum Nei distance of 0.17 from the public maize programme of the University of Hohenheim, a figure they attribute to Legarra et al. (2023), and place it in their simulated cycles 3 to 4. The \(F_{ST}\) of 0.223 of Kadoumi et al. (2025), from a private programme, is placed by the same reading at about cycle 2. Both figures are second-hand here: the original of Legarra et al. (2023) was not read for this chapter.

A useful side effect is that sparse testcrossing slowed the erosion of within-pool variance. It stops selection from piling onto closely related lines that happen to nick with one tester. The authors recommend combining it with optimum contribution selection (Chapter 15; Santantonio and Robbins (2020)).

The advocates of incomplete factorials (Seye et al. 2020; Lorenzi et al. 2022; Lorenzi et al. 2024; Melchinger and Frisch 2023) reach a different conclusion. In González-Diéguez et al. (2026), an approximate incomplete factorial matched 3 to 5 testers, with much heavier logistics. Two costs were not modelled. Five testers need ten isolation blocks. And in many public programmes hand pollination is already routine, which lowers the extra cost of the factorial.

TipBox 5 – Choosing testers: theory against practice

The classical recommendation is a low-performing tester, or one carrying recessive alleles at the loci of interest. Such a tester discriminates among candidates best (a recommendation classically attributed to Hull (1945) and Rawlings and Thompson (1962), not checked at page level here). Commercial practice uses elite testers, chosen for seed production and per se performance, because the testcross may itself become a product. The consequence follows from Section 5.11: GCA is measured against the tester’s allele frequencies. An elite tester carries the favourable allele at many loci and shrinks the visible differences there, so a few elite testers sample the opposite pool’s frequencies poorly. Several testers spread across the pool, the sparse-testcross recipe, sample them better (C. Longin et al. 2006; Jong et al. 2023).

19.9 What sparsity does to variance components and to the BLUP

Two questions are often merged in the literature, and they should be kept separate:

  1. Do the predictions stay accurate?
  2. Do the variance-component estimates stay unbiased?

The second is barely covered. None of the six primary studies reports bias in the GCA, SCA or G x E variances (\(\sigma^2_f\), \(\sigma^2_m\), \(\sigma^2_s\), \(\sigma^2_{GE}\)) under sparsity. The indirect evidence is thin:

  • In Hild Aono and Chawade (2026) the G x E term degraded prediction in the scenarios with less than 66 % of the data per environment (it was comparable at 66 %), which the authors attribute, without testing it, to too few individuals to estimate it. They then used models without the term.
  • In Li et al. (2026) propagating prediction uncertainty barely changed the GCA estimates (correlation 0.9974 to 0.9998 with the point estimates). That is a sensitivity of GCA, not of the variance components.

Two diagnostics separate the questions.

  • The dispersion slope \(b_1\), from regressing truth on prediction. \(b_1 = 1\) means no scale bias. \(b_1 < 1\) means the predictions are too spread out, and \(b_1 > 1\) means they are over-shrunk.
  • PEV and reliability. These can be computed before any data exist (Box 4), so they serve as design tools.

Accuracy and estimation quality do not degrade at the same rate. A design can rank candidates well and still estimate the SCA and G x E variances badly (Cullis et al. 2006; Piepho and Mohring 2007; Smith and Cullis 2018; Tolhurst et al. 2019). The simulation of Section 19.11.3 measures this directly. That is the gap in the primary literature it was designed to fill.

19.10 Sparse testing with phenomics

Phenomic data pose a specific problem. Markers can be read from a seedling whatever happens in the field. Sensor data exist only for plots that were actually grown, so they cannot serve as a covariate for an individual that was never observed. There are three ways out:

  • multi-trait models with image indices as secondary traits;
  • kernel regression on the indices;
  • the proposal of Hild Aono and Chawade (2026): use the images to build an environment kernel.

The environment kernel is built in three steps. A PCA of the image traits is run within each environment. The first-component eigenvector of each environment is extracted. The kernel entries are the correlations between those eigenvectors.

At 30 % data availability the comparison varied with the combination of environments. Across all scenarios with at least 33 % complete data per environment, the environment kernel matched or outperformed the genomic kernel, reaching \(r^2 = 0.997\) on pairs of environments. At 10 and 20 % “no consistent trend emerged; however, the G kernel generally performed better in most scenarios” (Hild Aono and Chawade 2026), that is, the genomic kernel, not the image-derived one. The authors warn of possible information leakage, because the kernel came from the same trial that supplied training and validation. The result should be reported with that caveat.

The approach is attractive for programmes without routine genotyping. It is also promising for maize, where sensor phenotyping exists but has not been tested in this arrangement with hybrids. It connects to envirotyping. When locations are close together, coarse-grid weather covariates discriminate poorly, and images capture differences that the weather data miss (Costa-Neto et al. 2020; Crossa et al. 2021; Rincent et al. 2017). NIRS-based phenomic prediction in maize points the same way, in a non-sparse setting (DeSalvio et al. 2024).

Sensor data enter sparse testing as descriptors of environments, not of genotypes. This inversion solves the availability problem. In programmes that genotype little, it can partly replace the genomic matrix.

19.11 Simulation as a sizing tool

19.11.1 Why simulate before designing

None of the published numbers transfers directly to another programme. Each one depends on the between-environment correlations, the family structure, the \(\sigma^2_{SCA}/\sigma^2_{GCA}\) ratio and the heritability of the programme that produced it, and the authors say so themselves (Werner, Covarrubias-Pazaran, et al. 2025; González-Diéguez et al. 2026). A simulation can do three things an experiment cannot:

  • it knows the true values;
  • it can rerun the programme a hundred times;
  • it can isolate one factor at a time.

The tools exist. AlphaSimR covers the genetics , Chapter 9, FieldSimR covers plots and G x E (Werner et al. 2024), and sommer or BGLR fit the models (Covarrubias-Pazaran 2016). Staged-allocation theory provides the deterministic cross-check (Mi et al. 2014; C. F. H. Longin et al. 2006). Simulation does not replace the experiment. It replaces guessing at the design of the experiment.

19.11.3 What the simulation finds

The model. The study simulated a female x male factorial of single crosses, tested with one plot per hybrid x location cell. The phenotype is

\[ y = \mu + \text{loc} + \mathrm{GCA}^f + \mathrm{GCA}^m + \mathrm{SCA} + \mathrm{GCA}^f{\times}\text{loc} + \mathrm{GCA}^m{\times}\text{loc} + \mathrm{SCA}{\times}\text{loc} + e . \]

The target is the value in the TPE, \(\mathrm{GCA}^f + \mathrm{GCA}^m + \mathrm{SCA}\). In every replicate, 20 % of the hybrids get no plot at all. These are the CV1 hybrids, predicted only through relationships.

The estimands. Three accuracies are reported, and they are three different correlations, computed within a replicate and then averaged over replicates. acc_all is the Pearson correlation between a hybrid’s BLUP and its true TPE value \(\mathrm{GCA}^f + \mathrm{GCA}^m + \mathrm{SCA}\), over all 400 hybrids of the factorial, planted or not. acc_cv1 is the same correlation over the hybrids with no plot. acc_cv2 is the correlation, over the missing cells of planted hybrids, between the location-specific prediction and the location-specific true value (which includes the interactions with location, among them \(\mathrm{SCA}\times\text{loc}\), which the analysis model cannot carry). None of the three is the acc_gca_all of Chapter 18, a correlation of GCA estimates with true GCA over lines. For the in-repo sweep these definitions are those of one_fit() in regenerate.R; for the cached REML study they are assumed to be the same, and the code of that study is not in this repository.

The planted share. In the in-repo sweep the 80 % of hybrids that are planted are drawn at random from the 20 x 20 factorial (the cached study is described as leaving 20 % untested, and its mechanism is assumed to be the same), so every line stays a parent of about 16 planted crosses. That makes the “CV1 is T2” correspondence of the callout above true here, and it makes shared parents abundant. Both are properties of this configuration (see Scope).

The calibration is sparse_vc():

  • \(\sigma^2_f = \sigma^2_m = 1\) and \(\sigma^2_s = \sigma^2_{SCA} = 0.25\), so that \(\sigma^2_G = 2.25\) and \(\sigma^2_s/\sigma^2_g = 0.25\);
  • every interaction with location is half its main effect, so that \(\sigma^2_{GE} = 1.125\);
  • plot \(h^2 = \sigma^2_G/(\sigma^2_G + \sigma^2_{GE} + \sigma^2_e)\) of 0.20 or 0.40, which fixes \(\sigma^2_e\).

In sparse_vc() the components are s2f, s2m, s2s (\(\sigma^2_{SCA}\)), s2fl, s2ml (the two GCA x location variances), s2sl (\(\sigma^2_{SCA\times L}\), written \(\sigma^2_{s\ell}\) in Section 20.2) and s2e; s2res is the residual of the analysis model, \(\sigma^2_{res} = \sigma^2_{SCA\times L} + \sigma^2_e\).

These choices imply a genetic correlation between locations of \(\sigma^2_G/(\sigma^2_G+\sigma^2_{GE}) = 0.667\). That sits inside the 0.36 to 0.67 measured for plant height in maize (Li et al. 2026) and close to the 0.60 of Werner, Covarrubias-Pazaran, et al. (2025). The ratio \(\sigma^2_s/\sigma^2_g\) of 0.25 lies among the values reported by González-Diéguez et al. (2026) for their own ratio. The plot heritabilities 0.2 and 0.4 lie above the 0.10 of Werner, Covarrubias-Pazaran, et al. (2025)’s founder population. The 0.30 of González-Diéguez et al. (2026) is a broad-sense \(H^2\) over all hybrids, a different quantity from the plot-level \(h^2\) of Box 2, so it does not bracket the values here. Plot heritability is also defined differently in the other chapters: in Chapter 18 the interaction with location is folded into the plot error, and in Chapter 14 it is the heritability in a single environment. The three values are not on one scale.

The four strategies, all at the same cost of \(k\) locations per hybrid out of \(L\) in the trial (\(k\) is not the testers per pool of Chapter 18):

  • complete;
  • random: each hybrid goes to \(k\) locations drawn at random;
  • optimised blocks: an exchange algorithm on the mixed-model coefficient matrix, with replicated checks;
  • parentage: the factorial is cut into cyclic groups, each holding every female and every male, and groups are dealt to locations so that every location receives both whole pools (the rule that Chapter 20 calls coverage).

The last is sparse_alloc(strategy = "parentage").

19.11.3.6 6. Two stages: covering more candidates sparsely gave more gain at the same budget (four replicates)

The two-stage scenario works as follows:

  • A pool of 400 candidate hybrids is available.
  • Stage 1 tests \(N_1\) of them sparsely and keeps the top \(q\) by BLUP.

19.11.3.7 Limitations of the simulation

The simulation has eight limitations.

  1. One plot per cell. SCA x location then has the same incidence as plot error, and the analysis model absorbs it into an i.i.d. residual that estimates \(\sigma^2_{SCA\times L} + \sigma^2_e\). Equal incidence alone does not make the two inseparable: drawn with the kernel \(\mathbf A_f \otimes \mathbf A_m\), as sparse_simulate() draws it, SCA x location is correlated between related crosses in one location and could be carried as a structured term; Section 20.2 measures what that buys in one configuration. Within-location replication was not simulated.
  2. G x E is full-rank and symmetric in the fast layer, and rank one in the genomic layer. Neither layer has the factor-analytic structure of real trials or a crossover/non-crossover split (Burgueño et al. 2012; Werner, Covarrubias-Pazaran, et al. 2025).
  3. The pools are symmetric and unselected. There are no cycles of reciprocal recurrent selection, so the simulation says nothing about long-run pool trajectories (Chapter 23) or about unbalanced pools.
  4. Cost is counted in plots, not money. Opening a location costs more than adding plots to an existing one. The advice to spread plots assumes the marginal location is moderately priced (Section 18.7 has the cost structure).
  5. The grid is reduced. It has at most 15 replicates per cell, no 200-hybrid level, and the high-SCA block run separately. The two-stage layer has 4 replicates and the genomic layer 8.
  6. Components are checked by mean bias only. No per-replicate confidence intervals were computed for them, and none for the contrasts of result 3, the two-stage comparison of result 6 or the minimal allocation of result 1 beyond the standard errors printed with each.
  7. A dense factorial, planted at random. The 20 x 20 factorial has 80 % of its hybrids planted, so every line is a parent of about 16 planted crosses. Parents are shared by construction. The sparse factorials of Chapter 18 (a few crosses per line out of 200 candidates per pool, where connectivity binds) and the 2,000-lines-per-pool pipeline of Figure 19.1 are different regimes. Results 2 to 4, and the “CV1 is T2” correspondence of the callout in Section 19.3, depend on this density and were not measured at another.
  8. The cached parentage arm comes from a defective search. Its criterion returned \(-\infty\) for any disconnected start (1), so results 3 and 4 concern that layout, not sparse_alloc("parentage") of this repository.

19.12 Practical recommendations by stage

Stage 1 (first agronomic testcross trial).

Each item below carries its basis: a result of this chapter, a cited study, or neither (“not tested here”, design reasoning only).

  • Give priority to the number of candidates and the number of locations in the trial. At fixed plots per hybrid, spread them over more locations: in result 2 accuracy rose from two to six locations and showed no resolved further gain from six to ten (one configuration; 0.8 and 0.1 standard errors).
  • Use 3 to 5 testers in sparse testcrossing, with full-sibs spread across testers (basis: González-Diéguez et al. (2026), a simulation of reciprocal recurrent selection; not tested here).
  • Use low partial replication with checks replicated in every block (basis: a p-rep of 0.1 in Werner, Covarrubias-Pazaran, et al. (2025); no p-rep value, 0.1 or 0.2, is compared in this chapter, so the level is not tested here).
  • When plots per hybrid fall to one or two, a layout that puts both pools in every location is cheap to adopt and was not worse than random allocation in the cached grid; its advantage there was small and not resolved after multiplicity correction (results 3 and 4).
  • The estimation target is GCA. In the cached grid the SCA variance had an upward median bias at small \(k\) whose cause these data do not separate, so treat an SCA estimate from a stage-1 trial as unverified (result 5).

Stage 2 (pre-commercial hybrids).

  • Test fewer candidates, and raise replication and environmental coverage (not tested here: the simulation covers stage 1 and one stage-2 step in result 6).
  • Keep a common core large enough to estimate the between-environment genetic correlations (not tested here, and set against the overlap sweep of Section 19.6, where at a fixed training size a larger common core lowered accuracy over all hybrids; the item concerns estimating the correlations, which that sweep did not examine).
  • The estimation target is now specific performance and stability, so SCA and G x E matter (design reasoning; see 2).

Decisions that span both stages.

  • Genotype the parents, not the hybrids.
  • Check connectivity before planting (Box 4).
  • Match the between-environment covariance to the amount of data. Do not force an unstructured matrix on a very sparse design.
  • Advance on GBLUP rather than BLUE when heritability is low to moderate and locations are few (Atanda et al. 2022; Endelman et al. 2014).

What not to do.

  • Put everything in one location, however many candidates that allows. It gave the most variable gain in Werner, Covarrubias-Pazaran, et al. (2025), with replicates of negative gain.
  • Use one tester at stage 1 when there is infrastructure for three.
  • Transfer a sparsity percentage from another crop or programme without your own simulation.
  • Assume that a G x E term helps under extreme sparsity: Hild Aono and Chawade (2026) found that it degraded prediction when less than 66 % of the data per environment were available, whereas Lubanga et al. (2025) found that it raised predictive ability at 20 % of records per environment. The two used different data and models, and this chapter has not compared them.

The right sparse design changes from stage to stage because the parameter of interest changes: GCA in stage 1, specific performance and stability in stage 2.

TipBox 6 – A checklist for designing a sparse trial
  1. Define the TPE and the market segment.
  2. Estimate the genetic correlations between candidate locations from historical data.
  3. Fix the budget in plots.
  4. Choose the axis of sparsity: environments, testers, or both.
  5. Fix the number of testers.
  6. Fix the partial-replication rate.
  7. Choose the checks and how often they appear.
  8. Generate the allocation by restricted randomisation or a formal criterion. Where plots per hybrid are fewest, consider keeping both pools in every location (results 3 and 4: a small advantage, not resolved after correction).
  9. Compute connectivity and the expected PEV before planting (Box 4).
  10. Simulate the design with the programme’s own parameters (sparse_vc(), sparse_simulate(), sparse_blup()).
  11. Fix the analysis model and the covariance structure a priori.
  12. Fix the decision metric and the cut-off a priori.

19.13 Limitations of the evidence, and perspectives

The authors of the primary studies declare their own limitations:

  • Li et al. (2026): a narrow genetic base and a single year.
  • Atanda et al. (2022): no way to identify the optimal overlap, and families of 1 to 9.
  • Werner, Covarrubias-Pazaran, et al. (2025): no cost model, and no hybrid structure.
  • Lubanga et al. (2025): a training set that was not optimised.
  • González-Diéguez et al. (2026): no G x E, no epistasis, no diversity management, and the logistic cost ignored.
  • Hild Aono and Chawade (2026): possible information leakage, and very similar environments.

As a research agenda, the open gaps are these.

  • SCA estimation, and prediction of specific hybrids, under sparsity. In the cached grid the SCA variance had an upward median bias of +12 %, +24 % and +30 % at \(k = 4\), 3 and 2 and +220 % at \(k = 1\) (with 42.6 % convergence). Its coverage and precision, and a comparison with product-development designs, were not measured, so whether it is recoverable at stage 1 is open. The accuracy of specific unseen crosses is also unmeasured.
  • A three-dimensional connectivity metric, female x male x location. It would join Section 18.2.3 to Box 4. Section 20.4 defines a pair of counts for it.
  • The long-run effect on pool divergence and within-pool variance, in interaction with optimum contribution selection (Chapter 23; Chapter 15; Santantonio and Robbins (2020)).
  • Phenomics-assisted sparse testing in maize hybrids.
  • Pools unequal in size and breeding level, which is the usual commercial case.
  • Cost-accuracy frontiers in money, including isolation blocks and hand pollination.
  • The rate of wrongly discarded candidates as the evaluation metric, in place of the correlation.
  • Prospective empirical validation. Almost all the evidence for sparse testing comes from resampling complete data or from simulation, not from trials designed sparse from the start (Alemu et al. 2024; Crossa et al. 2017).

Sparse testing changes the stage-1 question. It is no longer “which genotypes can I evaluate well?”. It becomes “which haplotypes can I evaluate well, and against what sample of the opposite pool?”. The evidence reviewed supports considering sparse designs at stage 1; it does not support adopting them with confidence. Almost all of it comes from resampling or simulation (above). In this chapter’s own simulation a sparse allocation came within 5 % of the complete design in one cell by a margin of 0.5 standard errors (result 1), the structured allocation was not resolved against random after correction (result 3), the two-stage comparison has four replicates (result 6), and every simulated result comes from one dense factorial (limitation 7). The one result that stands beyond two standard errors is that, at a fixed number of plots, more locations raised accuracy up to about six (result 2). It is not sufficient to prescribe a universal sparsity level. That decision remains specific to each programme, and should be sized by the programme’s own simulation.

19.15 Where this connects

  • Chapter 18 compared designs of which crosses to make. This chapter asked where to plant them, mostly with the factorial taken as given (80 % of it planted at random). Result 6 traded locations per candidate against a smaller stage-1 factorial at equal plots; what it leaves is the version in which every line stays in the data and the unmade crosses are predicted, so that an extra location per hybrid costs a cross rather than a parent. Result 2 never varied the locations per hybrid; it held two plots per hybrid and widened the trial. The joint problem, Section 18.9’s second gap, is Chapter 20.
  • The GCA/SCA decomposition and the tester-frequency argument behind Box 5 are derived in Section 5.11.
  • The relationship matrices that make sparse designs analysable are the pedigree matrices of relmat_halfsib() here, and marker matrices in the cached genomic layer. Chapter 14 fits one additive GBLUP and does not fit the pool-specific or SCA kernels of this chapter.
  • The staged-trial trade-off of Chapter 22 (a later chapter, in the Evidence and practice part) is an analogy to result 6, not a replication of it: that chapter models locations as independent draws, whereas result 6 uses a genetic correlation between locations of 0.667.
  • Spreading testers and parents to slow within-pool erosion is the same concern as Chapter 15 and Chapter 23. Sparse testcrossing does by design what a diversity constraint does by optimisation.
