16  Combinatorial selection

Chapter 15 posed the problem – choose \(n\) of \(N\) hybrids to maximise the mean index \(\bar u_S\) subject to \(\alpha(S) \le \alpha_{\max}\) (Equation 15.2) – and bounded it from above (Equation 15.7). It left to this chapter the discrete side: the decoder that turns a real-valued optimiser vector into \(n\) distinct hybrids, the per-line cap max_use, and the order in which to try methods. The chapter builds the strategies from the two that need no optimiser (truncation, and truncation under a per-line cap), through a greedy swept over an index weight, to differential evolution (DE), and measures each at the book scale.

What it finds depends on the evaluation budget of the DE, and the introduction says so rather than promise otherwise. Evaluations are counted nominally as NP \(\times\) itermax. The chapter runs sel_de() at 30,000 evaluations (NP = 120, itermax = 250) and, for its five-budget frontier, at 18,000 (NP = 100, itermax = 180); the printed defaults of sel_de() are NP = 300, itermax = 2000, that is 600,000. At the chapter’s budgets, and with the default warm start, the mean of the DE’s index over repeated seeds is below the best feasible greedy plan at each of the five diversity budgets tried (Section 16.5). At the 1% diversity budget, the only one run at a larger evaluation budget, that order reverses at 120,000 and at 600,000 evaluations (Section 16.4.2); the other four diversity budgets were not run above 18,000. The warm start is worth a measured amount over a cold start at 30,000 evaluations, the only budget at which a cold start was run, and the DE does improve on a greedy plan when that plan is among its seeds (Section 16.4.1; 1% budget, 30,000 evaluations). What the DE offers at the chapter’s budgets is a budget as its interface; the last section states what the three budgets do and do not support, within the limits of these measurements, which are one simulated dataset at the book scale.

The decision is a concrete one, and it is the last of a cycle in a cross-pollinated crop. Two heterotic pools, A and B, hold \(L_A\) and \(L_B\) inbred (or doubled-haploid) lines. Every candidate is one cross \(A_a \times B_b\), so the candidate set is the factorial \(N = L_A \times L_B\) – in maize, the set of single crosses that could be made from the current elite lines without introducing anything new. The \(n\) you keep are the crosses that go forward: into multi-location testing, into seed increase, and – through their parents – into the next recycling cycle. Keeping too few distinct parents is not a statistical inconvenience, it is a decision to narrow the pool that the programme will breed from next.

What has to be in hand before any line of this chapter runs is the three-object contract of Chapter 6:

  • X, the \(N \times m\) hybrid marker matrix, coded \(\{0, 0.5, 1\}\) as the average of the two homozygous parents;
  • f, the \(N \times N\) molecular coancestry of Caballero and Toro (2002), values in \([0, 1]\), built from X and never from a centred genomic relationship matrix (Section 2.2);
  • hybrids, carrying each cross’s parents and its predicted traits, from which build_ctx() forms the single index ctx$index, standardised to mean 0 and unit variance over the candidate set (Chapter 6).

Assumptions, declared once and not violated afterwards. They are those of Chapter 15, restated for the claims made here.

  1. Parent lines are homozygous, so a hybrid’s genotype is the mean of its parents and X takes only the three values above. Markers are treated as neutral.
  2. The index is the truth. Every index value below is ctx$index, computed from known marker effects, not a prediction. Nothing is re-predicted inside an optimiser, so the plans of this chapter are not evaluated against the error that a real index carries. Section 12.5 simulates one scenario of such error, correlated with relatedness; that scenario is not used here and is not a property the chapter relies on.
  3. The constraint pins one lens. \(\theta\), \(GD\) and \(\alpha\) are the homozygosity lens; \(F_{\text{drift}}\), anchored on the frozen frequencies ctx$p0, is not pinned by \(\alpha\) (Chapter 10). ctx$p0 is the allele-frequency base of the candidate set, set once in build_ctx().
  4. Diversity loss \(\alpha\) is always measured against a random subset of the same size n_sel (Section 11.9), never against the whole candidate set.
  5. Dominance appears nowhere in the objective: it is already inside the predicted hybrid performance, and the between-pool divergence that generates heterosis is a quantity to preserve, not to minimise (Chapter 5).

Scale. “Book scale” is 25 + 25 lines, 2000 markers, simulation seed 1, \(N =\) 625, \(n =\) 60, and is what every number below refers to unless a sentence names another scale. Four other scales are named where they occur, because the word “production” has been used for more than one of them: the benchmark book scale and the benchmark production scale of search_benchmark.csv (Chapter 17; regenerated in this repository by book/scripts/regenerate.R search), which the file records as \(N =\) 625, \(L =\) 50, \(n =\) 60, cap 5 and \(N =\) 10,000, \(L =\) 200, \(n =\) 200, cap 4; the cached-run scale of de_constraints.csv, \(N =\) 3,600 hybrids with \(L = 120\) lines (an external file, below); and the package production scale, 71 + 71 lines and \(N =\) 5,041, used only to count dimensions. The benchmark book scale has this chapter’s \(N\), \(L\) and \(n\) but a cap of 5, where this chapter’s capped truncation uses 4.

The per-line usage cap, defined once. A line \(a\) is used once by each selected hybrid it parents, so the number of uses is \(k_a\), with \(\sum_a k_a = 2n\) (Appendix A). The cap, \(k_{\max}\), is the largest \(k_a\) a plan may have: at most \(k_{\max}\) of the \(n\) selected hybrids may share a line as a parent. It is a count of crosses, not of seeds or plants. The argument that carries it is called cap in sel_truncation_cap() and max_use in sel_greedy(), make_fitness(), sel_de() and stage2_select() (and in decode_lines(), make_fitness_fast(), sel_de_fast(), sel_local_search() and ocs_round() of Chapter 17); it is one quantity under two argument names. max_use = NULL is the default everywhere and means no cap; it reproduces the behaviour before the cap existed bit for bit. sel_truncation_cap() has no NULL and defaults to ceiling(2 * n_sel / n_lines) + 1, one above even use. The realised value of a plan is max_line_use(), printed as max_line. In make_fitness() the cap enters as the number of parent slots above \(k_{\max}\), summed over lines and divided by \(2n\), added to the violation of \(\alpha\) and charged at the same penalty (R/03_de_select.R); in the greedy it is a feasibility test on the move.

Read it as a breeder would. 60 crosses are kept out of 625, which is 9.6% of the factorial. Three of those six rows are the same plan. greedy_w1 and greedy_w3 return exactly the truncation set (the chunk asserts it) – same index, same \(\alpha\), same max_line – because both index weights sit far above the crossover measured in the w-units chunk below, where the merit term swamps the coancestry term and the greedy argmax degenerates to the index ranking. Read them as one row until that measurement has been made; it is the reason an \(\omega\) grid of round numbers is a trap.

Read the columns in this order, because each answers a different question and three of them are routinely skipped:

  1. index, in standard deviations of the candidate set – what the plan is worth.
  2. alpha_pct, the loss of gene diversity relative to a random subset of the same size – what it costs, on the lens the constraint will pin. Negative means more diverse than random.
  3. Ns, the status number \(N_s = 1/(2\theta)\) (Section 11.3). On the molecular coancestry \(\mathbf{f}\) it lies in \([1/2, 1]\) and is a monotone rescaling of \(\theta\), not a head count; unlike \(\alpha\) it carries no reference to a random subset of the same size.
  4. Ne_parents, the effective number of parent lines, which is computed from counts alone and is the metric that catches “\(\theta\) looks fine but twelve lines did all the work”.
  5. max_line, the largest number of selected crosses sharing one parent, the realised \(k_{\max}\) – the operational number, because that line is a seed parent and has to be increased, maintained and, in a CMS system, converted.

Truncation is the plan of maximum mean index: the optimum with no constraint, and the reference for the budget (\(\alpha_{\text{trunc}}\)). It is what every programme does by default, and the interesting question is not whether it loses diversity but how little index a constrained plan has to give up relative to it. It is not the \(n\)-subset of lowest diversity (Chapter 15): a plan confined to fewer lines loses more.

The procedure, stated as a breeder would run it:

  1. Rank all 625 candidate crosses by index, best first.
  2. Walk down the ranking. Admit a cross only if both its parents are still below the cap.
  3. Stop when n_sel crosses have been admitted, or when the ranking is exhausted – in which case the returned vector is shorter than n_sel and the cap was infeasible for this set size.
  4. A cap \(k_{\max}\) admits at most \(L_A k_{\max}\) crosses through pool A and at most \(L_B k_{\max}\) through pool B, so \(n \le k_{\max}\min(L_A, L_B)\) is necessary for the walk to reach \(n\): here 60 against
    1. It is not sufficient, because the walk is greedy and can exhaust the ranking while admissible slots remain on lines that no remaining cross uses together:

The default cap is ceiling(2 * n_sel / ctx$n_lines) + 1, one above even use, which is 4 here. Filling 60 slots under it required scanning 413 of the 625 ranked candidates, and only 16 of the resulting crosses are also in the truncation set: the cap does not trim the top of the ranking, it reshuffles most of it. The last two rows count the same reshuffling in lines rather than in crosses, and they are the reason \(N_{e,\text{par}}\) is not a line count: the capped plan touches 32 distinct parent lines against truncation’s 33, out of 50 available – to within one line, the same set size – while its effective number of parents is 31 against 13. What the cap changes is how evenly the 120 parent slots are spread over the lines that are used, not how many lines are used at all.

What that buys and what it costs, separately. The cap cuts the diversity loss from 4.12% to 0.33% and raises the effective number of parents from 13 to 31, at 41% of truncation’s index. Whether that is a good trade is a programme decision, not a statistical one – but note that the capped plan is feasible at a 1% budget while truncation is not, and it was found by counting alone.

Before reaching for an optimiser, run this. If a per-line cap already meets your constraint at an acceptable index, you are done. The failure mode at this step is silent: a returned vector shorter than n_sel, which every downstream metric will happily evaluate on the wrong set size – length() it.

16.2 Greedy, with incremental updates

Adding hybrid \(j\) to an existing set changes the submatrix total by \(2 s_j + f_{jj}\), where \(s_j\) is the sum of \(f_{ij}\) over the already-chosen. That makes each step \(O(N)\) instead of \(O(n^2)\), and the whole construction affordable.

One step \(k\), in the order the code performs it:

  1. theta_new <- (total + 2 * s + d) / k^2 is the group coancestry the set would have, for every candidate at once, with total the current submatrix sum, s the running vector of coancestry sums against the chosen set and d = diag(f). The normaliser is \(k^2\), the size after the addition.
  2. score <- w * ctx$index - theta_new – merit against relatedness.
  3. Candidates already chosen are set to -Inf; with max_use set, so is every hybrid of a line that has reached its cap. The cap is therefore a feasibility test on the move, never a penalty on the result – the partition-matroid treatment of Chapter 17.
  4. The argmax is taken, total and s are updated in \(O(N)\), and the parent counts incremented.
  5. If no finite score remains, the loop exits early and the returned vector is short – the same check as for the capped truncation.

The score is \(\omega\,u_i - \theta_{\text{new}}\), where \(\omega \ge 0\) is the index weight of Appendix A and w is its argument name in the code (it is not the line-usage vector \(\mathbf{w}\) of Section 12.1). At \(\omega = 0\) the greedy returns the most diverse plan it can build – a heuristic, with no certificate that it is the minimum of \(\theta\) over all \(n\)-subsets – at near-zero gain. As \(\omega \to \infty\) it converges to truncation. Sweeping \(\omega\) traces a frontier – but only if the grid is in the right units, and that is the trap at this step.

\(\omega\) is an exchange rate. Under the score \(\omega\,u_i - \theta_{\text{new}}\) two candidates are indifferent when \(\omega\,\Delta u = \Delta\theta\), so \(\omega = \Delta\theta / \Delta u\), in units of group coancestry per index standard deviation: the greedy argmax accepts, for one standard deviation of index, a rise of \(\theta\) of up to \(\omega\). The multiplier \(\lambda\) of Equation 15.7, in \(V(\lambda) = \max\,\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{u} - \lambda\,\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{c}\), has the reciprocal dimension, index units per unit of \(\theta\); how the numerical values of the two relate is not examined here. The two quantities \(\omega\) converts between differ by four orders of magnitude, and that ratio is measurable before any sweep is run:

16.3 The encoding problem

Differential evolution searches a box of real numbers, and an encoding turns a point of the box into a set of \(n\) distinct hybrids. Three encodings appear in this book, and they differ in dimension and in what a coordinate means:

  1. \(n\) keys (decode()): \(n\) coordinates, each naming a candidate by its integer part. This is what sel_de() searches; the dimension is \(n =\) 60 here.
  2. \(N\) keys (decode_rank()): one random key per candidate, the \(n\) smallest keys selected; the dimension is \(N\), which is 625 here, 3,600 in the cached run below and 5,041 at the package production scale. A 0/1 vector of length \(N\) has the same dimension; the package implements the key form and I did not test the 0/1 form.
  3. \(L\) line weights (decode_lines()): one weight per line, dimension \(L\).

The package searches \(n\) coordinates, not \(N\), because the dimension of the \(N\)-key search grows with the candidate set. Whether that makes the \(N\)-key search infeasible, or only under-powered at a given budget, is not settled by anything in the book: the only measurement of the \(N\)-key encoding is the cached external run below, at one budget, and its limits are stated with it.

The decode of the first encoding, step by step:

  1. as.integer(par) truncates each coordinate toward zero, so the real interval \([i, i+1)\) maps to candidate \(i\). sel_de() searches the box \([1,\, N + 0.999]\), so candidates \(1\) to \(N-1\) each own a full unit interval and candidate \(N\) owns \([N,\, N + 0.999]\) – narrower by one part in a thousand, because the upper bound stops just short of \(N + 1\) instead of reaching it.
  2. Coordinates outside the box are clamped to the ends.
  3. duplicated() marks every coordinate after the first that lands on an already-used candidate.
  4. Each duplicate is replaced by the lowest index not otherwise in use, so the result always has exactly n_sel distinct candidates.

16.3.1 A third encoding: one weight per line

Section 12.1 showed that \(\theta\) depends only on the line-usage vector. The line weight shifts each hybrid’s score by the weights of its two parents, and hybrids are taken greedily subject to the per-line usage cap – so the cap is satisfied by construction rather than by penalty. In detail:

  1. Each candidate scores ctx$index + w_div * (p[a] + p[b]), with p the line-weight vector being searched and w_div = 3 by default. A line’s weight therefore moves every cross it parents, which is what makes the decision a line decision.
  2. Candidates are taken in decreasing score, skipping any whose parents have reached max_use.
  3. If the cap makes n_sel unreachable, the selection is topped up ignoring it, and the fitness function’s penalty term then sees the violation – so an infeasible cap degrades gracefully instead of returning a short vector.

At p = 0 the decoder is exactly capped truncation, which is the natural zero of the encoding; Chapter 17 verifies that identity numerically where the encoding is actually driven.

The file does not record the number selected \(n\), the DE population size NP, the iteration count or the penalty multiplier.

16.4 Differential evolution

The run, as a procedure:

  1. Fix n_sel first, then obtain gd_ref from null_distribution() at that same size. A reference computed at another size is a different quantity and every \(\alpha\) downstream is wrong by a fixed factor.
  2. Choose \(\alpha_{\max}\) below \(\alpha_{\text{trunc}}\), printed at the top of this chapter, by the procedure of Chapter 15.
  3. Call sel_de(). It builds its warm start, injects it into the initial population, and hands DEoptim the fitness \(-(\bar u_S - 1000 \cdot \text{viol})\) to minimise, with \(\text{viol} = \max(0, \alpha - \alpha_{\max})\), an absolute excess in units of \(\alpha\) – plus, when max_use is set, the cap term defined above.
  4. Check the returned plan before using it: realised \(\alpha\) against the budget, max_line, \(N_{e,\text{par}}\), and the gap to the bound. The diversity constraint is a penalty, so a returned plan is not guaranteed feasible – the violation is only made expensive.

The realised loss sits just inside the budget, which is the sign that the constraint is active and the penalty correctly scaled: with penalty = 1000 multiplying the absolute violation, one tenth of a percentage point of excess loss costs a full index standard deviation. 16.4.3 Checking convergence against the bound

Section 15.4 derived the bound. For every plan \(S\) with \(\theta_S \le \theta_{\max}\), where \(\theta_{\max} = 1 - GD_{\text{ref}}(1 - \alpha_{\max})\) (Equation 15.3) and \(\bar u_S\) is the mean index (Equation 15.2),

\[\bar u_S \;\le\; \min_{\lambda \ge 0}\,\bigl[\,V(\lambda) + \lambda\,\theta_{\max}\bigr]\]

(Equation 15.7, with \(V(\lambda)\) the optimum of the continuous relaxation, Equation 15.5; \(\lambda\) the multiplier on \(\theta\), in index units per unit of \(\theta\)). Every \(\lambda\) gives a valid bound, so a coarse grid costs tightness, not validity. ocs_bound() returns the minimum over a grid of \(\lambda\), doubles the number of projected-gradient iterations until the value changes by less than a relative \(10^{-3}\), and sets a converged attribute: a stalling criterion on that doubling, not a certificate (Section 15.4). An under-converged relaxation reports \(V(\lambda)\) too small, which makes the “bound” too small, so the attribute has to be read before any gap is quoted (Section 17.7).

At the book scale the second is limited by the benchmark of Chapter 17: its simulated-annealing plan reaches 1.528 against its own bound 1.554, a gap of 1.7% (3 seeds; the benchmark computes its reference from \(B\) = 500 random subsets, this chapter from 400, hence the slightly different bound). So at this scale and evaluation budget most of the DE’s gap is the optimiser’s, not the relaxation’s; that reading holds only where the bound is certified and the relaxation is known to be tight. At the benchmark production scale the flag is FALSE in 36 of 36 rows, so no gap there is quoted, and “a large gap means the optimiser has not converged” cannot be applied.

TipIf the DE ever looks unconvincing on real data

De Beukelaer et al. (2018), optimising core-collection subsets with stochastic search rather than DE, rank three search algorithms of the Core Hunter framework on the same objective across four crop datasets – rice, coconut, maize and pea – in the Results section “Optimizing E-NE and A-NE with local searches”: parallel tempering first, a genetic algorithm second, and random descent – a plain stochastic hill-climber – third, with all twelve pairwise comparisons significant under a Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon test. The objective of that comparison is E-NE only, the average distance from each selected entry to its nearest other selected entry (the authors state they ran these experiments only for E-NE): a distance-based diversity measure, not allele coverage. The genetic algorithm therefore does beat the hill-climber on raw score. What it does not do is win, and the method that wins is the one assembled from many simple local searches run in parallel, not the one with the more elaborate update rule.

The inference, stated separately from that result and limited by it: extra sophistication inside a single-solution algorithm bought a rank there and not the win, which is a weak reason to spend complexity on the optimiser and no reason at all to expect a DE to be the right one. It is one application, on a distance-based objective without an index or a coancestry budget, so it transfers as a prior about where effort pays and not as a result. That direction is taken up in Chapter 17, which builds the local search on the O(L) swap this package already had and measures it against the DE at equal wall-clock.

One optimisation per budget, which is the \(\varepsilon\)-constraint method of Haimes et al. (1971): maximise one objective (the index) subject to a bound on the other (\(\alpha \le \alpha_{\max}\)). Each budget is run with 10 seeds, at NP = 100 and itermax = 180: 18,000 evaluations, against the 600,000 of the printed defaults of sel_de().

Three things to read, in this order.

The constraint does its job, softly. In 48 of 50 runs the DE plan’s \(\alpha\) is within its budget; the other 2 exceed it by at most 0.0011 percentage points, which the penalty prices at 0.011 index units at most – the constraint is a penalty, as the procedure above says, and the returned plan has to be checked. A plan that lands far inside its budget has not been constrained by it, and whatever else is limiting it – the cap, the penalty scale, an unconverged search – has to be identified before the frontier is read as a price list.

The comparison against greedy depends on the greedy grid. Each grey point is the index maximiser of the continuous problem at its own diversity, so by weak duality (Equation 15.6) it bounds every discrete plan at least as diverse as itself. The vertical distance to the bound at \(\alpha_{\max}\) is what Chapter 17 spends its effort on.

16.6 Which strategy to use

Two of the strategies have a benchmark with a cap and a budget together, at two scales, in the file of Chapter 17. It is read here on its capped rows, with the dispersion it carries.

Result. At the benchmark production scale the five capped routes return 1.1223 to 1.1225, a spread of 0.0002 with a seed standard deviation of 0 in every route, and \(\alpha\) of 0.31 to 0.32% against a budget of 1%. The cap binds there (max uses equals the cap) and the budget does not. At the benchmark book scale the capped routes reach 1.106 to 1.112 with \(\alpha\) near the budget (0.88 to 0.98%); de_hybrid_cap returns the tempering_cap row and de_lines_cap the ocs_round_cap row to the four decimals of the table, in index and in \(\alpha\). greedy_cap needs the \(N \times N\) matrix, which at \(N =\) 10,000 is 0.745 GiB in double precision. Interpretation: at the production scale the difference between capped routes is 0.0002 with no seed spread to judge it by, a tie, and with the budget slack the capped problem there is a cap-only problem, so the routes are ranked by cost and memory and not by index; at the book scale the routes differ in index by more than that, and the table does not rank them beyond reading it. Timings of a few milliseconds are means of 3 single calls and do not separate greedy_cap from ocs_round_cap at the book scale. All of this is one simulated dataset per scale and three seeds.

As an order of operations at the end of a cycle:

  1. Build the contract objects and print the instance panel at the top of this chapter. Confirm gd_ref was computed at the n_sel you will actually use.
  2. Compute \(\alpha_{\text{trunc}}\) from truncation. If it is below your intended \(\alpha_{\max}\), stop: the constraint is inactive and truncation is the answer.
  3. Run sel_truncation_cap(). It costs nothing and needs no matrix. If it meets the budget at an acceptable index, stop.
  4. Sweep sel_greedy() over an \(\omega\) grid that begins well below the crossover measured from your own data (here the index starts rising about 30 times below it) and ends above the point where the plan becomes truncation, not a grid of round numbers. Read the frontier and locate the knee.
  5. Only if a specific budget has to be hit, or an \(N_{e,\text{par}}\) floor applies, run an optimiser, with an evaluation budget of the order of the printed default of sel_de() (at the 1% budget of this chapter’s dataset 30,000 evaluations did not pass the best fine greedy plan and 120,000 did) – and warm-start it, with the best feasible greedy plan among the seeds (measured at 30,000 evaluations only).
  6. Whatever returned the plan, validate it on the second lens and on allelic richness before it leaves the analysis (Chapter 25).

The summary, within what was measured here, which is one simulated dataset at the book scale. At the chapter’s evaluation budgets (18,000 to 30,000), a fine greedy sweep reaches a higher index than sel_de() at each of five budgets, and at those budgets what the DE offers is a budget as its interface. At the 1% diversity budget, the only one run at larger evaluation budgets, the DE’s mean index is above the best fine greedy plan at 120,000 evaluations and at the printed default of 600,000 (Table 16.6), at a wall-clock cost that rises with the budget; seeded with the best greedy plan it improved on that plan at 30,000 evaluations (Table 16.4). The worth of the warm start at the larger budgets, and the five-budget comparison there, were not measured, and nothing here is measured at another scale. For the capped case at the benchmark production scale something simpler is as good on index, and the cost table of Section 12.2 is the reason to check which row you are in before writing any optimisation code. The claim that a restriction a greedy cannot test (an \(N_{e,\text{par}}\) floor) rules the cheap routes out rests on the argument of Chapter 17, not on a measurement in this chapter.
