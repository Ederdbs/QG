11  A catalogue of diversity metrics

Chapter 10 separated two lenses on one selection, \(F_{hom}\) and \(F_{drift}\), and read both against a resampled null of random subsets before that null had been defined. This chapter is where the catalogue of metrics is defined once, where the baseline of that null is derived (Section 11.9), and where every metric, those two lenses included, is read against it (Section 11.10). It also settles what “no loss” means, which turns out to be less obvious than it sounds.

Every metric in the catalogue has the same signature, f(idx, ctx) -> scalar. That uniformity is what makes metrics interchangeable inside an optimiser and what lets Chapter 12 benchmark them against each other on equal terms. It does not mean that they read the same object, and this chapter keeps four inputs apart (Chapter 2 gives the convention):

  • the molecular coancestry matrix ctx[["f"]]: theta_group(), gene_diversity(), status_number(), mean_offdiag(), ene(), ane(), eff_dim(), and the GD_WI_hyb column of gd_partition();
  • the marker frequencies of the selection, colMeans(ctx[["X"]][idx, ]), with the frozen base ctx[["p0"]] for the two lenses: theta_from_freq(), he_nei(), alleles_lost(), \(F_{hom}\) and \(F_{drift}\);
  • the pedigree, ctx[["ped"]], which says which lines parent each hybrid: ne_parents(), ne_lines_pool();
  • the line coancestry ctx[["fL"]] weighted by line usage, which is the pedigree again: theta_pools() and gd_partition().

Only the first group reads the hybrid-level matrix \(\mathbf{f}\), and a centred matrix stored in its place would reach only that group (Chapter 2).

11.1 Group coancestry, and the off-diagonal problem

The group coancestry (Cockerham 1967) of a set \(S\) of \(n\) individuals is the mean of all \(n^2\) elements, diagonal included. Caballero and Toro (2002, 290) average over all pairs of individuals and cite Cockerham (1967) for it (Cockerham’s own text was not checked here):

\[\theta_S \;=\; \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i \in S}\sum_{j \in S} f_{ij} \;=\; \frac{1}{n^2}\,\mathbf{1}'\mathbf{f}_S\mathbf{1}. \tag{11.1}\]

It is the probability that two alleles sampled at random from the whole set are identical – including the possibility that both come from the same individual. That is why the diagonal enters: it is the chance of sampling the same individual twice, which is entirely real when the set is used as a crossing pool.

Splitting the diagonal mean \(\bar f_d\) from the off-diagonal mean \(\bar f_o\) of Equation 11.1,

\[\theta_S \;=\; \frac{\bar f_d}{n} \;+\; \frac{n-1}{n}\,\bar f_o . \tag{11.2}\]

So the off-diagonal mean – the metric many programmes already report – is one of two terms. When \(\bar f_d\) varies little across individuals, which is the case in an all-F1 population, the two rank almost identically (Table 11.1).

Over the five selections the Spearman rank correlation between \(\theta_S\) and \(\bar f_o\) is 1; five points are a check of the ordering, not an estimate of the correlation.

The off-diagonal metric ranks correctly. What it does not give you is an interpretable scale or a defensible baseline – which is what the rest of this chapter supplies.

11.2 Gene diversity: \(\theta\) and \(H_e\) are the same quantity

This is the result that simplifies everything downstream. Write \(x_{ik} \in \{0, 0.5, 1\}\) for the marker value of individual \(i\) at marker \(k\), one of \(m\) markers. Starting from Equation 11.1, insert \(f_{ij} = m^{-1}\sum_k[x_{ik}x_{jk} + (1-x_{ik})(1-x_{jk})]\) (Equation 2.5) and swap the order of summation. Each marker contributes two double sums, and each factors:

\[\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_i\sum_j x_{ik}x_{jk} = \Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_{ik}\Big)^2 = \bar p_k^2, \qquad \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_i\sum_j (1-x_{ik})(1-x_{jk}) = (1 - \bar p_k)^2 = \bar q_k^2,\]

with \(\bar p_k\) the group’s allele frequency and \(\bar q_k = 1 - \bar p_k\). Hence

\[\theta_S = \frac{1}{m}\sum_k \big(\bar p_k^2 + \bar q_k^2\big), \tag{11.3}\]

and \(GD_S = 1 - \theta_S = m^{-1}\sum_k 2\,\bar p_k \bar q_k \equiv H_e\), which is Equation 1.2: Nei’s gene diversity (Nei 1973) in its plug-in form, at the set’s own frequencies and without the \(2n/(2n-1)\) small-sample correction. Group coancestry and expected heterozygosity are not two independent checks. They are one quantity written two ways. Table 11.2 gives both pairs for one random subset.

Show code 
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(theta_group(idx, ctx), theta_from_freq(idx, ctx))),
          isTRUE(all.equal(gene_diversity(idx, ctx), he_nei(idx, ctx))))
d_theta <- theta_group(idx, ctx) - theta_from_freq(idx, ctx)
data.frame(quantity = c("theta, via_submatrix", "theta, via_frequency",
                        "difference", "GD", "He_Nei"),
           value = c(sprintf("%.6f", c(theta_group(idx, ctx), theta_from_freq(idx, ctx))),
                     sprintf("%.1e", d_theta),
                     sprintf("%.6f", c(gene_diversity(idx, ctx), he_nei(idx, ctx)))))

The chunk asserts both equalities with all.equal() at its default relative tolerance, \(1.5 \times 10^{-8}\); the size of the printed difference is exactly 0. This equality is the implementation’s strongest sanity check. If it fails, f is centered, rescaled, or built on the wrong marker coding.

Three practical consequences:

  1. One fewer metric to justify to a technical committee.
  2. “Losing 5 % of diversity” acquires an exact meaning: \(GD_S\) is 5 % below the expected heterozygosity of a random subset of the same size (Section 11.9), not 5 % of the set’s own.
  3. Two computation routes with the same answer and very different costs. The matrix route is \(O(n^2)\); the frequency route is \(O(nm)\). For \(n = 200\) and \(m = 25000\) that is forty thousand operations against five million. Table 11.3 times both at this chapter’s scale.

The matrix route is the one that belongs in an optimiser’s inner loop. Section 12.1 makes it cheaper still.

11.3 Status number \(N_s\)

Lindgren et al. (1997) express group coancestry on the scale of individual counts:

\[N_s = \frac{1}{2\theta_S}. \tag{11.4}\]

\(N_s\) reads as a head count only on coancestry measured against a base population, in which unrelated non-inbred individuals have coancestry 0 with each other and \(1/2\) with themselves: a set of \(n\) of them has \(\theta_S = 1/(2n)\) and \(N_s = n\). Molecular \(\mathbf{f}\) is identity in state and is not measured against such a base: unrelated individuals sit at a floor set by the marker frequencies, not at 0 (Chapter 2, Equation 1.3). By Equation 11.3, \(\theta_S = m^{-1}\sum_k(\bar p_k^2 + \bar q_k^2)\), and \(\bar p_k^2 + \bar q_k^2 \ge 1/2\) at every biallelic locus, so on \(\mathbf{f}\) \(\theta_S \in [1/2, 1]\) and \(N_s \in [1/2, 1]\) for every set. Here \(N_s\) is a monotone decreasing rescaling of \(\theta_S\), not a number of individuals. The two edge cases are the two bounds (Table 11.4):

Show code 
fake <- ctx
fake[["f"]] <- matrix(0.5, 5, 5)      # every group frequency 0.5: theta at its lower bound
lo <- c(theta = theta_group(1:5, fake), Ns = status_number(1:5, fake))
fake[["f"]] <- matrix(1, 5, 5)        # all identical and homozygous: theta at its upper bound
hi <- c(theta = theta_group(1:5, fake), Ns = status_number(1:5, fake))
data.frame(case = c("all f = 0.5", "all f = 1"), rbind(lo, hi), row.names = NULL)

Five individuals give \(N_s = 1\), not 5. The five selections of this chapter fall between 0.727 and 0.747.

Warning\(N_s\) is a descriptor, not a drift projection

Toro et al. (2020) report, in their abstract, that under optimal molecular management diversity increases in the early generations, so that the rate-based effective size \(N_e = 1/(2\Delta F)\) (Equation 10.1) takes negative values, and that in the long term it does not reach an asymptote and behaves unpredictably. Only the abstract was read for this chapter.

This does not invalidate \(N_s\). \(N_s\) is a monotonic reparametrisation of \(\theta\) – a static descriptor of a set – while \(N_e\) is a rate between generations. Use \(N_s\) to compare sets; do not extrapolate it to project future drift. For the same reason, do not substitute the linkage-disequilibrium \(N_e\) here: that estimates population history, not the composition of a selected sample.

And, from Chapter 10: \(\theta\) tracks homozygosity, not drift. \(GD\) and \(N_s\) describe the expected heterozygosity \(H_e\) of the merged set at its own frequencies (Equation 1.2). That is not the heterozygosity the F1s carry. For the complete factorial of the selected lines at their usage weights the expected F1 heterozygosity is \(GD_T + GD_{BS}\) (Equation 5.6, Chapter 1), and the heterozygosity of the pairs actually kept is \(2\,GD_{WI\text{-}hyb}\), which a selection can move against \(GD\). In Table 11.10, from the random subset of this chapter to truncation, \(GD\) goes from 0.3259 to 0.3124 while GD_WI_hyb goes from 0.1814 to 0.1823; this is one founder draw and one random subset. Chapter 10 measures the sign of the truncation selection’s realised F1 heterozygosity against a random-subset null on twenty founder draws and finds no consistent sign (Table 10.6). \(GD\) is therefore not a measure of next season’s hybrid vigour, which needs the F1s’ own heterozygosity and dominance at the loci that matter (Chapter 5), not the coancestry among candidates. \(GD\) and \(N_s\) also do not certify that allele frequencies are moving as unmanaged drift would have moved them, which matters more across multiple recurrent cycles than for a single round of hybrid selection.

11.4 Effective number of parents

A metric independent of \(\mathbf{f}\), computed purely from the pedigree. With \(p_i\) the frequency with which line \(i\) appears as a parent among the selected hybrids,

\[N_{e,\text{par}} = \frac{1}{\sum_i p_i^2}, \tag{11.5}\]

the reciprocal of Simpson’s concentration index \(\sum_i p_i^2\) (Simpson 1949); the founder equivalents of Lacy (1989) are attributed the same form over founder contributions (UNVERIFIED: the 1989 paper was not available, only the concept was confirmed from secondary sources). It captures what coancestry can blur: in a hypothetical set of 200 hybrids that draws on only 12 lines, \(\theta\) can be acceptable if those 12 happen to be genetically distant from each other. The case is constructed, not computed here. Table 11.5 gives it for the five selections. It is bounded above by the number of lines, 50, a bound reached only if every line is used equally often.

Show code 
fmt(data.frame(Ne_parents = sapply(sels, ne_parents, ctx = ctx)), 2)

In relative terms, truncation reduces \(N_{e,\text{par}}\) by 62.2 % of the random set’s value and raises \(\theta\) by 2.01 %. On the z scale of Table 11.18 the order reverses on this founder draw, because \(\theta\) varies far less among random subsets than \(N_{e,\text{par}}\) does (Table 11.19 counts the draws, of twenty, on which it does). The cost of \(N_{e,\text{par}}\) is a tabulate call, which makes it a natural candidate to enter the objective function alongside \(\theta\) – and in Chapter 12 it does.

11.5 Allelic richness

\(\theta\) and \(H_e\) are averages across markers and are dominated by intermediate-frequency alleles. A lost rare allele barely moves them, yet in a closed population, with no reintroduction from outside germplasm, the loss is irreversible. López-Cortegano et al. (2019) (pp. 1-2) relate expected heterozygosity to the short-term response to selection and allelic diversity, which is more sensitive to bottlenecks, to the long-term one. So count markers that were polymorphic in the population and became rare or fixed in the selection, with MAF the minor allele frequency in the selection \((S)\) or in the whole population (pop):

\[A_{\text{lost}} = \#\{k : \text{MAF}^{(S)}_k < 0.05 \;\wedge\; \text{MAF}^{(\text{pop})}_k \ge 0.05\}. \tag{11.6}\]

Table 11.6 counts \(A_{\text{lost}}\) for the five selections.

The redundancy analysis of Chapter 12 does not single alleles_lost out as non-redundant with \(\theta\). That analysis is cached external data: metric_redundancy.csv has no producer in this repository, is not regenerated by any script here, and nothing in this chapter reproduces it. Read from the file, the Spearman correlation with \(\theta\) over its 621 selections is 0.932 for alleles_lost_MAF, -0.925 for Ne_parents and -0.967 for eff_dim. Chapter 12 reports that the metrics keeping most rank variation after regressing on \(\theta\) are allelic richness (the count of alleles retained) and \(N_{e,\text{par}}\), not alleles_lost. On that analysis the rank correlation of alleles_lost with \(\theta\) is 0.932 (\(\rho^2 =\) 0.87), strong but short of a monotone relation, so the case for tracking it rests on the argument above, that a lost rare allele barely moves \(\theta\), and on the result of López-Cortegano et al. (2019) reported in Section 11.5. It does not rest on the redundancy analysis. That analysis does not include F_drift or F_hom, so it says nothing about them; of the metrics in Table 11.18, F_drift separates truncation from random subsets most strongly on this draw.

López-Cortegano et al. (2019) compared, in a subdivided simulated population, strategies maximising expected heterozygosity (\(H_T\), the same family as \(GD\)) against strategies maximising allelic diversity (\(A_T\)). Maximising \(A_T\) retained more alleles and controlled molecular inbreeding better than maximising \(H_T\), except when all the weight of the optimisation is placed on the within-subpopulation component, where maximising \(H_T\) controlled molecular inbreeding best while still retaining fewer alleles. Their markers are multiallelic haplotypes of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP), not the biallelic markers whose minor-allele-frequency threshold defines \(A_{\text{lost}}\), so the result is read here as direction, not as a number to transfer.

The explanation the paper gives depends on the structure of the population. For a single undivided population it cites Fernández et al. (2004), as reported there: maximising heterozygosity leads alleles toward intermediate frequencies, which reduces the chance that rare alleles are lost by drift, so that in undivided populations maximising \(H_T\) can serve both heterozygosity and allele number; in that undivided case the allele-count method kept some rare alleles at low frequencies, where they were more likely to be lost eventually. For subdivided populations it reports instead that maximising global heterozygosity comes at the cost of more homozygosity in each subpopulation, at least in the short term, and of a substantial loss of alleles. This chapter offers no mechanism beyond that report: the step from “frequencies pushed toward 0.5” to a later loss of alleles under heterozygosity management in the subdivided case is not in the paper and is not tested here.

Their recommendation for structured conservation programmes was to make \(A_T\), not \(H_T\), the primary target. In this pipeline’s terms: alleles_lost deserves a place next to \(\theta\) as a tracked outcome, not only as a post-hoc sanity check, whenever the selection feeds several future cycles rather than a single hybrid release.

11.6 Distances: E-NE and A-NE

Odong et al. (2013) proposed, and Core Hunter 3 (De Beukelaer et al. 2018) implements, two measures, entry-to-nearest-entry (E-NE) and accession-to-nearest-entry (A-NE) distances, capturing opposite aspects that \(\theta\) alone cannot separate. Core Hunter 3 computes them on its own marker distances; this book uses \(\eta_{ij} = 1 - f_{ij}\):

\[\text{E-NE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i \in S} \min_{j \in S,\, j \ne i} \eta_{ij}, \qquad \text{A-NE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \min_{j \in S} \eta_{ij}, \tag{11.7}\]

with \(N\) the number of candidates in the population.

  • E-NE measures non-redundancy within the selection. It distinguishes “200 individuals spread out” from “100 near-identical pairs spread out” – configurations that can share the same \(\theta\).
  • A-NE measures representativeness of the population. It detects a selection that optimises \(\theta\) by concentrating in one region of genetic space and ignoring the rest.

Table 11.7 gives both for the five selections of this chapter.

Show code 
fmt(t(sapply(sels, function(s) c(ENE = ene(s, ctx), ANE = ane(s, ctx)))), 4)

# A-NE with the self-distance set to 0 (Odong et al. 2013), for comparison
ane_odong <- vapply(sels, function(s) {
  d <- 1 - ctx[["f"]][, s, drop = FALSE]
  d[cbind(s, seq_along(s))] <- 0
  mean(apply(d, 1, min))
}, numeric(1))
ane_pkg <- vapply(sels, ane, numeric(1), ctx = ctx)
ane_gap <- ane_pkg - ane_odong
stopifnot(all(ane_gap >= 0))

Note the direction: A-NE is a distance to the nearest selected entry, so lower is better for representativeness.

ane() departs from Odong et al. (2013) in one term of Equation 11.7. There an accession that is itself in the selection is its own nearest entry at distance 0; here \(\eta_{ii} = 1 - f_{ii} > 0\) for every heterozygous hybrid, so each selected individual contributes its own self-distance instead of 0. Over these five selections the self-distance-zero version is lower by 0.0174 to 0.0178, and the ranking of the five selections is unchanged. The package keeps its definition so that the z-scores of Table 11.18, computed in this chapter, and the optimisation chapters that call ane() stay on one definition. The cached redundancy analysis of Chapter 12 is external data without a producer in this repository, so this chapter cannot say which definition of A-NE it used.

11.7 Effective dimensionality

Over the eigenvalues \(\gamma_i\) of the double-centered submatrix,

\[D_{\text{eff}} = \frac{(\sum_i \gamma_i)^2}{\sum_i \gamma_i^2} \tag{11.8}\]

has the form of the participation ratio, a term from the physics of localised vibrational modes (the attribution to Bell and Dean (1970) is UNVERIFIED: the paper could not be opened) applied here to eigenvalues. It counts how many independent directions of genetic variation remain: 1 if all variation lies on a single axis, \(n - 1\) if spread evenly over the \(n - 1\) dimensions that double-centering leaves. Table 11.8 gives it for the five selections.

Show code 
fmt(data.frame(D_eff = sapply(sels, eff_dim, ctx = ctx)), 2)

# leading eigenvalue of the raw submatrix against n * theta (Rayleigh bound)
lead_ratio <- sapply(sels, function(s)
  eigen(ctx[["f"]][s, s], symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values[1] /
    (length(s) * theta_group(s, ctx)))
stopifnot(all(lead_ratio >= 1 - 1e-12))

# the uncentered participation ratio of the random subset, against the package value
D_pkg <- eff_dim(sels[["random"]], ctx)
l_unc <- eigen(ctx[["f"]][sels[["random"]], sels[["random"]]],
               symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values
D_unc <- sum(l_unc)^2 / sum(l_unc^2)

The submatrix is double-centered before the eigendecomposition. On a raw all-positive f the leading eigenvalue is at least \(n\theta\), because the Rayleigh quotient of \(\mathbf{f}_S\) at \(\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n}\) is \(\mathbf{1}'\mathbf{f}_S\mathbf{1}/n = n\theta_S\), and for the five selections here it exceeds \(n\theta_S\) by at most 0.017 %. An uncentered version of Equation 11.8 would only re-measure \(\theta\) under a more expensive name – a mistake worth naming because it is easy to make and produces a plausible-looking number.

The two forms are easy to tell apart. On the random subset of this chapter the package’s double-centered \(D_{\text{eff}}\) is 26.19 and the uncentered ratio is 1.47, a factor of 18 apart. The eff_dim column of the cached redundancy analysis (Chapter 12, external data, see above) has mean 1.49 and range 1.44 to 1.51, which is of the order of the uncentered ratio and not of the package value. That cache was built at 12000 markers (Chapter 12) and this chapter runs at 2000, so the comparison crosses scales, and without a producer script it cannot be confirmed which form the cache used. That the cached column is uncentered is therefore a reading of the numbers, not a verified fact, and settling it needs the producer script of that cache.

11.8 Decomposition by heterotic group

The diversity between the two heterotic pools and the diversity within each are different resources. With inbred parents, the complete factorial and flat dominance (\(d_k \equiv 1\)), the divergence term of expected heterosis is GD_BS itself (Chapter 5); with \(d_k\) varying across loci it is the \(d\)-weighted version, which GD_BS does not fix, so divergence is worth having where it lands on the dominance loci and worth nothing where it does not. The diversity within each pool is the fuel for future progress, which Chapter 23 demonstrates. The simulated index of this chapter has no dominance term, so GD_BS below is a component of a partition and not a heterosis measure. Following Caballero and Toro (2002), compute molecular coancestry between lines and weight it by each line’s usage among the selected hybrids:

\[\theta_A = \mathbf{w}_A' \mathbf{f}^{L} \mathbf{w}_A, \qquad \theta_B = \mathbf{w}_B' \mathbf{f}^{L} \mathbf{w}_B, \qquad \theta_{AB} = \mathbf{w}_A' \mathbf{f}^{L} \mathbf{w}_B, \tag{11.9}\]

with \(\mathbf{w}_A\) and \(\mathbf{w}_B\) the usage weights of the pool-A and pool-B lines, each rescaled to sum to 1 within its pool (line_weights(), Appendix A).

Table 11.9 gives the three for the five selections.

Two things to read in Table 11.10. GD_WI is zero to machine precision (largest absolute value across the five selections \(6.7 \times 10^{-16}\), asserted below \(10^{-12}\)): the parent lines are inbred, so there is no within-individual diversity at line level – the algebra of Chapter 3 predicted this, and it is why GD_WI_hyb, the heterozygosity actually carried inside the F1s, is reported separately. And GD_BS rises under truncation, from 0.0374 for the random set to 0.0524.

Table 11.11 compares the three sources. The rise is measured, and the index of this simulation is additive in the two parents (the chunk below asserts that a regression of the index on the parent lines has \(R^2 = 1\), Chapter 6), with no dominance term, so no pair of lines is complementary beyond the sum of its parents’ values and complementarity between lines cannot be the cause. Section 10.7.6 gives the algebra for the same effect on the same kind of selection: with \(\tilde f = (\theta_A + \theta_B)/2\) and \(\bar f = (\theta_A + 2\theta_{AB} + \theta_B)/4\), \(GD_{BS} = \tilde f - \bar f\) (Caballero and Toro (2002)), and concentrating usage inside one pool raises \(\tilde f\) about twice as fast as \(\bar f\) at fixed \(\mathbf{f}^L\), so \(GD_{BS}\) rises without any change in the lines. Truncation’s effective number of lines (ne_lines_pool()) is 4.5 in pool A and 11.8 in pool B, against 17.6 and 16.8 for the random set.

11.8.1 Where this could go next

\(\theta_A\), \(\theta_B\) and \(\theta_{AB}\) as computed here are diagnostic: reported, not fed back into the objective. The subdivided-population literature offers a ready-made way to promote them into a tunable target. Total gene diversity partitions as \(H_T = H_S + D_G\) (Nei 1973) (\(D_G\) is the symbol of López-Cortegano et al. (2019) for the between-subpopulation component, which they take from Nei; Nei’s own symbol was not checked, only his abstract was read), and Caballero and Rodríguez-Ramilo (2010) give the allelic analogue \(A_T = A_S + D_A\). López-Cortegano et al. (2019), managing a simulated population subdivided into five subpopulations, maximise the weighted forms

\[H_\lambda = D_G + \lambda H_S, \qquad A_\lambda = D_A + \lambda A_S, \tag{11.10}\]

where \(H_S\) and \(A_S\) are the within-subpopulation components, \(D_G\) and \(D_A\) the between-subpopulation components, and \(\lambda \in [0, \infty)\) is a weight chosen by the manager. The subscript \(\lambda\) marks this as the weighted objective, distinct from Nei’s unweighted total \(H_T = H_S + D_G\): the two are equal only at \(\lambda = 1\). Reading the two subpopulations as the two heterotic pools is this book’s analogy, not theirs. \(\lambda = 0\) optimises only between-pool divergence – protects the divergence term of heterosis, ignores each pool’s internal health. \(\lambda \to \infty\) optimises only within-pool diversity – protects each pool’s future options, ignores the cross.

That maps directly onto the two constraints a maize breeder already has in mind but applies only qualitatively: don’t blend the pools versus don’t paint either pool into a corner. Turning \(\lambda\) into a second explicit knob alongside \(\alpha_{\max}\) is the natural next step if pool erosion shows up in practice. The current implementation reports the ingredients and leaves the weighting to the analyst.

\(\lambda\) need not stay a matter of taste when the objective is expected heterosis. Take inbred parents, two pools with equal weight, which the A \(\times\) B complete factorial imposes, and per locus \(H_{S,k} = p_{Ak}q_{Ak} + p_{Bk}q_{Bk}\), \(H_{T,k} = 2\bar p_k(1 - \bar p_k)\) and \(D_{G,k} = H_{T,k} - H_{S,k} = y_k^2/2\), with \(\bar p_k\) and \(y_k\) as in Equation 5.5. Averaged over loci, \(H_S = m^{-1}\sum_k H_{S,k}\) and \(D_G = m^{-1}\sum_k D_{G,k}\), as in the partition of Chapter 3. By Equation 5.5 the expected heterozygosity of the A \(\times\) B crosses at locus \(k\) is \(H_{T,k} + D_{G,k}\), and since \(H_{T,k} = H_{S,k} + D_{G,k}\) it is also

\[p_{Ak} + p_{Bk} - 2p_{Ak}p_{Bk} \;=\; H_{T,k} + D_{G,k} \;=\; H_{S,k} + 2D_{G,k}. \tag{11.11}\]

The shared term of Equation 5.5 is \(H_{T,k}\), not \(H_{S,k}\): it already contains one \(D_{G,k}\). With \(d_k \equiv 1\) the expected heterosis, averaged over loci, is \(H_S + 2D_G = 2(D_G + H_S/2)\), twice \(H_\lambda\) of Equation 11.10 with \(\lambda = 1/2\) fixed by the algebra. With dominance effects \(d_k\) (Chapter 5), \(m^{-1}\sum_k d_k(H_{S,k} + 2D_{G,k}) = 2\bar d_D\,(D_G + \lambda_d H_S)\), where

\[\lambda_d = \frac{\bar d_H}{2\,\bar d_D}, \qquad \bar d_H = \frac{\sum_k d_k H_{S,k}}{\sum_k H_{S,k}}, \qquad \bar d_D = \frac{\sum_k d_k D_{G,k}}{\sum_k D_{G,k}} \tag{11.12}\]

are the \(H_S\)-weighted and \(D_G\)-weighted means of \(d\). Reading \(\lambda_d\) as a maximisation weight, \(\lambda_d \in [0, \infty)\), needs net directional dominance on the two weighted loci, \(\bar d_D > 0\) and \(\bar d_H \ge 0\): if \(\bar d_D \le 0\) the leading factor \(2\bar d_D\) reverses the objective (maximising expected heterosis would then minimise the bracket, or leave \(\lambda_d\) undefined at \(\bar d_D = 0\)), and \(\bar d_H \ge 0\) is what keeps \(\lambda_d\) non-negative. \(\lambda_d\) departs from \(1/2\) only when dominance lines up differently with within-pool heterozygosity than with between-pool divergence, and a frequency-only partition cannot see that because \(d\) does not appear in it. \(\lambda_d\) is computed from a plan’s own frequencies, so it is a local weight rather than a constant of the population, and it is the right weight only for expected heterosis as the objective: López-Cortegano et al. (2019) run \(\lambda = 1\) mainly and also \(\lambda = 0\), \(0.5\) and \(1000\) (p. 3); they take \(0.5\) from Bennewitz and Meuwissen (2006), as a value suggested to maximise the total genetic variance of a hypothetical quantitative trait (as reported there; that paper was not read here). The weight \(1/2\) is also the \(\lambda_d\) of flat dominance, in the same form \(D_G + \lambda H_S\), but the two arguments differ (the variance of a quantitative trait there, the expected heterozygosity of the F1 here) and whether they are related is not examined. In the partition of Chapter 3, \(H_T = GD_T\), \(D_G = GD_{BS}\) and \(H_S = GD_T - GD_{BS} = GD_{WI} + GD_{BI} = GD_{WS}\); Equation 11.11 is then \(1 - \theta_{AB} = GD_T + GD_{BS}\), Equation 5.6, which Chapter 5 checks on the full factorial and which the chunk below asserts on the five selections (Table 11.13), together with the per-locus algebra on random frequencies. \(1 - \theta_{AB}\) is the expected heterozygosity of the complete factorial of the selected lines at their usage weights. It is not the heterozygosity of the pairs actually kept, \(2\,GD_{WI\text{-}hyb}\) (the F1_het of Table 10.4), which the table gives in its last column.

Equation 11.12 is an identity, not a definition, so the chunk below asserts it (Table 11.14). The pool frequencies are those of the selected lines at their usage weights (line_weights(), simulate_lines()), and the dominance effects \(d_k\) are drawn from a gamma law, so they vary across loci; with \(d_k \equiv 1\), \(\lambda_d = 1/2\) exactly. The identity also equals the closed form heterosis_expected() divided by \(m\).

Show code 
Lh <- simulate_lines(book_cfg) / 2           # lines, 0/1; same draw as ctx
lambda_d_check <- function(s, d) {
  w <- line_weights(s, ctx)
  pA_ <- drop(w$A %*% Lh); pB_ <- drop(w$B %*% Lh)
  HS_ <- pA_ * (1 - pA_) + pB_ * (1 - pB_); DG_ <- (pA_ - pB_)^2 / 2
  dH <- sum(d * HS_) / sum(HS_); dD <- sum(d * DG_) / sum(DG_)
  lam <- dH / (2 * dD)
  lhs <- mean(d * (HS_ + 2 * DG_))
  rhs <- 2 * dD * (mean(DG_) + lam * mean(HS_))
  stopifnot(dD > 0, dH >= 0,                 # the conditions stated above
            isTRUE(all.equal(lhs, rhs, tolerance = 1e-12)),
            isTRUE(all.equal(lhs, heterosis_expected(pA_, pB_, d) / length(d),
                             tolerance = 1e-12)))
  c(lambda_d = lam, relative_error = abs(lhs - rhs) / lhs)
}
set.seed(8)
d_k <- stats::rgamma(ctx[["m"]], shape = 2, rate = 4)
lam_flat <- t(sapply(sels, lambda_d_check, d = rep(1, ctx[["m"]])))
stopifnot(all(abs(lam_flat[, "lambda_d"] - 1/2) < 1e-12))
lam_var <- t(sapply(sels, lambda_d_check, d = d_k))
fmt(cbind(lambda_d_flat = lam_flat[, "lambda_d"], lambda_d_gamma = lam_var[, "lambda_d"],
          relative_error = lam_var[, "relative_error"]), 4)

With flat \(d\) the five selections give \(\lambda_d = 1/2\) (asserted to 1e-12); with the gamma \(d_k\) (seed 8) \(\lambda_d\) ranges from 0.505 to 0.512, and the identity holds to a relative error of at most \(3.8 \times 10^{-15}\) (one draw of \(d_k\)).

11.9 The correct baseline

11.9.1 Why not compare against the whole population

The natural comparison – coancestry of the selected set against that of the whole population – is biased by construction. Applying Equation 11.2 to both:

\[\mathbb{E}[\theta_S] - \theta_N = \left(\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{N}\right)\left(\bar f_d - \bar f_o\right) > 0, \tag{11.13}\]

where \(\theta_N\) is Equation 11.1 with \(S\) the whole population of \(N\) candidates, the expectation is over random subsets \(S\) of size \(n\) drawn without replacement, and \(\bar f_d\), \(\bar f_o\) are the population’s diagonal and off-diagonal means; the inequality holds since \(\bar f_d > \bar f_o\) whenever individuals are not clones. For a given, non-random \(S\) the means of Equation 11.2 are the set’s own and the difference can take either sign: the max-diversity greedy of this chapter has \(\theta_S =\) 0.66976 against \(\theta_N =\) 0.67191. A random sample already looks less diverse than the population it came from, with no selection whatsoever. The bias is pure sampling and grows as the sample shrinks. Table 11.15 compares the prediction with the mean of 400 random subsets.

Show code 
fd <- mean(diag(ctx[["f"]])); fo <- mean(ctx[["f"]][upper.tri(ctx[["f"]])])
predicted_bias <- (1 / n_sel - 1 / ctx[["N"]]) * (fd - fo)

set.seed(11)
th <- replicate(400, theta_group(sample.int(ctx[["N"]], n_sel), ctx))
observed_bias <- mean(th) - theta_group(seq_len(ctx[["N"]]), ctx)
mc_se <- sd(th) / sqrt(length(th))
stopifnot(abs(observed_bias - predicted_bias) < 4 * mc_se)

data.frame(quantity = c("predicted", "observed", "mc_se"),
           value = formatC(c(predicted_bias, observed_bias, mc_se),
                           digits = 4, format = "g"))

Equation 11.13 is exact in expectation. The observed residual is 1.32 Monte Carlo standard errors of the mean over 400 subsets, and the chunk asserts that it stays within 4.

Note also that the off-diagonal mean of a random subset is an unbiased estimator of the population’s off-diagonal mean. So the metric many programmes already use is free of this particular bias – but it is not \(\theta_S\). By Equation 11.2 it omits the term \(\bar f_d/n\), the part of the set’s coancestry that depends on \(n\) and is real when the set is used as a crossing pool, so \(1/(2\bar f_o)\) is not the \(N_s\) of the set and the gap between the two grows as \(n\) shrinks.

11.9.2 Operational definition of loss

The “0 % loss” reference is the distribution of random subsets of the same size:

\[\alpha \;=\; \frac{GD_{\text{ref}} - GD_S}{GD_{\text{ref}}}. \tag{11.14}\]

Here \(GD_{\text{ref}}\) (gd_ref in the code) is the mean \(GD\) of random subsets of size \(n\). \(\alpha = 0\) means “as diverse as a random sample of the same size”; \(\alpha < 0\) means more diverse than chance. Resampling also delivers the standard deviation, and hence a z-score for any strategy. Table 11.16 gives the baseline of this chapter’s pool.

11.9.3 How to set the \(\alpha\) limit

The acceptable limit should not be chosen a priori. Pure truncation – the unconstrained optimum of the index – gives the \(\alpha\) (Equation 11.14) at which the constraint stops binding. This is not the largest \(\alpha\) over all subsets, which a minimum-diversity selection would reach; it is the largest \(\alpha\) a gain-maximising selection ever needs. If it is below your intended limit, the constraint restricts nothing and you have bought a false sense of control.

Show code 
# values used inline in the next paragraph
attainable <- alpha_loss(sel_truncation(ctx, n_sel), ctx, null$gd_ref)

At this book’s teaching scale (25 + 25 lines, 2000 markers, \(n =\) 60) truncation reaches \(\alpha =\) 4.13 %, so a 5 % limit would be inoperative. The value at the package’s production scale (71 + 71 lines, 25000 markers, as in A8 of Section 10.1) and at the sim_config default (50 + 50 lines, 5000 markers) is not computed in this chapter, and this one does not transfer to either. The correct procedure is to trace the \(\alpha\)-versus-gain frontier and choose its knee: Chapter 15 shows the frontier of the greedy over the merit weight and Chapter 16 gives the recipe.
