21  What the plant literature establishes

Chapter 12 derived a recommendation from the book’s own simulations. This chapter, the first of the Evidence and practice part, asks what the applied literature reports about the same quantities: where it agrees, and where it is silent. It follows the Mating and testing design part (Chapter 18, Chapter 19, Chapter 20) without using it. The corpus (Section 21.1) was screened for diversity metrics, heterotic structure, genetic resources and optimisation (Appendix D), not for crossing or testing design, so nothing here bears on the literature of that part and no result of it is carried over.

21.1 The corpus, and what it can support

Two cached files, both external literature screens with no producer in this repository (book/scripts/regenerate.R lists them as not regenerated here). Nothing computed from them is reproducible beyond what the shipped CSV contains. Each number in this chapter says which file it comes from.

  • The plant file, bibliography_plant.csv: 687 screened records, 2017-2026. relevance is 3 for the 158 directly relevant records and 2 for the 529 related-background records; 197 concern maize.
  • The animal file, bibliography_animal.csv: 324 records, 2018-2026.

The files are partly misfiled. The animal file holds plant studies (for example Yadav et al. (2025), wheat), and the plant file holds a record whose crop field reads “dairy cattle”. “In the plant file” and “in the animal file” in this chapter therefore describe a file, not a species. Appendix D describes both.

Both files carry one-line curator notes, not abstracts. The study-level numbers of this chapter are read from the abstracts of the papers cited by key, except that Kadoumi et al. (2025) and the final remark on Zhao et al. (2023) are read from the full text; they are not in the CSVs and were not recomputed. A sentence “not established in the retrieved set” is a statement about these two files, whose windows begin in 2017 (plant) and 2018 (animal) and so exclude earlier work such as Heerwaarden et al. (2012).

Two terms with more than one sense. Allelic richness is used in this chapter in four senses: \(A_{\text{ret}}\), the marker alleles retained by a set of inbred lines at biallelic markers (Equation 11.7, the book’s sense in Chapter 12 and Chapter 13); the figure a study reports under that name without saying how it is computed; the usage table’s merged category “Allelic richness / rare alleles”; and the expected number of alleles per locus at drift-mutation equilibrium at multiallelic loci. Each use says which. Status number in the literature is a count; on the book’s molecular \(\mathbf{f}\), \(N_s = 1/(2\theta) \in [1/2, 1]\) is a rescaling of \(\theta\) and not a head count (Section 11.3).

21.2 The headline qualification

Section 12.6 recommends, inside the homozygosity lens, for inbred parents and an index additive in the parents: two metrics inside the optimiser, the proportional loss \(\alpha\) of gene diversity against a same-size random-subset baseline (Chapter 15) and \(N_{e,\text{par}}\); three at validation, marker alleles retained \(A_{\text{ret}}\), the within/between-pool decomposition and \(N_s\); and \(F_{drift}\) and \(F_{hom}\) as monitoring against the random-subset null. That set was chosen by cost and by a residual criterion that Section 12.3 states is necessary and not sufficient. It is not claimed to be minimal, and the redundancy analysis behind it is cached external data with an unrecorded setting. This chapter compares those metrics with what the plant file reports.

On the screen’s own table (Table 21.1), the quantities from which the recommended metrics are built are reported unevenly, and none of the recommended metrics themselves has a category. The table is an external classification, which limits how far the statement can be pressed. The mapping of the recommended metrics to the table’s categories, given in this section, is this chapter’s, not the screen’s, and each pair is approximate.

  • \(\alpha\) is the proportional loss of \(GD = 1 - \theta\) against a random-subset baseline (Chapter 15). Its inner quantity, \(GD\), is Nei’s \(H_e\) computed on the group’s own frequencies (Section 11.2), so it maps to the category “Expected heterozygosity (He / gene diversity)”. The loss relative to a baseline has no category. \(\theta\) itself is a mean of the molecular coancestry \(\mathbf{f}\) over a usage vector, and only loosely the category “Marker coancestry / kinship / relatedness”, which holds pairwise kinship and relatedness of any kind.
  • \(N_{e,\text{par}}\), an effective number of parents of a usage vector, is nearest to “Effective population size / status number”, a different quantity.
  • \(A_{\text{ret}}\) is nearest to “Allelic richness / rare alleles”, the merged category of Section 21.1, not necessarily the same quantity.

Table 21.1 is plant_metric_usage.csv. Its percentages divide by the 687 screened records, 529 of which are background records, not by the 158 directly relevant ones; the maize column counts maize records, of which there are 197. The repository records neither how the categories were defined nor how records were assigned to them. The most likely source is the abstracts, which the CSVs do not hold, but that is an inference. The table is drawn in Figure D.2 (Appendix D).

Table 21.2 gives smaller counts than the usage table in every row. In the first four rows the table is 2.2 to 3.8 times the column; the column has 0 records with runs of homozygosity (ROH) and 1 effective-population-size record (1 maize) against 22 and 17. The gene-diversity pattern (row 7) finds 77 records against 101. The effective-size pattern does not contain the bare token “Ne”: the shipped column lists “effective alleles” in 7 records (2 of them as “(Ne)”), a per-locus allele count and not an effective population size, and the chunk of Table 21.2 asserts that none of them is matched. The shipped column lists coancestry, kinship or relatedness in 12 records (7 maize) where the usage table has 46 (22), and 0 records with runs of homozygosity where the table’s merged “Inbreeding coefficient / ROH” row has 22 (5 maize). The metric_used field is empty in 61 of the 687 records. The ROH query printed in Appendix D is the pattern of Table 21.2, anchored as a word, and returns 0 records. Unanchored, a case-insensitive “roh” returns 1 record, which matches the letters “roh” inside the word “microhaplotypes”, in an alfalfa record. The magnitudes of the usage table therefore cannot be reproduced from the shipped file, and I give them only as the curator’s classification. What the two sources share, among these six categories, is the order at the two ends: population structure and \(F_{ST}\) are well ahead of the other four in both, and runs of homozygosity and effective size are the two lowest in both. Allelic richness and coancestry tie in the table and are 18 against 12 records in the column counts. Expected heterozygosity is not in that order claim: it is third in the table and 77 records in the column. The statements in this chapter that rest on the usage table rest on the order, and not on the percentages.

Three observations follow.

Applied plant work often reports several metrics together. A panel of 376 elite sub-Saharan African inbreds reports, in one analysis, polymorphic information content (PIC) = 0.39, observed heterozygosity = 0.02, gene diversity = 0.37, minor allele frequency (MAF) = 0.29, Shannon = 6.86, Simpson = 949.09, allelic richness = 787.70 (the abstract does not say how it is computed) and an average genetic distance of 0.303 (Mukiti et al. 2025). The plant file’s annotation of that record lists “Rogers distance”; the abstract says “average genetic distance” and clusters on the identical-by-state (IBS) distance, so I write distance and not Rogers. The shipped metric_used column lists three or more items for 49% of the 687 screened records (44% of the 158 directly relevant ones), counting comma- or semicolon-separated annotation items. This is a count of annotation items, not a bound on how often several metric families are reported together: some items hold more than one family (splitting also on “/” and ” and ” gives 54% and 51%), and many items are not diversity metrics (“genetic gain”, “breeding value”, “QTL effects”). I did not define families.

The quantity inside \(\alpha\) is among the most reported; the other recommended metrics are not identified as categories of the screen. On the usage table (external classification), expected heterozygosity or gene diversity appears in 14.7% of screened records, rank 3 of 12; marker coancestry or kinship in 6.7%, allelic richness or rare alleles in 6.7%, and effective size or status number in 2.5%; against 44.1% for population structure, 17% for \(F_{ST}\) and 14.1% for PIC. By record count the coancestry and allelic-richness categories tie at rank 6, in the middle, and effective size ranks 12, the last. Under the mapping of Section 21.2, then, gene diversity itself has a category, and the baseline-relative loss and the effective number of parents are not identified as categories in the screen; \(A_{\text{ret}}\) maps only to the merged richness category, in the middle by record count. The absence of a category is not a measure of how often the papers report a quantity, which a screen of abstracts does not show. No category of the table corresponds to a proportional loss against a resampling baseline, the quantity \(\alpha\) constrains, or to an effective number of parents of a usage vector. The most reported categories describe structure. A screen of abstracts cannot see a constraint applied inside a selection programme, so “toward constraining” is not testable from it.

Nothing in either file computes \(\theta\) or \(N_s\) on a maize hybrid-usage vector. The formalism of Section 12.1 is not established in the retrieved set. That is a gap this work fills rather than a contradiction, but it means the approach has no published plant-breeding baseline to be validated against, which is a real limitation.

21.3 Why plant practice differs from animal breeding

Three structural facts separate the two literatures. They are reasons the animal methods do not transfer unchanged (Section 1.3), not a tested explanation of the metric choices in Table 21.1.

Lines are homozygous. Observed heterozygosity in an inbred panel is the residual heterozygosity of the lines, a measure of how far they are from fixation and not a descriptor of diversity: pearl millet parental lines report \(H_o = 0.031\) against \(H_e = 0.334\) (Kandarkar et al. 2024). Reading it as a purity control is this chapter’s, not the paper’s. Animal coancestry theory assumes a randomly mating heterozygous population; the plant analogue computes \(\theta\) directly on line genotypes, which is the setting where the line-level collapse of Section 12.1 applies, for inbred parents and an index additive in the parents.

The product is the F1, not the line. Diversity is tracked separately for each parental pool and for cross-pool complementarity, a structure with no livestock analogue. Association models are now built to partition additive and dominance effects by the heterotic group of origin of the allele (Beugnot et al. 2023).

Diversity is partitioned into two objects, not one. Chapter 3 gives the partition of gene diversity into within-pool and between-pool terms, and Section 1.1 derives that under drift alone in two closed pools of equal weight the within-pool term falls while the between-pool term rises. That derivation does not cover selection; the rise of \(GD_{BS}\) under run_cycles() is a simulation result (Chapter 23) with drift and selection acting together. A single pool-wide statistic sees the sum, which is why the book partitions it.

21.3.1 Distance measures, and the absence of a standard

The usage table’s row “Modified Rogers / genetic distance” has 83 records over all crops, of which 36 are maize. It is a merged category covering any genetic distance, not a share of modified Rogers distance (MRD) among the distance measures: the string “Rogers” appears in 6 metric_used cells of the plant file (5 maize). A 2025 methods paper is annotated in the plant file as a unified framework relating diversity metrics to distance (Cendoya et al. 2025); I have not read it beyond that note.

For inbred lines coded 0/1, \(f_{ij} = 1 - \text{MRD}_{ij}^2\), and the book checks it numerically to \(10^{-12}\) on simulated lines (Trap 1 of Chapter 13). It is not claimed for hybrids, and a reported MRD equals \(1 - f\) only for inbred lines on the same markers coded 0/1.

One result is worth carrying into practice. In 298 African highland maize inbreds, nearly 91% of pairs differ at 30-36% of the scored alleles, but only 32% have a relative kinship coefficient below 0.050; the authors read the gap as redundancy in allelic composition from repeated use of few source germplasm backgrounds (Wegary et al. 2019).

That is the study’s result. The following is an inference, and it limits what the result supports. On 0/1 inbred lines a pair differing at a fraction \(d\) of loci has \(f = 1 - d\), so if “scored alleles” means loci that differ, 91% of these pairs sit at \(f\) between 0.64 and 0.70 on the book’s definition. The “relative kinship coefficient” is on another scale, presumably relative to the sample, with unrelated pairs near zero; the abstract gives no estimator and the definition is in no versioned file. The two figures are therefore not a contrast between the book’s \(f\) and a distance: on inbred 0/1 lines \(f\) is a monotone function of the distance. Whether a reading from state and a reading relative to the sample disagree about redundancy is this chapter’s inference, not the study’s: the abstract gives no threshold on either scale at which a pair counts as redundant, compares the two figures nowhere, and the authors read both as evidence of redundancy. The book’s constraint is on state-based \(f\), which is the first reading. Whether \(\alpha\), \(N_{e,\text{par}}\) and \(A_{\text{ret}}\) would flag a panel like this one is not tested here, and the study is not an argument for a coancestry constraint over a distance constraint.

Other calibration benchmarks, all from abstracts: 329 North American ex-PVP (expired Plant Variety Protection) dent inbreds resolve into eight admixture subgroups reflecting heterotic patterns and company of origin (White et al. 2020); in China summer maize (490 inbreds, 525,141 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)), 66.05% of pairs show relative kinship near zero (Shu et al. 2021); sweet corn shows lower diversity, PIC and MAF than field corn across 514 versus 181 lines (Yang et al. 2024).

21.4 Heterotic groups

21.4.1 Assignment is not a solved problem

Records that compare ways of assigning lines to groups report different winners. In 41 early-maturing yellow inbreds crossed to five testers, the HSGCA method (heterotic-group-specific and general combining ability) was more effective than the alternatives compared, which the abstract does not list (Annor et al. 2020). In a 19-line diallel, crosses between groups formed on simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers linked to yield-contributing traits showed no superiority, whereas inter-group crosses formed on random SSRs were superior to intra-group crosses (Kumar et al. 2022). In the 376-line panel of Mukiti et al. (2025), the IBS distance with average-linkage clustering gave the highest cophenetic correlation among the methods compared and yielded three groups (Mukiti et al. 2025); a cophenetic correlation measures the fit of a tree to a distance matrix, not agreement with testcross performance. These are three records on panels of 19 to 376 lines with different criteria. They do not show a consistent winner, but I did not tabulate winners across the plant file, so “no consistent winner” is not a finding of the corpus.

This matters for the pipeline: the line-level collapse assumes pool structure is given. Group assignment is itself contested, so the pool-balance constraint inherits whatever uncertainty the assignment method carries. Treat it as an uncertain input, not a fixed given.

21.4.2 A longitudinal programme, and what it can and cannot show

Kadoumi et al. (2025) analysed forty years of breeding data and about 84,000 genotypes from one private European dent maize programme. Their abstract states that differentiation between Stiff Stalk (SS) and Non-Stiff Stalk (NSS) groups increased significantly over time while genetic diversity within both groups declined. Their results are narrower. \(F_{ST}\) between the groups rose from 0.178 to 0.223. \(H_e\) fell from 0.371 to 0.279 in NSS, while \(H_e\) of SS remained stable (0.278 to 0.281), but in the full text the proportion of SS markers with \(\text{MAF} < 0.05\) “also increased by 20.76 %, reaching 22.3 % of SNPs in era 4”, so the stability of \(H_e\) in SS does not mean an unchanged allele-frequency spectrum. The abstract states that marker fixation and linkage disequilibrium increased; I did not locate an SS-specific linkage-disequilibrium result and claim none. The mean between-group Hamming distance fell slightly, from 0.430 to 0.420. The authors attribute the main germplasm-wide loss to the merging and homogenisation of the Iodent and Lancaster groups into NSS, so NSS was not a closed pool. They call the changes “not critical” and say they “only suggest that increased caution in diversity management is now needed”. They write that, without phenotypic information, they “can only speculate” that selection caused the rise in divergence. Before the genomic-selection era (eras 0-2) the dataset holds only the inbreds that fed the modern programme, a survivor sample whose bias in the early diversity estimates they call uncertain in order of magnitude and direction. They recommend enhancing group divergence while preserving diversity across selection cycles. Section 1.1 gives the same numbers with their limits; Heerwaarden et al. (2012), outside this corpus’s window, report increasing separation of breeding pools across the history of North American maize lines and attribute little of the genome-wide pattern to selection.

This is one company’s programme, observed, with a merger and a survivor sample, and no counterfactual. It shows that falling diversity in one group and rising \(F_{ST}\) occurred together in a commercial programme. It does not show that a constraint is needed, and it does not isolate drift or selection as the cause. The between-group Hamming distance, the quantity nearest the hybrid’s heterozygosity (Section 1.1), fell while \(F_{ST}\) rose, and it is not a column of the panel s1_monitor() logs (Table 13.3). The paper is one programme with both movements, not a test of that panel (Chapter 13).

What the plant literature adds on why divergence is held, not maximised, is narrower than the book’s argument. Beugnot et al. (2023), in a Dent-by-Flint factorial and a diallel of admixed lines, report additive and dominance quantitative trait locus (QTL) effects for hybrid performance that are background-specific, depending on the heterotic group of origin of the allele, and say that group complementarity and differentiation increase over time. That supports background-specific effects. That divergence is not a bonus is the book’s argument: under drift, with a dominance coefficient drawn independently of the frequencies, expected heterosis is invariant to the divergence of the pools (\(F_{BN}\)), and under selection what happens to it can go either way (Section 5.8, Section 5.9). That is Trap 3 of Chapter 13: the prescription agrees, by a different mechanism from the one the QTL result suggests.

Crossing across the heterotic boundary for germplasm enhancement generated usable variation: in a connected population from four heterotic groups, 10.8% of the hybrids of one population (POP5/C229) exceeded the high-yielding check ND678 (Cheng et al. 2025). One population and one check bear on pre-breeding; they do not bear on whether the commercial hybrid-parent pools should be crossed. My reading is that the boundary is maintained for hybrid-parent pools and porous for pre-breeding.

21.5 Selecting within the pool

21.5.1 The constraint alone is not sufficient

Optimal cross selection under a diversity constraint is simulated in a crop in a two-part programme with rapid recurrent genomic selection by Gorjanc et al. (2018) (animal file). In a simulation of a global programme with bridging and elite components, Sanchez et al. (2023) conclude that, with realistic plant breeding population sizes, diversity depletion in closed programmes “appears ineluctable”, calling for the introduction of relevant diversity donors; bridging populations made by crossing genetic resources to elite lines, taking 25% of experimental resources, appear highly beneficial in their simulations, with optimal cross selection maintaining a constant diversity to incorporate improved donors.

Inference, from one simulation under its assumptions: a constraint on the existing pool can slow the loss of diversity but may not stop it. Plan periodic external injections rather than treating the line pool as closed. Chapter 23 simulates an injection in the book’s own setting; that is not an operating programme.

21.5.2 Cycle length, and what goes unreported

Shortening cycles is reported with gain-rate benefits in simulation and in one breeding programme:

  • In a simulation of a sorghum programme in Haiti, genomic-assisted recurrent selection at two or three cycles a year had a maximum relative genetic gain advantage over phenotypic recurrent selection at one cycle a year of 12-88% for an oligogenic architecture and 26-165% for a polygenic one, observed for the oligogenic case only in the first few cycles. Prediction accuracy declined substantially after several cycles, and for small populations and oligogenic traits the cost per unit gain was lower under phenotypic selection (Muleta et al. 2019).
  • In intermediate wheatgrass, genomic selection from 2017 cut the generation time from 2-5 years to one cycle a year. About 100 of about 4,000 seedlings were chosen as parents on their genomic estimated breeding value (GEBV); the genomic relationship matrix was used to represent genetic diversity in the 1,000-1,200-genet validation populations that train the model, not in parent selection (Crain et al. 2021).
  • In simulation, two-cycle genomewide selection in maize gave 124-178% of the largest gain from phenotypic selection at the same cost and time; the abstract reports no diversity metric (Bernardo 2021).

The diversity cost per unit of cycle-time reduction is not established in the plant file. It is addressed only in part elsewhere. Gorjanc et al. (2018) (animal file) simulates optimal cross selection under rapid recurrent genomic selection, and Chapter 22 measures the relative loss of gene diversity from year 1 to year 15 in paired simulated runs with genomic selection switched on in year 6, in an inbred-line, wheat-type programme, and reports the mean cycle length of each arm beside it; that is a stock after fifteen years, not a cost per unit of cycle-time reduction, which Chapter 22 does not compute. Neither is a hybrid maize programme, so the item stays open as worded.

Simulation of reciprocal recurrent selection in hybrid rice finds that larger population sizes give the highest rates of improvement and the smallest decrease in additive genetic variance due to drift (Fritsche-Neto et al. 2023). That study predicts single-cross performance rather than shortening cycles, and its abstract says nothing about how diversity is managed.

21.5.3 What is hybrid-specific, and what is not

This distinction decides which literature transfers. Of the records the screen scored, every usefulness-criterion and progeny-variance study I found operates upstream, at inbred-line development: usefulness criterion with parental contributions for multi-parental crosses (Allier et al. 2019), barley validation against realised populations (Neyhart and Smith 2019), soybean family mean and variance prediction (Wartha and Lorenz 2024), wheat cross-progeny variance (Oget-Ebrad et al. 2024). None of them, in the abstracts and notes, operates on F1 hybrids of fixed inbreds. That is a negative on abstracts, and a paper that does so without saying it in the abstract is invisible to it.

That fits the book’s decision to drop the progeny-variance term for hybrids: all F1 hybrids of one pair of fixed inbreds are genetically identical, so there is no within-cross variance to predict after fixation, and the term is real only before it. It is why usefulness_criterion() belongs at S2 and nowhere else (Chapter 13).

One caution is hybrid-specific, and it concerns the mean, not diversity. Where dominance is present, the expected value of an F1 is not the mean of its parents’ additive values, and an additive-only index misses the specific combining ability (SCA) part (Section 5.11); the index of Chapter 14 is additive by construction, with no SCA. Two consequences are the book’s, not the corpus’s. The diversity side of the collapse (Section 12.1) holds whatever the index, but the mean index is a function of the line-usage vector only if it is additive in the parents. And the line merit of the rounded route of Chapter 17 is a general combining ability (GCA) against the opposite pool, a property of the line against that tester pool. So line-usage optimisation on \(\alpha\) should not be conflated with mean-performance ranking of the same crosses. I attach no source to the claim that hybrid-mean prediction gains from additive-plus-dominance models: the plant file holds records on hybrid prediction with dominance modelled (for example 10.3835/plantgenome2018.10.0082), which I have not read to a conclusion, and the book fits no dominance model in prediction.

21.6 Genebanks, core collections and introgression

Core-subset construction converges on a shared objective, maximising retained diversity at minimum accession count, but diverges on method: GenoCore selecting 310 from 3,333 sorghum accessions (Mufumbo et al. 2022); rice cores of 247 (core) and 30 from 2,242 accessions (Choudhury et al. 2021); a lettuce core of 268 from 811 accessions capturing 99.4% of total variation (Huo et al. 2025); cassava core collections built by two strategies (two plant-file records).

The quantitatively strongest result: in Arabidopsis, Populus and sorghum panels, marker-based subsets captured 3-78-fold more haplotypic diversity than subsets from geographic or environmental data (Reeves et al. 2020). For cores assembled in those settings, assemble them on markers.

Maize-specific core-collection algorithm choice is not established in the retrieved set. Maize genetic-resource work in it is oriented toward bridging and targeted introgression instead:

  • DNA-pool genotyping of 156 landraces (2,340 individuals) against 327 elite lines spanning the 20th century gave two indicators of a landrace’s genomic contribution to elite germplasm (Arca et al. 2023). The abstract reports that lines derived from landraces with limited contributions enriched the haplotype diversity of the reference lines more than those from high-contribution landraces.
  • Haplotype mining in about 1,000 landrace-derived doubled haploids (DH) found landrace haplotypes outperforming breeding-line alternatives, stable across populations and environments (Mayer et al. 2020).
  • Rapid-cycling genomic selection within a landrace population (899 DH lines for training, 600K array) improves donor material before it contacts the elite pool; the abstract also reports that prediction accuracies declined across cycles (Polzer et al. 2025).
  • For capturing landrace variation, the doubled-haploid route beats gamete capture in prediction accuracy (Hölker et al. 2022).

A \(\theta\)- or \(N_s\)-based accounting of introgression cost against a same-size resampling baseline is not established in the retrieved set.

21.7 Matrix choice: the animal file

The animal file tags 163 records with the theme “Coancestry / relationship matrix choice”. In the records read for this chapter the optimal-contribution selection (OCS, Chapter 15) or optimal-cross framework is common ground and the matrix placed inside it is not settled. The records differ in species, criterion and design:

  • Drift-, homozygosity- and identity-by-descent (IBD)-based inbreeding could differ substantially in genomic OCS unless diversity management was based upon IBD , Chapter 10.
  • A deviation-based coancestry matrix retained more genetic variability, while VanRaden’s kept allele frequencies closer to those of the base population (Morales-González et al. 2021). The criteria differ, so this is a trade-off between variability kept and frequency change.
  • A linkage-and-pedigree-informed matrix had the highest correlation with the true IBD, the most accurate rate of inbreeding \(\Delta F\), and was the only scheme to stay below the inbreeding target while minimising frequency change at neutral loci (Wæge et al. 2026).
  • In pigs, genomic mating limited inbreeding accumulation better than other mating approaches at the same expected gain, and ROH-based relatedness in genomic mating gave faster gain than SNP-based relatedness (Zhao et al. 2023). That is a mate-allocation study, not OCS, and in the full text the authors saw no trend using ROH-based inbreeding coefficients to assess the inbreeding trend.

These records do not rank the matrices, and this book relies on no ranking (Section 12.6, caveats): a table of Chapter 10 reports that in the nine schemes of Meuwissen et al. (2020) the homozygosity-based matrix, to which the book’s \(\mathbf{f}\) is affine, misses the drift target by the widest margin. The line-level collapse of Section 12.1 is algebra for any symmetric line-level \(\mathbf{f}^L\), given that the hybrid-level matrix is defined by Equation 12.1; whether that definition is right for a centred VanRaden matrix is not established there, and managing on molecular coancestry controls homozygosity, not drift. So the open choice of matrix does not change the cost and identity results of Chapter 12 and does change what the constraint controls.

On allelic variation, Allendorf et al. (2024) write about the expected number of alleles per locus at drift-mutation equilibrium in a species-conservation setting, which is the fourth sense of allelic richness (Section 21.1) and not \(A_{\text{ret}}\). They report that allelic variation is much more sensitive to bottlenecks than heterozygosity, that the usual adjustments of \(N_e\) for departures from the ideal population do not predict the loss of allelic variation well, that the expected number of alleles at equilibrium is linearly related to the reduction of the census size, and they recommend extending the guideline of long-term \(N_e > 500\) to allelic variation. Those results concern multiallelic loci. The book has not shown that they transfer to \(A_{\text{ret}}\) on biallelic SNPs of inbred lines, Section 11.5 declines the transfer, and Chapter 12 says that whether the MAF-threshold loss and allelic richness carry the same information was not examined. The paper is a reason to expect allele counts and heterozygosity to respond differently to a bottleneck, which is why \(A_{\text{ret}}\) is reported beside \(\alpha\). It does not test the recommendation.

On effective size, Toro et al. (2020) report in their abstract that under optimal molecular management the rate-based \(N_e = 1/(2\Delta F)\) takes negative values in early generations and reaches no asymptote in the long term (abstract only, as in Section 11.3). Allendorf et al. (2024) is a statement about \(N_e\) as a predictor of allelic loss. The first cautions against reading a rate-based \(N_e\) off a managed population, and the second is a separate limit of \(N_e\); Section 12.6 says not to report a rate-based \(N_e\) under active management, and reports \(N_s\) as a descriptor of a plan. That is not a recommendation to stop measuring \(\Delta F\): Chapter 12 monitors \(F_{drift}\) and \(F_{hom}\) against the random-subset null, and Chapter 23 measures \(\Delta F\) from the trajectory of \(F_{\text{drift}}\) with a diagnostic for a saturating series.

21.8 Optimisation machinery reported in plants

This is where the corpus is thinnest. In the abstracts and notes the screen read:

  • Optimal contribution methods in plant records (for example Sanchez et al. (2023)) do not state solver type, runtime or complexity class.
  • Core-subset search is greedy or distance-based heuristic; only input and output set sizes are reported, not runtime.
  • Evolutionary and metaheuristic algorithms are applied in plants, to other objectives. Yadav et al. (2025) (animal file) compare a genetic algorithm (GA), differential evolution (DE), particle swarm optimisation (PSO) and simulated annealing (SA) for choosing parents under Optimal Haplotype Selection and Optimal Population Value (OPV), on 583 wheat lines with 29,972 SNPs scored for stripe rust. Yadav et al. (2026) (animal file) use a genetic algorithm to select 50 donor parents for introgression into one elite cultivar. Villiers et al. (2024) (plant file), titled “Evolutionary computing to assemble standing genetic diversity …”, compare optimal haplotype stacking, optimal population value, truncation and OCS over 100 generations in a wheat simulation: OCS and truncation had exhausted diversity while considerable diversity remained under haplotype stacking. A plant-file record optimises breeding-programme design by stochastic simulation with evolutionary algorithms (10.1093/g3journal/jkae248); I have not read it beyond its note.
  • Problem sizes are reported as candidate-cross counts (330,078 possible barley parent combinations, Neyhart and Smith (2019)) or collection sizes, never as optimiser performance.

In neither file does a record apply DE or another metaheuristic to a diversity-constrained selection of hybrids, or report a runtime or complexity comparison against quadratic-programming OCS solvers. The tasks of the records in this section are haplotype stacking and introgression, not selection under a coancestry constraint. That is a statement about notes and abstracts.

AlphaMate (Gorjanc and Hickey 2018), whose note in the animal file says it optimises with an evolutionary algorithm, has 58 citations at screening, rank 9 of 324 by citations in that file, so the file does not support calling it the most cited tool.

The ranking in Yadav et al. (2025) is of algorithms on one problem. As Chapter 17 reads it, the order by convergence area under the curve (AUC) for the OPV objective is GA 0.879, DE 0.860, PSO 0.820 and SA 0.769, with DE comparable in final fitness; the abstract says GA “consistently achieved high fitness and rapid convergence” and DE “showed robustness but required longer runtime and careful tuning”. The AUC values and the 583 are not in either CSV, whose note for the record only says that it compares DE, GA, PSO and SA, and are in no versioned file here. This is evidence about algorithms on a haplotype objective. It is not evidence about encodings: Finding 4 of Section 12.4 is a verdict on the N-dimensional key encoding at one budget (three seeds, one setup, four restrictions), and Chapter 17 finds sel_de() ahead of the line-encoded DE only without a cap and a swap local search ahead of both at equal wall-clock. For this book’s problem the algorithm and its move structure matter as well as the encoding. The wheat study tests neither claim: it has a different objective, no OCS and no swap local search.

21.9 What remains unestablished

Stated strictly, from the screened abstracts and notes. The distinction that matters is between unestablished in the literature, the claim of this chapter, and unaddressed in this book. Table 21.3 lists the items and, for each, the answer the book gives. Those answers are simulations and measurements under stated assumptions, none from an operating programme; they narrow what the book leaves open and do not change what the corpus establishes. The third and seventh rows rest on Chapter 23 and Chapter 22, which had no final review record when this chapter was reviewed; read them as conditional on those chapters. The seventh row’s answer is a year-15 stock beside the arms’ mean cycle lengths, not a rate per unit of cycle-time reduction.

21.10 Consequences for the pipeline

  1. Keep the two metrics of Section 12.6 inside the optimiser and report the wide package outside it.
  2. Plan for periodic germplasm injection. On the one simulation reviewed (Sanchez et al. 2023), a closed elite pool depletes however well the within-pool constraint is managed.
  3. Bridging, rather than direct introduction, for exotic material is the recommendation of the Sanchez et al. (2023) abstract, which I could not confirm against a direct-introduction arm; I read it as a hypothesis for this book, not as an established comparison. Sanchez et al. (2023) select donors on phenotype, not on genomic predictions calibrated on the ongoing programme. The choice of donor criterion is not established: Arca et al. (2023) report that lines derived from landraces with limited contributions enriched the haplotype diversity of the reference lines more than those from high-contribution landraces, and compare no ranking by contribution with a ranking by raw diversity.
  4. Treat heterotic-group assignment as an uncertain input: the pool-balance constraint is only as good as the assignment behind it.
  5. Hold between-pool divergence in a band rather than maximising it (Trap 3 of Chapter 13); the width of the band is a programme decision.
  6. Do not conflate the diversity constraint with mean prediction. The diversity side is a function of line usage and needs no dominance model; the mean index needs one wherever dominance is present (a consequence of Section 5.11, not a corpus result).
  7. Report the diversity cost per cycle explicitly when shortening cycles (Chapter 22 reports the year-15 stock and the cycle length, not a per-cycle rate).
  8. If assembling a core or donor set, do it on markers rather than passport data, within the settings of Reeves et al. (2020).
  9. Do not read a high marker-based distance as the absence of redundancy: a constraint on state-based \(f\) is a constraint on distance for inbred lines (Trap 1 of Chapter 13). In the 298-line study (Wegary et al. 2019) a large share of pairs is distant on the state scale while a majority is above 0.050 on the relative-kinship scale; the authors read both as redundancy. That the two scales disagree is this chapter’s inference, not a result of the study.
  10. Validate on real genotypes the redundancy result of Section 12.3, which is cached external data with an unrecorded setting, before treating it as settled.
