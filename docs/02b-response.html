4  Response to selection: the other half of the objective

The preceding chapters define what a selection programme spends: coancestry (Chapter 2) and its partition over the two heterotic pools (Chapter 3), within the programme of Chapter 1. None of them writes down what selection buys. This chapter follows Chapter 3 because that chapter leaves two things to it: it names the component \(GD_{BI}\) of gene diversity (\(GD\)) as the raw material of the next cycle’s response without saying how response depends on it, and it defers the genic-variance argument for a mixture base. Section 4.2 carries both, and item 3 of the Preface (the diversity that protects response is the within-pool part) is what that section establishes.

The asymmetry is not cosmetic. Chapter 23 will report a constrained programme overtaking an unconstrained one after several cycles, at a crossover cycle that varies with the simulation seed. The book can report that because it simulated it, not because anything in the preceding chapters predicts it, and this chapter does not predict it either: a reader who wants to know when a crossover arrives in their programme still has to re-run the simulation with their own parameters. What this chapter installs are the pieces a prediction would be built from: the variance selection acts on, the equation that turns that variance into response, and the restricted selection index, which is set beside the book’s \(\alpha\) constraint at the end of the chapter (as a comparison, not as its proof).

The data come from simulate_pools(), the generator that Chapter 6 sets beside its own simulate_lines() and simulate_data(): it uses the same pool allele frequencies, drawn under the Balding-Nichols model (Balding and Nichols 1995) with parameter fst from ancestral frequencies, the same homozygous lines GL (0/1) and the same complete A \(\times\) B factorial of hybrids X (0/0.5/1), but a different trait. Its trait has n_qtl markers taken at random as quantitative trait loci (QTL) with independent standard-normal effects beta (zero elsewhere), and an index equal to the standardised true genetic value plus independent normal noise of variance \(1/h^2 - 1\), then standardised: before that last step the genetic value has unit variance, so \(h^2\) is the share of the index variance it contributes in expectation. Table 4.1 gives the size of the draw used in the rest of the chapter.

4.1 The breeder’s equation, and how well it does here

Response to one round of truncation selection is

\[R \;=\; i \, r \, \sigma_A \tag{4.1}\]

with \(i\) the selection intensity (a property of the selected fraction alone), \(r\) the accuracy of the criterion as a predictor of genetic value, and \(\sigma_A\) the additive standard deviation (Falconer and Mackay 1996).1 The equation is used here in the accuracy form \(i\,r\,\sigma_A\), which for \(r = h\) is the familiar \(i\,h\,\sigma_A\). Here the genetic value of the hybrids is the vector \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\) (gv), purely additive in the marker coding by construction of simulate_pools() (no dominance, \(d_k \equiv 0\)), so the genotypic value of a hybrid equals its additive value and \(\sigma_A\) is estimated by sd(gv). sel_intensity() returns \(i\); later chapters use it inside the usefulness criterion (Chapter 13) and, in Chapter 9, in a replicated check of an equation of this form on a different object. Table 4.2 sets the predicted response of Equation 4.1 beside the realised one for the top 60 hybrids on the index.

What “response” means in this chapter. The realised response is the mean genetic value of the selected hybrids minus the mean over all candidates: the genetic selection differential on the hybrids that were selected, not the change transmitted to a next generation. In the programme of Chapter 1 the hybrids are not recycled, lines are (assumption A3 there), so what a cycle transmits is a line’s value against a tester pool, a different object from the differential measured here. The comparison of Table 4.2 therefore asks one thing: whether Equation 4.1 reproduces the genetic selection differential of truncation selection on this generator. It is not a test of the response transmitted across cycles.

Show code 
n_sel <- cfg$n_sel
N     <- nrow(X)

r      <- cor(I, gv)
sel    <- order(I, decreasing = TRUE)[seq_len(n_sel)]

In this single draw (seed 1) the equation overpredicts, by about 13 percent. Table 4.3 splits that gap into the intensity and the regression parts.

fmt(data.frame(
  quantity = c("intensity, normal theory", "intensity, realised",
               "response with realised intensity (sim. units)",
               "response from regression (sim. units)",
               "realised response (sim. units)", "index skewness"),
  value    = c(i_norm, i_real, i_real * r * sd(gv),

               mean(scale(I)^3))), 3)

Two identities can be read off it. (1) The response from regression and the response with realised intensity are the same quantity written twice, since \(b\,S_I = i_{\text{real}}\,r\,\sigma_A\) identically (with \(b = \operatorname{Cov}(\mathrm{gv}, I)/\operatorname{Var}(I)\), \(S_I\) the selection differential on the index, \(i_{\text{real}} = S_I/\operatorname{sd}(I)\), \(r = \operatorname{Cov}(\mathrm{gv}, I)/(\operatorname{sd}(\mathrm{gv})\operatorname{sd}(I))\) and \(\sigma_A = \operatorname{sd}(\mathrm{gv})\), all on the \(N - 1\) scale, which cancels). (2) The regression residual \(e = \mathrm{gv} - \overline{\mathrm{gv}} - b\,(I - \bar I)\) has mean zero over the candidates, so the realised response minus the response from regression is exactly the mean residual over the selected set, \(\bar e_{\text{sel}}\): the part of the gap that is not an intensity effect is the selected hybrids’ average departure from the linear regression of genetic value on the index. The chunk below asserts both, to the relative tolerance \(1 \times 10^{-12}\) of the setup.

Show code 
S_I  <- mean(I[sel]) - mean(I)
e    <- (gv - mean(gv)) - b * (I - mean(I))
dev1 <- dev_rel(b * S_I, i_real * r * sd(gv))
dev2 <- dev_rel((mean(gv[sel]) - mean(gv)) - b * S_I, mean(e[sel]))
stopifnot(dev1 < tol_exact, dev2 < tol_exact, abs(mean(e)) < tol_exact)

Deviations: identity (1) exactly 0, identity (2) \(1.1 \times 10^{-16}\); the mean residual over the selected hybrids is -0.546 simulation units.

With \(N =\) 625 candidates and \(n =\) 60 selected, \(i = \varphi(z)/(n/N)\), with \(z\) the standard-normal truncation point that leaves a fraction \(n/N\) above it and \(\varphi\) the standard-normal density, is the intensity of an infinitely large normal population (Falconer and Mackay 1996). At this finite \(N\) the expected standardised mean of the top \(n\) of \(N\) independent normal draws is smaller. A single draw cannot tell whether the remaining gap is structural or sampling, so Table 4.4 repeats the same simulate_pools() call over 30 seeds, and the finite-\(N\) intensity is estimated from 4000 normal samples.

Show code 
one_seed <- function(s) {

The stopifnot() in that chunk asserts the chapter’s own claim, not an identity, so its error rates are computed, not assumed. The rule is \(|\bar g| < 2\,\mathrm{s.e.}\) with the s.e. estimated from the same \(n =\) 30 seeds. If the true mean gap is zero and the per-seed gaps are normal, \(\bar g/\mathrm{s.e.}\) follows a \(t\) distribution with \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom, and the rule fails to hold (a false alarm that stops the render under a different seed set) with probability \(P(|t_{n-1}| > 2)\). Against a true bias \(\delta\) the ratio is non-central \(t\) with parameter \(\delta\sqrt{n}/s\), \(s\) the per-seed s.d. of the gap.

Show code 
s_gap    <- sd(rep_seeds[, "rel_gap"])
p_accept <- function(delta) {          # P(rule holds) at a true mean gap delta
  ncp <- delta * sqrt(n_rep) / s_gap
  pt(2, n_rep - 1, ncp = ncp) - pt(-2, n_rep - 1, ncp = ncp)
}
false_alarm <- 1 - p_accept(0)
delta80     <- uniroot(function(d) (1 - p_accept(d)) - 0.8, c(0, 10 * s_gap))$root
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(false_alarm, 2 * pt(-2, n_rep - 1))))

That is a false-alarm rate of 5.5 percent, about one seed set in 18. The assertion fails (flags a bias) with probability 0.8 only when the true mean gap is 0.046 in relative gap (4.6 percent of the predicted response), using this sample’s per-seed s.d. of 0.088; smaller true biases are flagged less often. A passing assertion therefore excludes biases of about that size and above, not smaller ones.

Over the 30 seeds the mean relative gap is 0.006 (standard error 0.016), the prediction exceeds the realised response in 19 of 30 seeds, and the mean realised intensity is 1.776 against a finite-\(N\) normal expectation of 1.771 (s.d. 0.044) and the infinite-population value 1.774.

The replicated mean gap is within two standard errors of zero, so at this scale the seed-1 overprediction is consistent with sampling variation. What 30 seeds can resolve is limited: two standard errors of the mean gap are 0.032, so a mean relative gap smaller than about that cannot be excluded, and the result is the absence of a detectable gap above that size, not a proof that the gap is zero. The two sizes, 0.046 and 0.032, answer different questions and are not two estimates of one bound: the first is the true mean gap that the rule above flags with probability 0.8 (its power, at this sample’s per-seed s.d.), the second is two standard errors of the observed mean gap, the threshold of the rule itself. In a normal approximation the first is the threshold plus 0.84 standard errors, that is 1.42 times the second; the non-central \(t\) computation above gives 1.43 times. (The seed-1 draw is one of the 30 seeds, not an independent replicate.) Limits of scope: only the teaching scale (25 + 25 lines, 2000 markers, 300 QTL, \(h^2 = 0.5\), Balding-Nichols fst = 0.15) was measured, so nothing here says how the gap behaves at production scale or under selection across cycles; the object is the genetic selection differential of the selected hybrids on an additive trait (see the definition of response before Table 4.2), not a transmitted response. The replicated sweep of Chapter 9 tests the equation on a different object, the change in the progeny mean, with \(r\) taken against the breeding value because that trait has dominance, so it is not a replication of this check and its numbers are not reproduced here.

4.2 Genic variance, and what it has to do with gene diversity

The additive variance in Equation 4.1 is not a fixed property of the population. Split it in two.

Genic variance is what the loci would contribute if they were independent:

\[V_{\text{genic}} \;=\; \sum_k \beta_k^2 \operatorname{Var}(x_k) \tag{4.2}\]

with \(\beta_k\) the effect of marker \(k\) and \(x_k\) its hybrid genotype. This is a definition of the chapter, not a result attributed to a source.

Realised variance is what the population actually has, \(\operatorname{Var} (\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta)\), which is the sum of the whole covariance matrix rather than only its diagonal. The difference is exactly the covariance term of Equation 4.3, an identity of the covariance matrix that needs no source:

\[\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta) - V_{\text{genic}} \;=\; 2\sum_{k<l}\beta_k\beta_l \operatorname{Cov}(x_k, x_l) \tag{4.3}\]

Bulmer (1971) is the reference for the mechanism by which selection makes this term negative (negative gametic-phase disequilibrium among trait loci, which lowers the additive variance below its genic value); the primary text was not read for this book, and the statement is reported, not checked. The term that Table 4.5 evaluates for the seed-1 draw is a different thing, and three assumptions decide what it is. (i) In simulate_pools() the genotypes of the lines are drawn independently across loci given the pool frequencies. (ii) No selection precedes the evaluation. (iii) Each pool has finitely many lines (25 + 25 here). Over the complete A \(\times\) B factorial, an identity of the design, \(\operatorname{Cov}(x_k, x_l) = [\operatorname{Cov}_A(u_k, u_l) + \operatorname{Cov}_B(v_k, v_l)]/4\), with \(\operatorname{Cov}_A\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}_B\) the covariances across the lines of each pool (up to the \(N\) against \(N - 1\) divisor), so no between-pool term enters. Under (i) and (ii) each of the two pool covariances has expectation zero, so the term of the table is the sampling covariance among loci of 25 + 25 founder lines, with expectation zero; by (iii) it is not zero in a draw, and its sign and size change with the seed. The seed-1 value therefore cannot be read as the size or the sign of a Bulmer effect. Measuring a selection-induced term needs a control in which the same founders are selected at random, which is what Chapter 24 does.

Cx  <- cov(X)

The design identity for \(\operatorname{Cov}(x_k, x_l)\) is asserted next in population-divisor form on the seed-1 draw, and the distribution of the term is computed over the 30 seeds of Table 4.4 and over a disjoint block of 200 seeds, so that the reading of the seed-1 value does not rest on a block that contains it.

On the pool covariances, the factorial identity holds to a relative deviation of \(1.5 \times 10^{-15}\) (tolerance \(1 \times 10^{-12}\)). The seed-1 value, a share of -0.108, lies 0.53 s.d. from zero: it is the spread of the sampling covariance among loci, not an effect.

How does \(V_{\text{genic}}\) relate to gene diversity? Let \(p_{Ak}\) and \(p_{Bk}\) be the frequencies of the allele coded 1 at locus \(k\) among the inbred lines of pools A and B, \(q = 1 - p\) (so \(\bar q_k = 1 - \bar p_k\)), and \(\bar p_k = (p_{Ak} + p_{Bk})/2\) the hybrid-pool frequency. A hybrid of the complete A \(\times\) B factorial carries \(x_k = (u_k + v_k)/2\), with \(u_k \in \{0, 1\}\) the allele of its pool-A parent and \(v_k \in \{0, 1\}\) that of its pool-B parent (the \(u_{ik}\), \(v_{jk}\) of Section 5.11 without the line index), drawn independently from the two pools, so over the factorial (population-variance scale)

\[\operatorname{Var}(x_k) \;=\; \frac{p_{Ak}q_{Ak} + p_{Bk}q_{Bk}}{4} \;=\; \frac{\bar p_k \bar q_k}{2} - \frac{(p_{Ak} - p_{Bk})^2}{8}. \tag{4.4}\]

Show code 
pA <- colMeans(U)
pB <- colMeans(V)
pH <- (pA + pB) / 2
v_pop <- colMeans(X^2) - colMeans(X)^2                    # population scale (divisor N)
dev_var1 <- dev_rel(v_pop, (pA * (1 - pA) + pB * (1 - pB)) / 4)
dev_var2 <- dev_rel(v_pop, pH * (1 - pH) / 2 - (pA - pB)^2 / 8)
# scale: genic_var() and var(gv) use the divisor N - 1
dev_scale <- dev_rel(V_genic, N / (N - 1) * sum(sim$beta^2 * v_pop))
# equal effects (beta = 1 at all m loci): sum(v_pop) = m * GD_WS / 4, GD_T = GD_WS + GD_BS
m_loc <- ncol(X)
GD_WS <- mean(pA * (1 - pA) + pB * (1 - pB))
GD_BS <- mean((pA - pB)^2 / 2)
GD_T  <- mean(2 * pH * (1 - pH))
dev_eq1 <- dev_rel(sum(v_pop), m_loc * GD_WS / 4)
dev_eq2 <- dev_rel(GD_T, GD_WS + GD_BS)
stopifnot(c(dev_var1, dev_var2, dev_scale, dev_eq1, dev_eq2) < tol_exact)
# special case (a), pA = pB = pbar: Var(x_k) = pbar qbar / 2, equal effects m GD_T / 4
dev_mix <- dev_rel(sum(pH * (1 - pH) / 2), m_loc * GD_T / 4)
stopifnot(dev_mix < tol_exact)

Deviations (tolerance \(1 \times 10^{-12}\), relative to the larger of 1 and the quantity): the two forms of Equation 4.4 against the empirical population variance of the hybrids, \(1.8 \times 10^{-16}\) and \(1.8 \times 10^{-16}\); the scale factor below, \(5.6 \times 10^{-16}\); the two equal-effect statements below, \(2.5 \times 10^{-15}\) and \(5.6 \times 10^{-17}\); and the mixture-base limit, \(3.1 \times 10^{-15}\).

The formula is on the population-variance scale (divisor \(N\)); genic_var() and var(gv) use the divisor \(N - 1\), so for this factorial genic_var(X, beta) equals \(N/(N-1)\sum_k \beta_k^2\,(p_{Ak}q_{Ak} + p_{Bk}q_{Bk})/4\) exactly (the factor \(N/(N-1)\) on the population-scale genic variance): here 25.1648 on the population scale and 25.2051 from genic_var(), a ratio of 1.0016 against \(N/(N-1) =\) 1.0016. The per-locus variance entering \(V_{\text{genic}}\) (Equation 4.2) is therefore not proportional to \(\bar p_k \bar q_k\): the ratio \(\operatorname{Var}(x_k)/(\bar p_k \bar q_k)\) ranges from 0.107 to 0.5 across these loci. Gene diversity of the hybrid pool, \(\frac{1}{m}\sum_k 2 \bar p_k \bar q_k\), is \(GD_T\) of Equation 1.2 for the complete factorial of inbred lines with equal pool weights (Chapter 3, Table 3.6). It contains the between-pool term \(GD_{BS} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_k (p_{Ak} - p_{Bk})^2/2\) that Equation 4.4 removes (since \(2\bar p_k\bar q_k = p_{Ak}q_{Ak} + p_{Bk}q_{Bk} + (p_{Ak} - p_{Bk})^2/2\)), while \(V_{\text{genic}}\) among hybrids sees only the within-pool part \(GD_{WS} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_k (p_{Ak}q_{Ak} + p_{Bk}q_{Bk})\), weighted by squared effect. \(GD_{WS} = GD_{WI} + GD_{BI}\) (Equation 3.9), so with inbred parents (\(GD_{WI} = 0\)) it is the \(GD_{BI}\) that Chapter 3 names: the two symbols are one number here, and this chapter writes \(GD_{WS}\) because the sum over the two pools is what enters \(V_{\text{genic}}\). The inference is that gene diversity is a proxy for genic variance only through \(GD_{WS}\), and only if effect sizes are unrelated to which loci lose diversity; this chapter does not test the second condition.

Two special cases of the same formula fix the scope, each under stated conditions. (a) A mixture base: with \(p_{Ak} = p_{Bk} = \bar p_k\) (equivalently, a mixture of sources intermated to Hardy-Weinberg proportions, \(x_k\) the mean of two independent alleles each Bernoulli(\(\bar p_k\))), \(\operatorname{Var}(x_k) = \bar p_k\bar q_k/2\) and \(V_{\text{genic}} = \sum_k \beta_k^2 \bar p_k\bar q_k/2\). With effects of equal size \(\beta\) at all \(m\) loci, and markers whose frequencies represent those of the trait loci, that is \(m\beta^2 GD_T/4\), so maximising \(GD_T\) then maximises \(V_{\text{genic}}\); with unequal effects the two differ by the squared-effect weighting. This is the statement that Chapter 3 uses for a base population and defers here. (b) Hybrids of two pools never intermated, equal effects: \(V_{\text{genic}} = m\beta^2 GD_{WS}/4\), which equals \(m\beta^2 GD_T/4\) only when \(GD_{BS} = 0\), the case (a). The trait of this chapter (300 loci, \(N(0,1)\) effects) satisfies neither equal-effects condition, so (a) and (b) are statements about the proxy, not about the gv above. Neither \(GD_T\) nor \(V_{\text{genic}}\), being functions of allele frequencies and not of haplotypes, sees linkage disequilibrium.

The proxy is incomplete in two further ways. Gene diversity weights every locus equally, so it cannot distinguish a marker carrying half the genetic variance from one carrying none. And – more seriously – no frequency-based metric sees the covariance term, as the next box sets out.

WarningThe frequency-based metrics cannot see the covariance term

\(\theta_S\), \(GD_S\) (and \(GD_T\)), \(N_s\), allelic richness, \(F_{hom}\) and \(F_{drift}\) – including \(\theta_S\), the only metric the differential evolution (DE) selector constrains – are all functions of allele frequencies one locus at a time. The covariance term of Equation 4.3 does not move a single allele frequency. eff_dim() is the exception: it is computed from the spectrum of the double-centred \(f\), a multilocus quantity, so it does respond to disequilibrium, but it weights loci by nothing, not by effect. A flat gene diversity trajectory therefore does not imply flat additive variance, for two separate reasons. First, \(GD_T = GD_{WS} + GD_{BS}\) and only \(GD_{WS}\) enters \(V_{\text{genic}}\) among hybrids, so a flat \(GD_T\) can accompany a falling \(GD_{WS}\) whenever \(GD_{BS}\) rises (Chapter 3 gives the drift case). Second, the covariance term of Equation 4.3 is invisible to every one-locus metric, and enters \(\sigma_A^2\) and from there the predicted \(R\) of Equation 4.1. Chapter 24 tests the second reason only: it measures, against a random-selection control, whether selection builds this term and how quickly it decays, and does not compare a gene-diversity trajectory with a variance trajectory.

4.3 Combining ability against a tester pool

Additive variance is the variance of breeding values, not of genotypic values. Two statements about combining ability separate the chapter’s additive trait from the dominance case, and the dominance assumption is declared where each is made. The model of Equation 1.1 splits a hybrid value into general combining ability (GCA) of the two parent lines and specific combining ability (SCA) of the pair; the concepts are those of Sprague and Tatum (1942), and the exact orthogonal split used here is the book’s own, derived in Section 5.11.

Without dominance (\(d_k \equiv 0\), the case of gv): the effect on the hybrid value of substituting one allele at locus \(k\) is \(\beta_k/2\) whatever the pool it is substituted into (a parental allele fixes one half of the hybrid coding \(x_k \in \{0, 1/2, 1\}\), so one substitution moves \(x_k\) by \(1/2\) and \(\mathrm{gv}\) by \(\beta_k/2\); this is the additive effect \(a_k\) of Chapter 1 and Chapter 5 for \(d_k \equiv 0\), since the two homozygotes, worth \(\pm a_k\) there, differ by \(2a_k = \beta_k\) here), a line’s GCA does not depend on the tester set (Chapter 1), the value of a hybrid is \(\mathrm{gv}_{ij} = a_i + b_j\) with \(a_i = \sum_k \beta_k u_{ik}/2\) and \(b_j = \sum_k \beta_k v_{jk}/2\); the GCAs are \(a_i\) and \(b_j\) centred on their means and the SCA is zero up to rounding. Over the complete factorial \(a_i\) and \(b_j\) are uncorrelated, so \(\operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{gv}) = \operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{GCA}_A) + \operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{GCA}_B)\): the whole \(\sigma_A^2\) of Equation 4.1 is GCA variance, with no interaction part. The chunk below computes the split from the factorial table of gv itself (row and column means, the interaction as the residual), asserts these statements, and also estimates the GCA of the pool-A lines against only five of the pool-B lines as testers.

Show code 
nA <- nrow(U); nB <- nrow(V)
GV <- matrix(gv, nA, nB)                       # ped varies pool A fastest
a_i <- drop(U %*% sim$beta) / 2                # a_i = sum_k beta_k u_ik / 2
b_j <- drop(V %*% sim$beta) / 2
gca_A_add <- rowMeans(GV) - mean(GV)           # GCA of A lines against all of pool B
gca_B_add <- colMeans(GV) - mean(GV)
sca_add   <- GV - mean(GV) - outer(gca_A_add, gca_B_add, "+")
tester    <- seq_len(5)                        # a five-line tester set from pool B
gca_A_sub <- rowMeans(GV[, tester]) - mean(GV[, tester])
dev_a_b   <- dev_rel(GV, outer(a_i, b_j, "+"))
dev_gca   <- dev_rel(c(gca_A_add, gca_B_add), c(a_i - mean(a_i), b_j - mean(b_j)))
dev_sca0  <- dev_rel(sca_add, 0)
dev_vadd  <- dev_rel(var(gv), var(rep(gca_A_add, times = nB)) +
                              var(rep(gca_B_add, each  = nA)))
dev_tstr  <- dev_rel(gca_A_sub, gca_A_add)
stopifnot(c(dev_a_b, dev_gca, dev_sca0, dev_vadd, dev_tstr) < tol_exact)

Deviations (tolerance \(1 \times 10^{-12}\)): \(\mathrm{gv}_{ij} = a_i + b_j\), \(7.8 \times 10^{-16}\); GCAs equal to the centred \(a_i\), \(b_j\), \(5.9 \times 10^{-16}\); largest SCA, \(1.1 \times 10^{-14}\); \(\operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{gv}) = \operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{GCA}_A) + \operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{GCA}_B)\), \(7.9 \times 10^{-16}\), both sides being 22.4837; GCA of the pool-A lines against 5 testers instead of all 25, \(4.9 \times 10^{-16}\).

With dominance (\(d_k \not\equiv 0\)): the dominance part of a line’s GCA is an average effect evaluated at the opposite pool’s allele frequencies (Chapter 1), so combining ability is then a property of a line against a tester pool, never of the line alone, and an SCA component appears. The chunk below applies the split to an illustrative, dominance-only value \(H\) = heterosis_value(X, d), with dominance effects \(d_k \sim N(0.3, 0.1^2)\) drawn here and unrelated to \(\boldsymbol\beta\). The draw is not the one of Chapter 5 (a gamma-distributed \(d_k\), seed 4); that chapter owns the decomposition and its tests, and the shares below depend on the draw of \(d\). They are shares of that illustrative heterosis, not of the genetic variance of gv. The GCA and SCA of this half are those of \(H\) (Equation 5.14), not those of the cross mean gv of the paragraph above.

4.4 What weights = NULL is assuming

stage2_select() defaults (weights = NULL) to equal weight on every trait, and every chapter of this book including Chapter 25 takes that default. The criteria it weights are not phenotypes but the predicted trait values \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) of the hybrids (hybrids columns, passed to build_ctx() as traits). build_ctx() standardises each column before weighting, so the default index is \(\sum_j \hat g_j / \operatorname{sd}(\hat g_j)\), up to a positive affine transformation that does not change the ranking. In trait units that is the index \(\mathbf{a}'\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) with economic weights \(a_j = 1/\operatorname{sd}(\hat g_j)\). What the default assumes is therefore an economic statement: one standard deviation of predicted value is worth the same in every trait. It is a choice, not a neutral one.

Whether \(\mathbf{a}'\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) is the best index for those \(\mathbf{a}\) is a separate question, and the answer depends on what \(\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) is. Let \(W = \mathbf{a}'\mathbf{g}\) be the aggregate genotype (the breeding objective), with \(\mathbf{g}\) the vector of the traits’ genetic values (one entry per trait; not a vector over individuals). The weights \(\mathbf{b}\) on a vector of criteria \(\boldsymbol\xi\) that maximise the correlation between \(\mathbf{b}'\boldsymbol\xi\) and \(W\) are \(\operatorname{Var}(\boldsymbol\xi)^{-1}\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol\xi, \mathbf{g}')\,\mathbf{a}\). When \(\boldsymbol\xi = \hat{\mathbf{g}}\) is the multi-trait best linear unbiased predictor (BLUP) of \(\mathbf{g}\), \(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\mathbf{g}}, \mathbf{g}') = \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\mathbf{g}})\) (a standard property of BLUP, used here without a citation and verified for the example below), so \(\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}\): weighting the predictions by the economic weights is then the optimum, not a departure from it. The condition fails in general for predictions that are not BLUP of \(\mathbf{g}\), single-trait BLUPs of genetically correlated traits included.

When \(\boldsymbol\xi\) is instead the vector of phenotypic records \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{g} + \mathbf{e}\), \(\mathbf{e}\) independent of \(\mathbf{g}\), with \(\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{y}) = \boldsymbol\Sigma_P\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{g}') = \boldsymbol\Sigma_G\), the same expression is the Smith-Hazel index (Smith 1936; Hazel 1943) (the two papers were not read for this book: the expression is checked here by algebra and by the BLUP chunk below, not against them, and the matrix notation is modern, not theirs), displayed in Equation 4.5:

\[\mathbf{b} \;=\; \boldsymbol\Sigma_P^{-1}\boldsymbol\Sigma_G\mathbf{a}, \tag{4.5}\]

with \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_P\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_G\) the phenotypic and genetic covariance matrices of the traits (P and G in the code; \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_G\) is not the relationship matrix \(\mathbf{G}\)) and \(\mathbf{a}\) the economic weights. Table 4.7 evaluates Equation 4.5 for a two-trait example.

Show code 
P <- matrix(c(4.0, 1.2, 1.2, 1.0), 2)      # phenotypic covariance
G <- matrix(c(2.0, 0.3, 0.3, 0.3), 2)      # genetic covariance
a <- c(1, 1)                               # equal economic weight
stopifnot(all(eigen(G, only.values = TRUE)$values > 0),      # G is a covariance
          all(eigen(P - G, only.values = TRUE)$values > 0))  # so is P - G
Show code 
fmt(data.frame(trait = c("yield", "moisture"),
               `economic weight` = a,
               `Smith-Hazel weight` = index_smith_hazel(P, G, a),
               check.names = FALSE), 3)

Equal economic weights do not give equal index weights on phenotypes here. \(\mathbf{b}\) is proportional to \(\mathbf{a}\) if and only if \(\mathbf{a}\) is an eigenvector of \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_P^{-1}\boldsymbol\Sigma_G\). A sufficient condition is \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_G = c\,\boldsymbol\Sigma_P\) – every trait with heritability \(c\) and genetic correlations equal to the phenotypic ones – which requires neither equal variances nor uncorrelated traits.

Show code

stopifnot(c(dev_prop, dev_blup1, dev_blup2) < tol_exact)

The first statement is the sufficient condition, checked for \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_G = 0.5\,\boldsymbol\Sigma_P\) (deviation of \(\mathbf{b}\) from \(0.5\,\mathbf{a}\): \(2.2 \times 10^{-16}\); tolerance \(1 \times 10^{-12}\)). The chunk also checks the two statements that tie the phenotypic and the predicted index together, for the BLUP from one record per trait with known means, \(\hat{\mathbf{g}} = \boldsymbol\Sigma_G\boldsymbol\Sigma_P^{-1}\mathbf{y}\): \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\mathbf{g}})^{-1}\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\mathbf{g}}, \mathbf{g}')\,\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{a}\), and \(\mathbf{a}'\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) is the Smith-Hazel index \(\mathbf{b}'\mathbf{y}\) of Table 4.7. So the gap between \(\mathbf{b}\) and \(\mathbf{a}\) in that table is a property of an index on phenotypes; it is not a defect of weights = NULL, whose criteria are predictions. What the default does assume is the economic weighting \(a_j = 1/\operatorname{sd}(\hat g_j)\), and that the predictions behave as multi-trait BLUP; this chapter tests neither against a real breeding objective. The deviations of the two BLUP statements are \(2.2 \times 10^{-16}\) and \(1.1 \times 10^{-16}\).

4.5 The \(\alpha\) constraint beside the restricted index

Chapter 15 argues that a diversity budget is approvable where a weighted sum is not: a breeding committee can approve a statement that the loss of diversity will be no more than a stated fraction, and cannot approve a price of \(\lambda\) index units per unit of diversity.

That argument is usually presented as a point about human decision-making. The constrained form has two earlier lineages, reported here with what was and was not read.

Restricted selection indices. Kempthorne and Nordskog (1959) is the reference the book cites for the index that maximises aggregate gain subject to zero expected change in specified traits. The paper is not open access and was not read for this book, so what it contains, and whether it solves the problem in the form below, is not verified here. The matrix form is the book’s own statement, in the \(\mathbf{C} = \boldsymbol\Sigma_G\mathbf{R}\) form used by index_restricted() (not claimed to be their notation), with \(\mathbf{R}\) the \(n_t \times n_r\) matrix of restricted directions for \(n_t\) traits and \(n_r\) restrictions and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_P\), \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_G\), \(\mathbf{a}\) as in Equation 4.5:

\[\mathbf{b} \;=\; \boldsymbol\Sigma_P^{-1}\left[\boldsymbol\Sigma_G\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{C} (\mathbf{C}'\boldsymbol\Sigma_P^{-1}\mathbf{C})^{-1}\mathbf{C}'\boldsymbol\Sigma_P^{-1} \boldsymbol\Sigma_G\mathbf{a}\right], \qquad \mathbf{C} = \boldsymbol\Sigma_G\mathbf{R} \tag{4.6}\]

By direct substitution \(\mathbf{C}'\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}\), that is \(\mathbf{R}'\boldsymbol\Sigma_G\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}\). The chunk below checks it for a two-trait example, and that is all that is checked: the derivation of Equation 4.6 as the maximiser is not given here.

Constraining a group coancestry while maximising gain. In optimum contribution selection, as set out in the Methods of Meuwissen et al. (2020), the genetic level of the selected parents \(\bar g = \mathbf{c}'\hat{\mathbf{g}}\) is maximised over the vector of contributions \(\mathbf{c}\), with the rate of inbreeding constrained through the group coancestry of the selected parents, \(K = \tfrac12\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{G}\mathbf{c}\), where \(\mathbf{G}\) is the relationship matrix chosen for managing diversity; that paper compares several, among them molecular ones (\(\mathbf{G}_{0.5}\) and VanRaden’s). Its Introduction credits Meuwissen (1997) with optimum contribution selection that maximises gain for a given rate of inbreeding; the 1997 paper itself was not read for this book. The paper gives no formula for \(\mathbf{G}_{0.5}\); Chapter 2 derives one (Equation 2.7), from which, for contributions summing to one, the group coancestry of the molecular matrix \(\mathbf{f}\) behind \(\theta_S\) is an affine function of the \(\mathbf{G}_{0.5}\) group coancestry, so the two order selections identically. So restricting a group coancestry while maximising gain is not new to this book, and the restricted direction is not its contribution. What the book adds, as far as the sources read here go, is stated in the text of Section 11.9 and Chapter 15 (a budget measured against random subsets of the same size, applied to hybrids of two pools); this chapter has not checked that against the wider literature.

As an independent check of the formula, a literal Monte Carlo selection on traits drawn from the two covariance matrices (\(\mathbf{g} \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol\Sigma_G)\), \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{g} + \mathbf{e}\), \(\mathbf{e} \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol\Sigma_P - \boldsymbol\Sigma_G)\)) selects the top fraction on each index.

Show code

  mc_gain[k] <- mean(gm[top, 2])
  mc_se[k]   <- sd(gm[top, 2]) / sqrt(sum(top))
}
# 4 s.e.: false-alarm probability 6e-5 per index
stopifnot(abs(mc_gain - gain_m) < 4 * mc_se)

Monte Carlo against formula, in trait units (400000 draws; the Monte Carlo s.e. is the s.d. of the selected moisture values over the square root of their count): Smith-Hazel 0.217 (s.e. 0.003) against 0.22; restricted -0.002 (s.e. 0.003) against zero. Both lie within the 4 s.e. the chunk asserts.

For the restricted index the covariance with moisture is zero, and for the Smith-Hazel index it is not. The expected gain in moisture per round of selection with intensity \(i\) is \(i\,\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{b}'\mathbf{y}, \text{moisture})/\sqrt{\mathbf{b}'\boldsymbol\Sigma_P\mathbf{b}}\) (the slope of the regression of the genetic value on the index times the selection differential \(i\,\operatorname{sd}(\mathbf{b}'\mathbf{y})\)), so it is zero exactly when the covariance is. That is a statement about an expectation: it holds for known \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_P\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_G\) and a linear regression of genetic value on the index, and the realised change of the restricted trait in a finite selected sample, or with estimated covariance matrices, is not zero.

Four differences keep the restricted index from being the \(\alpha\) constraint, or a proof of it.

  1. Form. The restriction is an equality, linear in the index weights, with a closed form under the assumptions above. The \(\alpha\) constraint is an inequality, \(\alpha(S) \le \alpha_{\max}\), on the loss of gene diversity of a selected set \(S\) relative to the mean over random subsets of the same size (Section 11.9); \(\theta_S\) is a quadratic form in the contributions of a discrete selection, which the book solves by search (Chapter 15), not by a closed form.
  2. Expectation against realisation. The restricted index fixes an expected gain. \(\alpha(S)\) is a deterministic function of the selected set, so the bound holds on the realised set itself, given the reference value it is measured against.
  3. Which quantity is restricted. The budget acts on \(GD_T = 1 - \theta_S\), which contains the between-pool term \(GD_{BS}\) that Section 4.2 showed does not enter \(V_{\text{genic}}\) among hybrids. The restricted direction is therefore not the one that carries response, as item 3 of the Preface also notes; it protects variance only through \(GD_{WS}\).
  4. Equality against inequality. What changes when the equality becomes an inequality is not worked out in this chapter, and Chapter 15 does not work it out either: it states the constraint as \(\alpha \le \alpha_{\max}\) (bounded relative to a resampling baseline, not held at zero) and notes that a budget above the attainable maximum restricts nothing.
NoteWhat the lineage does and does not show

Constraining rather than pricing is not specific to this book: the restricted selection index (for a trait, reported from Kempthorne and Nordskog (1959), not read) and optimum contribution selection with a bound on a group coancestry (Meuwissen et al. (2020), whose optimum-contribution paragraph and Introduction were read; Meuwissen (1997) reported through it) both have that form. The \(\alpha\) constraint is closest to the second, and the four differences above are why neither is a proof that it is the right constraint; whether to prefer a budget to a price remains the argument of Chapter 15.

4.6 Where this connects

Chapter 10 and Chapter 11 report the frequency-based metrics whose link to variance this chapter limited to the within-pool part (Equation 4.4); neither computes \(V_{\text{genic}}\). Chapter 14 measures a prediction accuracy for a different object from the \(r\) here, which this chapter constructs as the correlation between the index and the true genetic value, set by \(h^2 =\) 0.5; Finding 5 of Chapter 12 shows what happens when accuracy is assumed rather than measured. Chapter 15 states the \(\alpha\) constraint as an inequality and sets the budget; it does not derive the constraint from the restricted index, and nothing here does. Chapter 24 tests whether selection builds the covariance term of Equation 4.3, against a random-selection control: it finds a term that is significant against that control, largest in early cycles and decaying with recombination, and it argues from the decay that constraining allele frequencies and ignoring this term is justified. It ranks that cost below the permanent cost of changed allele frequencies, on the condition that the decay is fast. Chapter 5 completes the Foundations part with the dominance side of the hybrid value; the Simulation part then opens with Chapter 6, which builds step by step the generators, one of which (simulate_pools()) drew the data here in one call.
