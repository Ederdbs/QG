3  Diversity in a subdivided population

A breeding programme is never one population. It is heterotic groups, or accessions, or landraces, or cycles – and the diversity of the whole is not the average of the diversity of the parts. Caballero and Toro (2002) give the partition that makes this precise, and it is the analytical backbone of everything that follows: the diversity-loss constraint \(\alpha\) (Equation 11.14, used in Chapter 15) bounds its total (Table 3.6), and the within/between asymmetry that makes plant hybrid breeding different from animal breeding is a statement about which term of it you are allowed to minimise (the second of the three departures of Section 1.3).

This chapter follows Chapter 2. There the molecular coancestry \(\mathbf{f}\) is defined between single individuals; here the same quantity is averaged over the individuals of subpopulations, and the partition of Caballero and Toro (2002) is stated in those terms. Chapter 1 states the partition for a set of hybrids (Equation 1.2); the step from subpopulations of individuals to hybrids partitioned over their parent lines is made in Section 3.11 below.

The paper’s central claim is uncomfortable and worth stating first: a method that ranks accessions by distance alone can invert the correct conclusion. In the paper’s own imaginary example, an accession can be the most distant while having so high an internal coancestry that removing it raises the diversity of the pool. Section 3.7.1 reproduces that result numerically.

3.1 Notation

  • A metapopulation of \(n\) subpopulations; subpopulation \(i\) has \(N_i\) individuals, \(N_T = \sum_i N_i\). In this chapter \(n\) and \(N\) count subpopulations and individuals, not the selected and candidate hybrids of
  • A tilde, \(\tilde{x}\), is a weighted mean within subpopulations: \(\tilde{f} = \sum_i f_{ii} N_i / N_T\), \(\tilde{F} = \sum_i F_i N_i / N_T\), \(\tilde{s} = \sum_i s_i N_i / N_T\). On an allele frequency, \(\tilde{p}_{k,i}\) is the frequency of allele \(k\) in subpopulation \(i\) (the mean over its individuals).
  • A bar, \(\bar{x}\), is a mean over all pairs in the metapopulation, between-subpopulation pairs included: \(\bar{f} = \sum_{i,j} f_{ij} N_i N_j / N_T^2\).
  • Always \(\bar{f} \le \tilde{f} \le \tilde{s}\). The differences between those three numbers are the partition of diversity. \(\bar{D} = \sum_{i,j} D_{ij} N_i N_j / N_T^2\), the mean over all pairs of the Nei distance \(D_{ij}\) of Equation 3.5, equals \(\tilde f - \bar f\) and enters Equation 3.7.
  • Sums and means (\(f_{ij}\), \(s_i\), \(\bar f\), \(\tilde f\), \(\tilde s\), the gene diversity (\(GD\)) terms) are computed per locus and then averaged over loci; never average frequencies first. The ratios (\(\alpha_i\), \(G_i\), \(G\), \(F_{IS}\), \(F_{ST}\), \(F_{IT}\)) are formed from those locus-averaged numerators and denominators (a ratio of means), not averaged locus by locus; the identities of Equation 3.10 hold in that form and not for the mean of per-locus ratios.
  • Quantities with the subscript \(i\) or \(ij\) (\(f_{ii}\), \(f_{ij}\), \(s_i\), \(F_i\)) refer to subpopulations: \(f_{ij}\) is the mean of the individual coancestry matrix of Chapter 2 over the block of an individual of \(i\) with an individual of \(j\), and \(s_i\) the mean self-coancestry of the individuals of \(i\). A single individual is the case \(N_i = 1\), which is the meaning these symbols have in Chapter 2 and Section 1.2.3. \(\mathbf{f}\) in bold is, in the worked example, the \(n \times n\) matrix of subpopulations.
  • \(k\) indexes the alleles of one locus in the formulas below (they are written for a multiallelic locus; at a biallelic locus the sum has two terms). The first bullet of Section 3.7.2 uses \(k\) for a biallelic locus instead, as Chapter 2 does for a marker.
WarningThe subtle point, and the one most often got wrong

Every coancestry here includes the pairs of an individual with itself – group coancestry in the sense of Cockerham (1967). That is what makes \(1 - f_{ii}\) exactly Nei’s gene diversity \(1 - \sum_k \tilde p_{k,i}^2\) (Nei 1973) – the plug-in form, without the small-sample correction \(2N_i/(2N_i - 1)\) (the factor that removes the bias derived under Equation 3.3) – and it is what makes the algebra close. The identity \(1 - f_{ii} = H_e\) for a set is Equation 1.2, derived in Section 11.2. Drop the diagonal and none of the identities of this chapter hold.

3.2 The three coancestry blocks

3.2.1 \(f_{ij}\), between subpopulations

The probability that two alleles, one sampled at random in subpopulation \(i\) and one in \(j\), are identical. From markers, \(f_{ij} = \sum_k \tilde p_{k,i} \tilde p_{k,j}\).

Read it as redundancy between two materials. If you cross plants of \(i\) with plants of \(j\), the progeny has expected inbreeding \(F = f_{ij}\). It answers: what do I gain by recombining these two accessions?

On this molecular scale no fixed level of \(f_{ij}\) marks two materials as redundant. \(f_{ij}\) is identity in state, and it equals the pedigree coancestry only if the base population has all its alleles different (Caballero and Toro 2002, 292), which a biallelic panel cannot satisfy: for unrelated lines the expected \(f_{ij}\) is the base-population floor of the panel, \(m^{-1}\sum_k (p_{b,k}^2 + q_{b,k}^2) \ge 1/2\), and the observed level of a set is the panel floor of its own frequencies, which moves with the markers retained (Section 2.3, Table 2.5). The book’s simulated pools (book_cfg, Chapter 2) show a level well above zero for lines of different pools:

3.2.2 \(f_{ii}\), within a subpopulation

The case \(i = j\). From markers \(f_{ii} = \sum_k \tilde p_{k,i}^2\), the expected homozygosity, so

\[1 - f_{ii} \;=\; H_{e,i} \;=\; \text{Nei's gene diversity of accession } i. \tag{3.1}\]

A landrace accession regenerated twenty times with forty ears per cycle accumulates coancestry and watches \(f_{ii}\) rise silently. It is the cheapest alarm for genetic erosion inside a genebank – and it is precisely what a distance tree cannot see.

3.2.3 \(s_i\) and \(F_i\), self-coancestry and molecular inbreeding

\[s_i = \sum_k \sum_l \frac{p_{k,l,i}^2}{N_i}, \qquad F_i = 2 s_i - 1, \qquad 2(1 - s_i) = 1 - F_i = H_{o,i} \tag{3.2}\]

with \(p_{k,l,i}\) the frequency of allele \(k\) within individual \(l\) of subpopulation \(i\): 0, 0.5 or 1 for a diploid, that is, the 0/1/2 dosage divided by 2 (ct_partition() does exactly this). For a biallelic marker and the reference allele, \(p_{k,l,i}\) is the within-individual frequency that 1 writes \(x\); there \(x_{ik}\) indexes an individual and a marker, here \(k\) indexes an allele. On the 0/1/2 scale itself \(s_i\) would leave \([0.5, 1]\) and \(F_i\) would leave \([0, 1]\). \(H_{o,i}\) is the observed heterozygosity, the mean over individuals of the fraction of loci at which the individual is heterozygous. A heterozygote has self-coancestry 0.5 at the locus, so \(1 - s_i\) is half the observed heterozygosity, not all of it.

Warning\(F_i\) here is molecular, and is not \(F_{IS}\)

By Equation 3.2 \(F_i = 1 - H_{o,i}\) is the observed fraction of homozygous loci of the individuals of \(i\), a function of their genotypes alone; it is not measured against a base. It is therefore not an inbreeding coefficient relative to a pedigree base, which would need the base to have all its alleles different (Section 3.2.1), and its level depends on the allele frequencies of the marker panel (the \(H_o\) of an outbred individual does), so it is not comparable across studies using different marker panels. For the question “is this population mating at random?”, use \(\alpha_i\) of Equation 3.3, read against its baseline (Equation 3.4).

In soybean, rice and wheat, pure lines have \(s_i \to 1\), so \(F_i \to 1\) and \(H_o \to 0\). That is expected, not a symptom. It changes only where the diversity resides.

3.3 Two per-subpopulation metrics

\[\alpha_i = \frac{F_i - f_{ii}}{1 - f_{ii}}, \qquad G_i = \frac{s_i - f_{ii}}{1 - f_{ii}} = \frac{1 + \alpha_i}{2} \tag{3.3}\]

\(\alpha_i\) is the subpopulation’s analogue of \(F_{IS}\), not the diversity-loss constraint \(\alpha\) of Chapter 15, and \(G_i\) is not a genomic relationship matrix \(\mathbf{G}\). A positive \(\alpha_i\) means an excess of homozygotes (selfing, mating among relatives, a Wahlund effect from internal substructure, null alleles); negative means an excess of heterozygotes (dioecy, self-incompatibility, selection, or a freshly formed F1 or synthetic) – but both are read against the baseline of the plug-in form, which is not zero.

The baseline under random mating. Take \(N_i\) diploid individuals whose genotypes are drawn independently at Hardy-Weinberg proportions from a population with allele frequencies \(p_k\) at a locus, and let \(\tilde p_k\) be the sample frequency, over the \(2N_i\) alleles. Then \(\mathbb{E}[F_i] = \sum_k p_k^2\) (the Hardy-Weinberg homozygosity) and \(\mathbb{E}[f_{ii}] = \mathbb{E}\sum_k \tilde p_k^2 = \sum_k p_k^2 + (1 - \sum_k p_k^2)/(2N_i)\), because \(\mathrm{Var}(\tilde p_k) = p_k(1 - p_k)/(2N_i)\). So \(\mathbb{E}[F_i - f_{ii}] = -(1 - \sum_k p_k^2)/(2N_i)\), \(\mathbb{E}[1 - f_{ii}] = (1 - \sum_k p_k^2)(2N_i - 1)/(2N_i)\), and the ratio of these locus-averaged expectations (the form in which the chapter computes \(\alpha_i\), Section 3.1) is \[\mathbb{E}[F_i - f_{ii}] \,/\, \mathbb{E}[1 - f_{ii}] \;=\; -\frac{1}{2N_i - 1}, \qquad\text{so}\quad G_i = \frac{1 + \alpha_i}{2} \;\to\; \frac{N_i - 1}{2N_i - 1} < \tfrac12 . \tag{3.4}\] This is exact for the ratio of expectations and a first-order statement for the expectation of the ratio. Under exact random mating a negative \(\alpha_i\) and a \(G_i\) below \(1/2\) are therefore the null for a small accession, not evidence of an excess of heterozygotes: \(\alpha_i = -1/7\) for \(N_i = 4\) plants and \(-1/23\) for \(N_i = 12\), vanishing only as \(N_i\) grows. The same \(2N_i/(2N_i - 1)\) that multiplies \(1 - f_{ii}\) in the unbiased gene diversity removes this bias. Only the departure of \(\alpha_i\) from \(-1/(2N_i - 1)\) is read in the mating column of Table 3.1. Two limits of the derivation: it assumes independent sampling of individuals from a Hardy-Weinberg population (no subdivision inside the sample, no linkage effect on the expectation). It is algebra, so the chunk alpha-baseline-check checks it twice: by exact enumeration of the Hardy-Weinberg samples, and by simulating random-mating samples and passing them through ct_partition().

Show code 
# (1) Exact. One biallelic locus, N diploids at Hardy-Weinberg: enumerate every
#     genotype count (n0, n1, n2) with its multinomial probability. Per count,
#     F = homozygous fraction and f_ii = pt^2 + (1 - pt)^2, pt = (n1 + 2 n2)/(2N).
enum_alpha <- function(N, p) {
  n <- as.matrix(expand.grid(n0 = 0:N, n1 = 0:N))
  n <- cbind(n, n2 = N - rowSums(n))
  n <- n[n[, "n2"] >= 0, , drop = FALSE]
  pr <- exp(lfactorial(N) - rowSums(lfactorial(n)) +
            drop(n %*% log(c((1 - p)^2, 2 * p * (1 - p), p^2))))
  pt <- (n[, "n1"] + 2 * n[, "n2"]) / (2 * N)
  c(total = sum(pr), EF = sum(pr * (n[, "n0"] + n[, "n2"]) / N),
    Ef = sum(pr * (pt^2 + (1 - pt)^2)))
}
N_chk  <- c(4, 12, 50)
p_grid <- c(0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 0.7, 0.95)
enum_dev <- vapply(N_chk, function(N) {
  e <- vapply(p_grid, function(p) enum_alpha(N, p), numeric(3))
  stopifnot(all.equal(e["total", ], rep(1, length(p_grid)), tolerance = 1e-12))
  a_loc <- (e["EF", ] - e["Ef", ]) / (1 - e["Ef", ])         # every locus
  a_avg <- mean(e["EF", ] - e["Ef", ]) / mean(1 - e["Ef", ]) # ratio of locus means
  c(max(abs(a_loc + 1 / (2 * N - 1))), abs(a_avg + 1 / (2 * N - 1)),
    abs((1 + a_avg) / 2 - (N - 1) / (2 * N - 1)))
}, numeric(3))
stopifnot(all(enum_dev < 1e-12))

# (2) Simulation: 60 random-mating samples of N = 4, 12, 50 plants at 3000 loci

  bs <- ct_partition(g, pop_sim)[["by_subpop"]]

The enumeration reproduces \(-1/(2N_i - 1)\) and \((N_i - 1)/(2N_i - 1)\) to \(4 \times 10^{-15}\) (machine precision), at every locus and for the locus-averaged ratio. The simulation gives a mean \(\alpha_i\) of -0.142, -0.043 and -0.01 for \(N_i = 4, 12, 50\) (baseline -0.143, -0.043, -0.01; largest deviation 1.0 Monte Carlo standard errors) and a mean \(G_i\) of 0.429, 0.478, 0.495, which is the baseline \((N_i - 1)/(2N_i - 1)\) and not \(0.5\).

\(G_i\) is the proportion of the accession’s diversity that sits between individuals rather than inside them, and it is probably the most underused quantity in the paper. It is a mating-system meter (Table 3.1):

Operationally, \(1 - G_i\) is the fraction of the diversity of the sample stored inside each plant. This is a qualitative guide to the sampling design, not a sample-size calculation (none is made here). With \(G_i \to 1\) (selfers), the diversity lies between plants, one plant carries almost none of it and many plants per accession are needed. With \(G_i\) near its random-mating baseline (maize), about half of the sample’s diversity lies inside each plant, so fewer plants are needed.

3.4 Distances, and what they actually are

Nei’s minimum distance between subpopulations (Toro and Caballero 2005, sec. 3a) is

\[D_{ij} = \frac{f_{ii} + f_{jj}}{2} - f_{ij} = \sum_k \tfrac{1}{2}(\tilde p_{k,i} - \tilde p_{k,j})^2 . \tag{3.5}\]

Note what that is: half the sum of squared allele-frequency differences – a variance component, not an evolutionary distance calibrated in time. It is the piece that enters \(F_{ST}\), and it is legitimate. The error in a distance-only method is not using \(D_{ij}\); it is using only \(D_{ij}\).

TipNo double sum over individuals is needed

Between individuals \(l\) and \(l'\) the distance is \(d_{ll'} = (s_l + s_{l'})/2 - f_{ll'}\), with \(s_l\) and \(f_{ll'}\) the self-coancestry and coancestry of single individuals (Equation 3.2 with \(N_i = 1\)); at a biallelic locus it equals \((p_l - p_{l'})^2\), \(p_l\) the within-individual frequency, and over loci it is averaged. The mean of \(d_{ll'}\) over all pairs of an individual of \(i\) and an individual of \(j\), \(\bar d_{ij}\), computed literally as a double sum over all pairs of individuals, costs \(O(N_i N_j m)\). The identity \(\bar d_{ij} = (s_i + s_j)/2 - f_{ij}\) is exact and needs only the subpopulation frequencies and self-coancestries, at cost \(O(N_T m + n^2 m)\). \(\bar d_{ij}\) is not the \(D_{ij}\) of Equation 3.5: its diagonal \(\bar d_{ii} = s_i - f_{ii}\) is not zero, and Nei’s distance is recovered as \(D_{ij} = \bar d_{ij} - (\bar d_{ii} + \bar d_{jj})/2\). Both identities follow by substituting the definitions of \(d_{ll'}\), \(s_i\) (Equation 3.2) and \(f_{ij}\). No timing at genebank scale was run for this chapter.

3.5 The equation that matters most in practice

\[\bar{f} = \sum_{i} \frac{N_i}{N_T} \Big[\, \underbrace{f_{ii}}_{\text{internal coancestry}} - \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_j D_{ij} N_j / N_T}_{\text{mean distance to the others}} \Big] \tag{3.6}\]

In Equation 3.6, each accession’s contribution to global coancestry – that is, to the loss of diversity in the pool – has two terms of opposite sign:

  • Term 1 (+). The more internally fixed an accession is, the more it raises global coancestry: it dilutes the pool with copies of the same allele.
  • Term 2 (−). The more distant it is from the others, the more it reduces global coancestry.

A distance-only method sees term 2 alone. That is the whole mechanism of the inverted conclusions.

3.6 F statistics and the partition

\[F_{IS} = \frac{\tilde F - \tilde f}{1 - \tilde f}, \qquad F_{ST} = \frac{\tilde f - \bar f}{1 - \bar f} = \frac{\bar D}{1 - \bar f}, \qquad F_{IT} = \frac{\tilde F - \bar f}{1 - \bar f} \tag{3.7}\]

with \((1 - F_{IT}) = (1 - F_{IS})(1 - F_{ST})\). \(\tilde F\) is the \(N_i\)-weighted mean of the \(F_i\) of Equation 3.2; the subscripts follow the usual reading, I for individual, S for subpopulation, T for the whole metapopulation. Eq. 6 of Caballero and Toro (2002) writes these formulas, which they call Wright’s (1969) F-statistics (Evolution and the Genetics of Populations, vol. 2, as cited there); Toro and Caballero (2005, sec. 3a) credit the form \(F_{ST} = \bar D/(1 - \bar f)\) to Cockerham (1969). Those are the attributions this chapter reports; the original papers were not read for it.

What this \(F_{ST}\) is, in heterozygosity terms. With the plug-in form of Section 3.1, \(1 - \bar f = H_T\) (\(= GD_T\), Equation 3.9 below) is the gene diversity of the pooled sample’s frequencies \(\bar p_k\), \(1 - \tilde f = H_S\) (\(= GD_{WS}\)) is the \(N_i\)-weighted mean of the \(H_{e,i}\) of Equation 3.1, and \(\tilde F = 1 - H_o\) with \(H_o\) the \(N_i\)-weighted observed heterozygosity. Hence \[F_{ST} = \frac{H_T - H_S}{H_T}, \qquad F_{IS} = 1 - \frac{H_o}{H_S}, \qquad F_{IT} = 1 - \frac{H_o}{H_T}, \tag{3.8}\] each \(H\) averaged over loci first, so that \(F_{ST}\) is a ratio of locus-averaged numerator and denominator (Section 3.1). It is also \(GD_{BS}/GD_T\) (Equation 3.10).

# One biallelic locus, two populations of equal weight, from the coancestries:
# the closed forms of the prose, over 200 random (p_A, p_B), and the worked case.
one_locus <- function(pA, pB) {
  f1  <- function(p) p^2 + (1 - p)^2                    # f_ii at a biallelic locus
  fAB <- pA * pB + (1 - pA) * (1 - pB)
  tw  <- (f1(pA) + f1(pB)) / 2
  fb1 <- (f1(pA) + f1(pB) + 2 * fAB) / 4
  pb  <- (pA + pB) / 2; dp <- pA - pB
  this <- (tw - fb1) / (1 - fb1); btw <- (tw - fAB) / (1 - fAB)
  stopifnot(all.equal(this, dp^2 / (4 * pb * (1 - pb)), tolerance = 1e-12),
            all.equal(btw, dp^2 / (2 * pb * (1 - pb) + dp^2 / 2), tolerance = 1e-12),
            all.equal(btw / this, 4 * pb * (1 - pb) / (2 * pb * (1 - pb) + dp^2 / 2),
                      tolerance = 1e-12),
            all(btw / this >= 1 - 1e-12), all(btw / this <= 2))
  rbind(this = this, between = btw)
}

It is not the only statistic written \(F_{ST}\). A statistic referenced to the coancestry between populations, \((\theta_w - \theta_{AB})/(1 - \theta_{AB})\) with \(\theta_w\) the mean coancestry within populations and \(\theta_{AB}\) between populations, is also written \(F_{ST}\); Section 1.2.4 records that the book computes both and that they differ. This chapter cites no external source for that usage and does not use the statistic. The two take different values on the same data. At one biallelic locus with allele frequency \(p_A\) in one population and \(p_B\) in the other, equal weights, \(\bar p = (p_A + p_B)/2\) and \(\Delta p = p_A - p_B\), the statistic of Equation 3.7 is \(\Delta p^2 / (4\bar p \bar q)\) and the between-referenced one is \(\Delta p^2 / (2\bar p \bar q + \Delta p^2/2)\); their ratio, \(4\bar p \bar q / (2\bar p \bar q + \Delta p^2/2)\), lies in \([1, 2]\) and tends to 2 as \(\Delta p \to 0\). For \(p_A = 0.2\), \(p_B = 0.6\) the two are \(1/6\) and \(2/7\). On the 30 random hybrids of the book’s simulated pools used in Section 3.11, the between-referenced statistic, computed from locus-averaged coancestries, is 0.224 against 0.126 for the statistic of Equation 3.7, a ratio of 1.78 (one draw, seed 7). A value of \(F_{ST}\) must therefore be read with its reference stated; in this chapter it is always Equation 3.7.

The partition itself, of gene diversity (\(GD\)) into the terms named in Table 3.2, is the heart of Caballero and Toro (2002):

\[(1 - \bar f) = \underbrace{(1 - \tilde s)}_{GD_{WI}} + \underbrace{(\tilde s - \tilde f)}_{GD_{BI}} + \underbrace{(\tilde f - \bar f)}_{GD_{BS}} \tag{3.9}\]

The four components satisfy the identities

\[\frac{GD_{WI}}{GD_T} = (1-G)(1-F_{ST}), \quad \frac{GD_{BI}}{GD_T} = G(1-F_{ST}), \quad \frac{GD_{WS}}{GD_T} = 1-F_{ST}, \quad \frac{GD_{BS}}{GD_T} = F_{ST}, \tag{3.10}\]

where \(G = (\tilde s - \tilde f)/(1 - \tilde f)\) is the metapopulation analogue of \(G_i\) (Equation 3.3). Since \(s_i - f_{ii} = G_i (1 - f_{ii})\), \(G = \sum_i N_i (1 - f_{ii}) G_i / \sum_i N_i (1 - f_{ii})\): a mean of the \(G_i\) weighted by \(N_i (1 - f_{ii})\), not the census mean \(\sum_i (N_i/N_T)\, G_i\). The two coincide when all \(G_i\) are equal or all \(f_{ii}\) are equal, and the first two identities then hold with either; when both the \(G_i\) and the \(f_{ii}\) differ the two means generally differ, and the first two identities fail with the census mean (the chunk of Table 3.3 exhibits one such case). They follow from \(1 - F_{ST} = (1 - \tilde f)/(1 - \bar f)\).

3.6.1 Why the partition changes the strategy by species

The three bullets below are this book’s reading of what the partition implies for different reproductive systems, not results of Caballero and Toro (2002), whose examples are livestock breeds and a diploid, randomly mating setting. The first two follow from the definitions (\(\tilde s = 1\) for inbred lines gives \(GD_{WI} = 0\), which the chunk of Table 3.3 exhibits); the third and the regeneration advice are judgement.

  • Outcrossers (maize, sunflower, forages, forest trees): \(GD_{WI}\) is large. The diversity is literally inside the plants. Conserving means maintaining heterozygosity – regenerate with many individuals and balanced contributions.
  • Selfers (soybean, wheat, rice, beans) and doubled-haploid or line collections: \(GD_{WI} \approx 0\). All diversity is \(GD_{BI} + GD_{BS}\). Conserving means maintaining the number of distinct lines; heterozygosity is not a target. Regenerating with few plants per line is acceptable; losing lines is not.
  • Clonal crops (potato, sugarcane, fruit trees): \(GD_{WI}\) is high and frozen – it does not decay by drift while the clone exists. The \(N_e\) algebra does not apply without adaptation.

This is the reason a hybrid maize programme cannot manage diversity with a single pool-wide number: it needs \(GD_{WS}\) and \(GD_{BS}\) reported separately. Under drift alone in two closed pools of equal weight (\(f_{AA}\), \(f_{BB}\), \(f_{AB}\) in the notation of this chapter, \(\theta_A\), \(\theta_B\), \(\theta_{AB}\) in the code (theta_pools(), chunk fst-two-forms) and in 1, equal when the usage weights equal the census weights), \(f_{AB}\) is unchanged in expectation while \(f_{AA}\) and \(f_{BB}\) rise, so an expected rise \(\Delta\) in \(\tilde f\) raises \(\bar f\) by \(\Delta/2\): \(GD_{WS}\) falls by \(\Delta\), \(GD_{BS}\) rises by \(\Delta/2\), and \(GD_T\) falls by only \(\Delta/2\). A single total understates the within-pool loss; Section 5.8 shows what the between-pool gain is worth. The argument is for neutral drift in closed pools; selection is not covered by it, and the pools are closed, so it is not the asymptotic regime of Equation 3.11.

The arithmetic is in Equation 3.5: \(D_{2j}\) contains \(f_{22}/2\), and \(f_{22} = 0.875\) is far above the other diagonal entries, so the subpopulation is distant from the others in part because it is internally nearly fixed, and the same \(f_{22}\) is what raises global coancestry in the first term of Equation 3.6. What produced the near-fixation (a small sample of \(N_2 = 4\) plants, a founder effect, drift in the source population, selection) cannot be inferred from a coancestry matrix, and the example is imaginary; the chapter does not claim a cause. The same pattern is reported for the reanalysis of eleven European pig breeds of Laval et al. (2000) in Caballero and Toro (2002, Table 5, column 3): FRBA (Basque, France; the breed codes are defined in the footnote to Table 4 of Caballero and Toro (2002)), the breed with the largest Weitzman loss (-15.2 %), shows +0.67 % when removed, the value also given in the corrected form of Toro and Caballero (2005, Table 4). These values are quoted from the tables, not recomputed. In that column the subpopulations are not weighted by their effective sizes; in the column that is (column 6), FRBA is \(-0.0\) %, and the authors argue that weighting by size is desirable (Caballero and Toro 2002, 295). The +0.67 % is therefore the result without effective-size weighting, and not the only one the paper reports.

NoteWhat this does and does not license

For a genebank: a rare accession with few plants and high homozygosity – the archetypal “unique” accession on a distance tree – may be contributing negatively to the pool. That does not mean discard it. It means the correct priority may be to regenerate it at larger \(N\), not to replicate it into a core collection. Storing seed is cheap; extinction is irreversible.

3.7.2 Optimal contributions

Maximising \(GD_T = 1 - \sum_{i,j} f_{ij} c_i c_j\) subject to \(c_i \ge 0\) and \(\sum_i c_i = 1\) is a quadratic programme:

Subpopulation 2 receives weight zero (computed \(\mathbf{c} = (0.522,\, 0.000,\, 0.270,\, 0.208)\)), and total diversity rises from 0.6744 to 0.6874, a gain of 1.9 %. Rounded to three decimals the solution equals the published \(\mathbf{c} = (0.522,\, 0,\, 0.270,\, 0.208)\) of Caballero and Toro (2002); the chunk asserts it.

Three direct uses in plant breeding:

  • Base population for recurrent selection. \(c_i\) gives the seed proportion from each source that maximises the neutral-marker \(GD_T\) of the base. (In this bullet \(k\) indexes biallelic loci.) With \(f_{ij}\) built from population frequencies as above, \(GD_T = 1 - \sum_{i,j} f_{ij} c_i c_j\) is the mean over loci of \(2\bar p_k \bar q_k\), where \(\bar p_k = \sum_i c_i p_{ik}\) is the frequency of the mixture and \(\bar q_k = 1 - \bar p_k\); it keeps the between-source term in full. If the mixture is intermated to Hardy-Weinberg proportions, the variance of the 0/0.5/1 genotype at locus \(k\) is \(\bar p_k \bar q_k / 2\), so the genic variance of the base (Equation 4.2, additive action; the variance is that of the mean of two alleles, each Bernoulli(\(\bar p_k\))) is \(\sum_k \beta_k^2 \bar p_k \bar q_k / 2\). With effects of equal size \(\beta\) at every one of the \(m\) loci and markers whose frequencies represent those of the quantitative trait loci, that is \(m \beta^2 GD_T / 4\): maximising \(GD_T\) maximises genic variance; with unequal effects the two differ by the squared-effect weighting. The case is different when the candidates are A \(\times\) B hybrids of two pools that are never intermated: Chapter 4, which derives the hybrid case and restates the case above as its limit with equal pool frequencies, shows that their genic variance contains only the within-pool part of gene diversity, not the between-pool term. In both cases neither \(GD_T\) nor the genic variance, being functions of allele frequencies and not of haplotypes, sees linkage disequilibrium.
  • Core collection. Instead of clustering heuristics plus proportional sampling, solve the quadratic programme directly with operational constraints (\(c_i \le\) multiplication capacity, \(c_i \ge\) a policy minimum for accessions of cultural importance).
  • Optimal contribution selection. Here \(\mathbf{c}\) and \(\mathbf{f}\) change object: \(\mathbf{c}\) is the vector of contributions of the \(N\) candidates and \(\mathbf{f}\) their \(N \times N\) coancestry matrix, not the contributions and the \(n \times n\) matrix of subpopulations above. Meuwissen (1997) maximises \(\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{u}\) subject to a bound on the group coancestry \(\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{c}\), with \(\mathbf{u}\) the vector of the candidates’ selection-criterion values (estimated breeding values in Meuwissen (1997), the multi-trait index \(u_i\) in Chapter 15); its Lagrangian form is \(\max \mathbf{c}'\mathbf{u} - \lambda\,\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{c}\), \(\lambda \ge 0\). This chapter is the case \(\lambda \to \infty\), pure diversity. A breeder operates in the middle of the curve – which is Chapter 15.

3.8 Equal contributions and effective size

Minimising \(\bar f\) maximises the status number \(N_s = 1/(2\bar f)\) (Lindgren et al. 1997) by definition. With census weights \(N_i/N_T\), \(\bar f\) is the mean of the individual coancestry matrix over all pairs, diagonal included, which is the group coancestry \(\theta_S\) of the whole set \(S\) (Chapter 2) whatever the subdivision; this \(N_s\) is then the \(1/(2\theta_S)\) of that chapter, and Table 3.6 writes \(GD_T = 1 - \bar f\) as \(1 - \theta_S\) (asserted in tbl-keystone-check). That chapter’s range \(N_s \in [1/2, 1]\) is a property of biallelic markers (\(\theta_S \ge 1/2\)); the multiallelic worked example of this chapter is not on it: \(N_s = 1/(2\bar f) = 1.54\) for its 30 individuals, and in neither case is \(N_s\) a head count. \(N_s\) is a static function of the current coancestry; the effective size \(N_e\) is defined from the rate at which inbreeding or coancestry rises across generations, so the two are not the same quantity, and lowering \(\bar f\) today does not by itself fix \(N_e\).

What links contributions to \(N_e\) is the asymptotic metapopulation effective size of Caballero and Toro (2002, Eq. 14), in the form restated by Toro and Caballero (2005, sec. 5a):

\[N_e \approx \frac{2Nn}{(V_W + V_B + 1)(1 - F_{ST})}, \tag{3.11}\]

with \(n\) subpopulations of equal size \(N\), and \(V_W\) and \(V_B\) the variances of the long-term contributions of individuals within and between subpopulations. Eq. 14 of Caballero and Toro (2002) has \((1 - F_{IT})\) in place of \((1 - F_{ST})\), and \((1 - F_{IT}) = (1 - F_{IS})(1 - F_{ST})\): Equation 3.11 is the case \(F_{IS} = 0\), random mating within subpopulations, which is its stated assumption. The model is a metapopulation of \(n\) subpopulations of constant size \(N\) with “any constant degree of migration” among them, discrete generations, and the formula holds only after enough generations for \(N_e\) and \(F_{ST}\) to reach their asymptotes. Two consequences. First, \(F_{ST}\) here is the asymptotic value of that metapopulation; it depends itself on the contribution variances and on migration, and for closed pools (the drift paragraph of Section 3.6.1) it tends to 1 and Equation 3.11 has no meaning. Second, the statement below holds the same \(F_{ST}\) on both sides. It does not apply as written to pools of inbred or doubled-haploid lines, where \(F_{IS} \ne 0\) and where the sampling unit is one allele copy per line rather than two per plant (Section 1.2). With random (Poisson) contributions within subpopulations and equal contributions of the subpopulations (\(V_W = 1\), \(V_B = 0\)) Equation 3.11 gives \(Nn/(1 - F_{ST})\); equalising contributions within subpopulations (\(V_W = 0\), \(V_B = 0\)) gives \(2Nn/(1 - F_{ST})\), double at the same \(F_{ST}\).

The chunk ne-equal-seed is the formula. The chunk ne-gene-dropping is the oracle: literal gene dropping in \(N = 100\) monoecious diploids with \(L = 500\) unlinked biallelic loci starting at \(p = 1/2\), and \(T = 25\) generations. In each generation every offspring takes one gamete (a random one of the two alleles at each locus, independently across loci) from its mother and one from its father; the schemes differ only in how mothers and fathers are chosen (selfing is possible in all three): independently at random (\(V_k = 2\)), every plant mother of exactly one offspring and the father at random (\(V_k = 1\)), every plant mother of exactly one offspring and father of exactly one (\(V_k = 0\)). The gene diversity \(H_t\) (the mean of \(2pq\) over loci) decays as \((1 - 1/(2N_e))^t\), so \(N_e\) is read from the slope of \(\log H_t\), one slope per independent pedigree (\(R = 30\) per scheme), inverted exactly: \(N_e = 1/(2(1 - e^{\text{slope}}))\). The comparison is made on the slope, against its Monte Carlo standard error over the 30 pedigrees, with tolerance 3 standard errors.

drop_genes <- function(N, L, T, scheme) {
  Z <- apply(matrix(rep(0:1, each = N), 2 * N, L), 2, sample)   # p = 1/2 at every locus
  A <- Z[seq_len(N), , drop = FALSE]; B <- Z[N + seq_len(N), , drop = FALSE]
  het    <- function() { p <- (colSums(A) + colSums(B)) / (2 * N); mean(2 * p * (1 - p)) }
  gamete <- function(par) {
    u <- matrix(stats::runif(N * L) < 0.5, N, L)
    ifelse(u, A[par, , drop = FALSE], B[par, , drop = FALSE])
  }
  H <- numeric(T + 1); H[1] <- het()
  for (t in seq_len(T)) {
    mo <- if (scheme == "random") sample.int(N, N, replace = TRUE) else sample.int(N)
    fa <- if (scheme == "both_once") sample.int(N) else sample.int(N, N, replace = TRUE)
    nA <- gamete(mo); nB <- gamete(fa); A <- nA; B <- nB
    H[t + 1] <- het()
  }
  H
}

That is the single-population case (\(n = 1\), \(F_{IS} = 0\)) of Equation 3.11, and nothing more.

The doubling assumes that each individual’s contribution is equalised as both female and male parent, which needs controlled pollination. In the single-population case Equation 3.11 reduces to \(2N/(V_W + 1)\), which is \(N_e = 4N/(2 + V_k)\) (Falconer and Mackay 1996) with \(V_W = V_k/2\), \(V_k\) the variance of the number of gametes an individual contributes. Harvesting the same number of seeds from every plant of an open-pollinated population equalises only the maternal contribution; with a maternal variance of 0 and a Poisson paternal variance of 1, \(V_k = 1\) and \(N_e = 4N/3\), a gain of 1.333 over Poisson contributions, not 2.

TipEqual contributions of both parents double \(N_e\)

Under controlled pollination, equal numbers of seeds from every plant as female and as male parent, and the same number of plants from every family, double the effective size (\(V_W = 0\) against \(V_W = 1\)). Equal seed numbers from open-pollinated plants alone give a factor of 4/3. Neither needs genotyping.

It is an intervention in germplasm management. The opposite – bulk harvest from a plot, which in practice samples the most productive plants disproportionately – inflates \(V_W\) and collapses \(N_e\).

3.9 The honest caveats

A breeder should take these seriously.

Criteria based on within-population variation favour the largest populations, which are often the commercial and least endangered ones (Toro and Caballero 2005, sec. 3e). A \(\bar f\) criterion can push a genebank toward abundant elite material – the opposite of the conservation objective. Beyond that:

  • Neutral markers do not measure adaptive value, disease resistance, stress adaptation or cultural value. A fixed accession may carry the only resistance allele to an emerging rust.
  • Allelic richness (which Caballero and Toro (2002, 297) call allelic diversity) is, they write, more sensitive to bottlenecks than average heterozygosity, and is often the more relevant criterion for pre-breeding. The argument that minimising \(\bar f\) maximises allelic richness in the long run (via the effective number of alleles being, by definition, the inverse of mean coancestry; (Toro and Caballero 2005, sec. 2)) is valid asymptotically but does not protect rare alleles in the short term. This is why allelic richness survives the redundancy analysis in Chapter 12 as the one genuinely independent axis.
  • Weighting between-group variation more heavily is arbitrary. A more defensible fix is to optimise \(\mathbf{c}\) with per-accession minimum constraints, as Toro and Caballero (2005, sec. 3e, Table 5) do for five Iberian pig strains.

Rule of thumb: use \(\bar f\) to decide proportions and regeneration effort; never use \(\bar f\) alone to decide discard.

3.11 What manages this quantity, and where

This chapter is the book’s keystone, and it is easy to read it as a self-contained treatment of Caballero and Toro (2002). The partition it derives (Equation 3.9) is not background: it is the object every later chapter operates on. Stated once, so the rest of the book can refer back rather than re-introduce it (Table 3.6):

For a set of hybrids the partition is taken over their parent lines. The “individuals” of Equation 3.9 are then the parent-line slots of the hybrids (one line of pool A and one of pool B per hybrid), the two pools are the subpopulations, and the weights are the usage weights of the lines, equal across the two pools by construction (Equation 1.2 and the identity \(F_{(ab)} = f^L_{ab}\) of Section 1.2.4; gd_partition() implements it). Within a pool the weights are the usage of each line, which is unequal in a random sample of hybrids (the chunk of Table 3.7 asserts it for its sample): a line used twice counts twice, so the individuals are the 30 line slots of each pool and not its 25 distinct lines. \(GD_{WI}\) (lines) and \(GD_{WI\text{-}hyb}\) (hybrids) are the same expression, \(1 - \tilde s\), over different individuals. The statements of the first row, and the identity \(GD_T = 1 - \theta_S = GD_{BI} + GD_{BS}\) of the second, are checked on 30 random hybrids of the book’s simulated pools with gd_partition() (Table 3.7):

Three consequences follow, and each is a chapter.

A single pool-wide statistic cannot express “conserve within, hold between”. That is why Chapter 11 reports the partition rather than only \(GD_T\), and why Chapter 13 makes stage S1 (conserving the heterotic groups, Table 1.1) a separate monitoring stage instead of folding pool conservation into the hybrid-selection constraint.

The between term is not a bonus to be maximised. Under the conditions of the \(GD_{BS}\) row (flat dominance, inbred parents, complete factorial, equal pool weights) expected F1 heterozygosity per locus is \(2\bar p\bar q + y^2/2\) (Equation 5.5), with \(2\bar p \bar q\) the “shared” term, which is \(GD_T\), and \(y^2/2\) the divergence term, which is \(GD_{BS}\). When the pools are redrawn further apart by neutral divergence from the same ancestral frequencies (Balding-Nichols draws, Section 5.8), the expectation of the sum does not change, so in expectation every unit of \(GD_{BS}\) gained is paid for, unit for unit, by \(GD_T\), and pushing the pools apart buys no heterosis. This is a statement about \(GD_T\) and \(GD_{BS}\), not about \(GD_{WS}\): by the drift accounting of Section 3.6.1 \(GD_{WS}\) falls by twice what \(GD_{BS}\) gains. It does not hold for non-flat dominance or for divergence that is aligned with the dominance effects, which Section 5.9 treats. The partition tells you where diversity is, not which direction to move it.

The within term is the one with a future. \(GD_{BI}\) is the raw material of next cycle’s response, which Chapter 4 makes precise: among A \(\times\) B hybrids genic variance is a sum over loci of the within-pool part of gene diversity, weighted by squared effect, while \(GD_T\) also carries the between-pool term that genic variance does not contain. Gene diversity is therefore a proxy for genic variance only through its within-pool part, only if effect sizes are unrelated to which loci lose diversity, and blind to disequilibrium; Chapter 24 is where the proxy is pushed until it breaks.
