2  Coancestry, and the two matrices

Section 1.2 has already defined, in words and with the identities the book leans on, identity by descent and by state, coancestry, the self-coancestry \(s_i\), the molecular inbreeding coefficient \(F_i\) and gene diversity \(GD_S = 1 - \theta_S\), and it points here for the equations (Equation 2.2, Equation 2.1, Equation 2.5). This chapter supplies that computation and the contrast that justifies it. The lines are inbred, as in assumption A2 of Chapter 1, and markers are biallelic.

Two matrices are built from the same marker data and they are not interchangeable. One predicts traits. The other measures diversity. Using the first for the second fails silently: it returns a number, and for the whole population that number is not a coancestry (Section 2.2). This chapter builds both, shows the failure, and defines in full the matrix used for diversity in the rest of the book. Where a conclusion rests on a published comparison of the two kinds of matrix, the source is cited with what it does and does not say.

2.1 From line to hybrid

Let \(\mathbf{M}^{L}\) be the \(L \times m\) matrix of line genotypes (\(L\) lines, \(m\) markers), with \(M^{L}_{ik} \in \{0, 2\}\) the count of the reference allele at marker \(k\). Inbred lines are homozygous, so the value 1 never occurs – residual heterozygotes must be imputed or discarded beforehand.

The F1 hybrid between lines \(a\) and \(b\) – the product of the cycle laid out in Section 1.1 – receives one gamete from each parent. Since both parents are homozygous, the gamete from \(a\) carries \(M^{L}_{ak}/2\) copies of the reference allele, so the row of the hybrid dosage matrix \(\mathbf{M}^{H}\) (one row per hybrid, \(m\) columns) for the cross \((ab)\) is

\[ M^{H}_{(ab)k} \;=\; \frac{M^{L}_{ak} + M^{L}_{bk}}{2} \;\in\; \{0, 1, 2\}. \tag{2.1}\]

The value 1 appears exactly at markers where the two lines differ. Those are the hybrid’s heterozygous loci. In the absence of epistasis, the heterosis of an F1 between two inbred lines (F1 minus mid-parent) is the sum of the dominance effects \(d_k\), the departure of the heterozygote from the homozygote midpoint at locus \(k\), over exactly these loci (Falconer and Mackay 1996, ch. 14); Chapter 5 develops that model (Equation 5.3 there).

Predicting the hybrid’s genotype is deterministic, not statistical. Given the homozygous parents declared above, it requires no further assumption – unlike its phenotype, which requires a great many.

From here on we work with the within-individual reference-allele frequency,

\[ x_{ik} \;=\; \frac{M^{H}_{ik}}{2} \;\in\; \{0,\; 0.5,\; 1\}, \tag{2.2}\]

collected for the \(N\) hybrids in the \(N \times m\) hybrid marker matrix \(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{M}^{H}/2\) (rows hybrids, columns markers). It is the natural quantity for coancestry: it is the probability that an allele drawn at random from individual \(i\) at marker \(k\) is the reference allele.

2.2 The VanRaden matrix, and why it cannot measure diversity

With \(p_k\) the reference-allele frequency in the hybrid population, \(\mathbf{1}\) a vector of ones of conforming length (\(N\) for \(\mathbf{1}\,2\mathbf{p}'\) and for \(\mathbf{1}'\mathbf{G}\mathbf{1}\) below), and \(\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{M}^{H} - \mathbf{1}\,2\mathbf{p}'\) the centered matrix,

\[ \mathbf{G} \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}'}{2\sum_{k} p_k (1-p_k)}. \tag{2.3}\]

This is VanRaden’s first parametrisation (VanRaden 2008). It estimates realised additive relationship and it is the right matrix for genomic best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP). It is implemented as vanraden_G(), which takes the 0/1/2 dosage matrix \(\mathbf{M}^{H}\), not the 0/0.5/1 matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) that molecular_coancestry() takes, and returns a list holding \(\mathbf{G}\), \(\mathbf{Z}\) and \(\mathbf{p}\):

Show code 
args(vanraden_G)
#> function (M) 
#> NULL

Now the failure. Centering on \(2\mathbf{p}\) uses frequencies from the population itself, so every column of \(\mathbf{Z}\) sums to zero:

\[ \mathbf{Z}'\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{1}'\mathbf{G}\mathbf{1} \;=\; \frac{\lVert \mathbf{Z}'\mathbf{1}\rVert^2}{2\sum_k p_k (1-p_k)} \;=\; 0 . \tag{2.4}\]

The sum of all elements of \(\mathbf{G}\) is identically zero. The group coancestry \(\theta_S\) of a set \(S\) of \(n\) individuals, the mean of all \(n^2\) entries of \(\mathbf{f}\) over \(S\) with the diagonal included (Section 1.2.3), is \(\theta_S = n^{-2}\,\mathbf{1}'\mathbf{f}_{SS}\mathbf{1}\), with \(\mathbf{f}\) the matrix of Section 2.3. \(\mathbf{G}\) is on the relationship scale, twice a coancestry, so substituting it gives \(\theta_S = (2n^2)^{-1}\, \mathbf{1}'\mathbf{G}_{SS}\mathbf{1}\) (theta_S_on_G in Table 2.1, for \(S\) the whole population). That is zero, and the status number \(N_s = 1/(2\theta_S)\) (Lindgren et al. 1997) diverges on \(\mathbf{G}\). On \(\mathbf{f}\) it does not diverge, but it is not a head count either: at biallelic loci \(\theta_S \in [1/2, 1]\) and so \(N_s \in [1/2, 1]\) for every set (Section 11.3), a monotone rescaling of \(\theta_S\):

G <- ctx[["G"]]

            theta_S_on_G = mean(G) / 2,
            Ns_on_G      = 1 / (2 * mean(G) / 2),
            min_G        = min(G))
knitr::kable(data.frame(Quantity = names(g_sums),

N <- nrow(G)

The sum is zero in exact arithmetic. The sum_G of Table 2.1 is floating-point residue, and Ns_on_G is the reciprocal of that residue: its magnitude and sign carry no information about the data. The zero itself is not a rounding artefact to be worked around; it is what centering means.

On \(\mathbf{f}\) the same quantity is finite and inside the stated interval, for the whole population and for random subsets (Table 2.2):

             row.names = FALSE, align = "lr")
stopifnot(Ns_all >= 0.5, Ns_all <= 1, Ns_sub >= 0.5, Ns_sub <= 1)

In a subset \(S\) of size \(n\) the sum is not zero. Because \(\mathbf{G}\) is a Gram matrix, \(\mathbf{1}_S'\mathbf{G}\mathbf{1}_S = \lVert \mathbf{Z}'\mathbf{1}_S\rVert^2 / (2\sum_k p_k (1-p_k)) \ge 0\), with \(\mathbf{1}_S\) the indicator vector of \(S\), so a subset sum is never negative, even though individual off-diagonal elements are (min_G in Table 2.1). For \(S\) drawn at random without replacement from the \(N\) hybrids, the finite-population sampling variance of a total gives \(\mathrm{E}[\mathbf{1}_S'\mathbf{G}\mathbf{1}_S] = n\,\frac{N-n}{N-1}\, \overline{G}_{ii}\), with \(\overline{G}_{ii}\) the mean diagonal: the factor \(n(N-n)/(N-1)\) is not a function of \(n/N\) alone, and the expectation returns to zero at \(n = N\). It is \(n^2\) times the scaled squared distance between the mean of the subset’s centred genotypes and the population centroid, which is the point that fixed \(\mathbf{p}\). It is therefore a well-defined distance, but it measures the subset against the sample’s own frequencies and is not a coancestry of the subset (Table 2.3 checks the expectation by Monte Carlo at a fixed \(n\)).

Show code 
set.seed(1)
S_sum <- vapply(seq_len(1000), function(r) {
  S <- sample(N, sample(2:(N - 1), 1))
  sum(G[S, S, drop = FALSE])
}, numeric(1))
stopifnot(min(S_sum) > -1e-8)                  # Gram matrix: 1_S'G1_S >= 0

# E[1_S'G1_S] = n (N - n)/(N - 1) mean(diag G), at fixed n = 100: Monte Carlo
n100 <- 100
set.seed(11)

}, numeric(1))

             row.names = FALSE, align = "lr")

  n100 * (n100 - 1) * (sum(G) - sum(diag(G))) / (N * (N - 1))
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(E100_exact, E100, tolerance = 1e-12)))
WarningThe root cause is the implicit base population

\(\mathbf{G}\) measures relatedness relative to the current population: its centering constant \(2\mathbf{p}\) is estimated from the individuals being measured, so the reference moves with the set. The diversity of a set needs a reference that does not depend on that set. Any matrix whose centering constant is estimated from the same data whose diversity you are measuring will have this problem, whatever it is called. The reference that \(\mathbf{f}\) uses is of a different kind, and Section 2.3 states what it is and what it is not.

2.3 Molecular coancestry

Malécot’s coancestry (Malécot 1948) between \(i\) and \(j\) is the probability that two alleles, one drawn from each individual at the same locus, are identical by descent relative to a base population (Section 1.2.3). Replacing identity by descent with identity by state – which is what markers actually observe – yields molecular coancestry (Caballero and Toro 2002, 292):

\[ f_{ij} \;=\; \frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m} \Big[\, x_{ik}\,x_{jk} \;+\; (1-x_{ik})(1-x_{jk}) \,\Big]. \tag{2.5}\]

The first term is the probability that both sampled alleles are the reference allele; the second, that both are the alternative. Three properties follow immediately:

  • \(f_{ij} \in [0, 1]\) always. A negative value is a symptom of the wrong matrix.
  • Self-coancestry, the diagonal element of \(\mathbf{f}\), is \(s_i = f_{ii} = (1 + F_i)/2\) (Section 1.2.3), with \(F_i\) the molecular inbreeding coefficient of
    1. What this chapter adds is that \(F_i\) is numerically the fraction of markers at which \(i\) is homozygous, which the chunk below checks; it is an individual quantity, not the population-level \(F_{hom}\) of
    2. For a fully heterozygous F1, \(s_i = 0.5\).
  • No centering and no scaling: no constant in \(\mathbf{f}\) is estimated from the set being measured. This is not the same as having no reference, as the paragraph “What the scale of \(\mathbf{f}\) is” below states.

What the scale of \(\mathbf{f}\) is. \(f_{ij}\) is identity in state, not an unbiased estimate of the identity-by-descent coancestry. Caballero and Toro (2002, 292) state that molecular and pedigree coancestry give the same result only if the base population has “an infinite number of loci … with all the alleles in each locus being different”, which biallelic markers cannot satisfy; Eding and Meuwissen (2001) report in their abstract a bias from alleles alike in state in the founder population. Under the assumptions declared in Section 1.2.3 for Equation 1.3 (neutral, unlinked loci, no mutation, a base population with allele frequencies \(p_{b,k}\)), \(\mathbb{E}[f_{ij}]\) is the pedigree coancestry \(A_{ij}/2\) plus \((1 - A_{ij}/2)\) times the base-population floor \(m^{-1}\sum_k (p_{b,k}^2 + q_{b,k}^2) \ge 1/2\). Unrelated individuals are therefore not expected at 0, and the minimum of \(\mathbf{f}\) (Table 2.4), above 0.5 for the book data, is consistent with that; the level of \(\mathbf{f}\) is set by the allele frequencies of the marker panel: the same individuals scored on another panel have another mean \(\mathbf{f}\). This is why, as in Section 1.2.3, \(\mathbf{f}\) is read as a comparison between sets measured on the same markers, and is not an absolute coancestry. What the construction avoids is a reference estimated from the set that is being compared, which is the failure of Section 2.2.

2.3.1 Computable form

Computed literally, with interpreted loops over pairs and markers, the double sum of Equation 2.5 costs \(O(n^2 m)\) scalar operations. Expanding,

\[ \sum_k \big[x_{ik}x_{jk} + (1-x_{ik})(1-x_{jk})\big] = 2\sum_k x_{ik}x_{jk} \;-\; \sum_k x_{ik} \;-\; \sum_k x_{jk} \;+\; m, \]

so, in matrix form,

\[ \mathbf{f} \;=\; \frac{1}{m}\Big( 2\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}' \;-\; \mathbf{X}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}' \;-\; \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}'\mathbf{X}' \;+\; m\,\mathbf{J} \Big), \tag{2.6}\]

with \(\mathbf{1}\) a vector of ones of conforming length (\(m\) or \(N\)) and \(\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}'\) the \(N \times N\) matrix of ones. This is a single matrix product. It is still \(O(n^2 m)\) – the same order and cost as building \(\mathbf{G}\) – and the gain over the literal sum is the constant factor of an optimised Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms (BLAS) product, not a lower order.

The level of \(\mathbf{f}\) is the panel floor, a quantity distinct from the base-population floor above. Summing Equation 2.5 over all \(N^2\) pairs at marker \(k\) gives \(N^2 (p_k^2 + q_k^2)\) exactly, with \(p_k\) the frequency in the \(N\) hybrids, so the mean of all entries of \(\mathbf{f}\) is the panel floor \(m^{-1}\sum_k (p_k^2 + q_k^2)\), which uses the frequencies of these \(N\) hybrids and not those of a base population (it is an identity of the set, not a measurement of the base-population floor), and the off-diagonal mean differs from it only by the diagonal. Recomputing \(\mathbf{f}\) on the same hybrids with a subset of the markers moves it (Table 2.5):

2.3.2 Reading the diagonal

The value 0.5 is the minimum of the diagonal, reached by a hybrid heterozygous at every marker. The excess above 0.5 is \(s_i - 0.5 = F_i/2\): half the fraction of markers at which the two parent lines carry the same allele. At those loci the F1 is homozygous and, in the absence of epistasis, contributes no dominance effect to heterosis.

Show code 
fd <- mean(diag(ctx[["f"]]))
F_i <- rowMeans(X != 0.5)                      # molecular inbreeding F_i
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(diag(f), (1 + F_i) / 2, tolerance = 1e-12)))
# heterozygous fraction = 1 - mean(F_i) = 1 - mean coancestry between A and B lines

fL_AB <- mean(fL[ctx[["pool"]] == "A", ctx[["pool"]] == "B"])
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(2 * (1 - fd), 1 - mean(F_i), tolerance = 1e-12)),
          isTRUE(all.equal(2 * (1 - fd), 1 - fL_AB, tolerance = 1e-12)))

The heterozygous_fraction of Table 2.6 is \(2(1 - \bar s)\), with \(\bar s\) the mean diagonal, that is \(1 - \bar F\) with \(\bar F\) the mean molecular \(F_i\). On the complete A \(\times\) B factorial of inbred lines (assumption A2) it equals one minus the mean coancestry between a pool-A and a pool-B line, \(1 - \bar f^{L}_{AB}\), because \(F_{(ab)} = f^{L}_{ab}\) for every cross (1); this is an identity of the realised factorial, not an expectation. It is not set by how far apart the heterotic pools are, provided the pools diverge neutrally from a common ancestral population in the Balding-Nichols model, with divergence parameter \(F_{BN}\) (fst in the code). Given the ancestral frequency \(p^{*}_k\) (Equation 6.1, stated later, in Chapter 6; a quantity of the draw, never observed, and not the frozen base \(p_{0,k}\) of the candidate set) the two pools’ frequencies are independent, each with mean \(p^{*}_k\), so the F1 is heterozygous at marker \(k\) with expected probability \(2p^{*}_k(1 - p^{*}_k)\) whatever \(F_{BN}\). What \(GD_{BS}\) gains, \(GD_T\) loses (Section 1.2, Section 5.4, Section 5.8). Chapter 6 measures the heterozygous fraction flat across its grid of \(F_{BN}\), one simulated pair of pools per point. Nothing is claimed here for divergence that is not neutral.

2.4 f is not an ad hoc substitute

Two published lines of work use this matrix, or an affine function of it, to manage diversity. This section reports what each says, and what each says against it, because it is otherwise easy to read \(\mathbf{f}\) as “the thing we used because \(\mathbf{G}\) broke”.

Nejati-Javaremi et al. (1997) proposed the allelic-similarity method, \(f_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{2}\delta_{kl}\) with \(\delta_{kl} = 1\) when the \(k\)-th allele of \(i\) equals the \(l\)-th of \(j\) (the formula as reproduced by Gómez-Romano et al. (2016), p. 3). At a biallelic locus that is the term in brackets of Equation 2.5, so the genome-wide mean over loci is the identity-by-state average of Equation 2.5.

Gómez-Romano et al. (2016), in their discussion, compare this matrix with matrices built from the cross-product of centred genotype scores, normalised or not by the allele frequency. They report that the values of such relationship estimates “range from -1 to x, where x can be substantially larger than 1”, so that they can lie outside the interval \([0, 1]\) valid for a coefficient of coancestry, and in the next sentence that “estimates of the average coancestry across the whole genome tend to be within the valid range” but that this may not hold for a small region with few genotyped single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). They add two objections: the cross-product of centred scores gives a pair homozygous for the minor allele a higher relationship than a pair homozygous for the most frequent allele, so carriers of rare alleles would be penalised in the optimisation, and centring makes the matrix non-invertible. For the allelic-similarity method they say that it has a “natural” interpretation in relation to the definition of coancestry, and that positive definiteness should hold. The guarantee of staying in \([0, 1]\) for \(\mathbf{f}\) is the present book’s inference from Equation 2.5, whose terms are probabilities, not a statement of the paper. The zero total of Equation 2.4 is not a range violation. It comes from centring on the sample frequencies and it implies \(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{Z}'\mathbf{1})/(2\sum_k p_k(1-p_k)) = \mathbf{0}\), so \(\mathbf{G}\) is singular, which is the non-invertibility the authors name.

The same matrix arrives, up to a fixed affine map, from a second direction. Meuwissen et al. (2020) (p. 3), citing Toro et al. (2014), state that using 0.5 as the base frequency for all loci, instead of the base-generation frequency \(p_{0,k}\), gives a relationship matrix – written there \(\mathbf{G}_{VR0.5}\) and here \(\mathbf{G}_{0.5}\) – that is “proportional to homozygosity and molecular coancestry”. The paper gives no formula or normalisation for it: the map below is derived in this chapter. With the 0/1/2 coding of Equation 2.1, \(2\sum_k p_k(1-p_k) = m/2\) at \(p_k = 0.5\) and

\[ \mathbf{G}_{0.5} \;=\; \frac{(\mathbf{M}^{H} - \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}') (\mathbf{M}^{H} - \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}')'}{m/2} \;=\; 4\,\mathbf{f} - 2\,\mathbf{J}, \tag{2.7}\]

with \(\mathbf{J}\) as in Equation 2.6. \(\mathbf{G}_{0.5}\) is therefore an affine function of \(\mathbf{f}\), not \(\mathbf{f}\) itself: its entries lie in \([-2, 2]\), negative exactly where \(f_{ij} < 0.5\); on the book data every \(f_{ij}\) exceeds \(0.5\), so the range in Table 2.7 is positive. For a vector of contributions \(\mathbf{c}\) with \(\mathbf{1}'\mathbf{c} = 1\), \(\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{J}\mathbf{c} = 1\) and \(\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{G}_{0.5}\mathbf{c} = 4\,\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{c} - 2\), so under that normalisation the two matrices order selections identically. Replacing \(2p_k\) with \(1\) removes the population-dependent term that forced \(\mathbf{1}'\mathbf{G}\mathbf{1} = 0\).

The constant that Equation 2.7 removes is \(\mathbf{G}_{0.5} = 4(\mathbf{f} - \tfrac12\mathbf{J})\): one half is the base-population floor of Equation 1.3 for a base at \(p_{b,k} = 0.5\) at every marker, since then \(p_{b,k}^2 + q_{b,k}^2 = 1/2\). It is a convention fixed in advance, not an estimate from the set being measured, and that is why \(\mathbf{1}'\mathbf{G}_{0.5}\mathbf{1}\) does not vanish. It is not the panel floor of the marker panel at hand, which is at least \(1/2\) and is generally larger, so \(\mathbf{G}_{0.5}\) is not zero for unrelated individuals either.

So, under normalised contributions, \(\mathbf{f}\) orders selections exactly as \(\mathbf{G}_{0.5}\), the VanRaden matrix centred at the fixed frequency \(0.5\). The two derivations of this section, the allelic-similarity route and the fixed-base route, are independent, and both land on the same matrix up to Equation 2.7. That is a consistency between two published treatments and this chapter’s algebra; it is not a proof that \(\mathbf{f}\) is the right matrix to constrain.

A limit of constraining this matrix. The sources of this section state one. Meuwissen et al. (2020) classify the molecular kinship matrix as homozygosity-based and report, in their abstract, that managing inbreeding with a homozygosity-based relationship “resulted in allele frequency changes toward 0.5 giving a low rate of increase in homozygosity for the panel used for management, but not for unmanaged neutral loci, at the expense of a high genetic drift”. In their Methods they state that with \(\mathbf{G}_{0.5}\) in optimal contributions “there will be pressure to move allele-frequencies toward 0.5”, with the covariance between the allele-frequency change and the base frequency negative (their Supplementary Information 1). Gómez-Romano et al. (2016) reach the same direction by intuition, not by a measurement, for allelic-similarity matrices, and say that the choice between matrices depends on the objective. A constraint on \(\theta_S\) computed from \(\mathbf{f}\) pins the homozygosity lens only; it does not pin \(F_{drift}\), which is computed from the allele frequencies against the frozen base (Section 1.2.3 for the scope, Chapter 10 for \(F_{hom}\), \(F_{drift}\) and their gap). The consequence of that gap for this book’s selections is measured where those metrics are reported, not here.

2.5 The division of labour

Table 2.8 sets the two matrices side by side.

This split is the convention throughout hybdiv: the coancestry-based diversity metrics (theta_group() and those built on it, gene_diversity() and status_number()) take ctx[["f"]], and ctx[["G"]] appears only where traits are predicted. The frequency-based metrics (theta_from_freq(), he_nei() and the He, alleles_lost, F_hom and F_drift of freq_metrics()) are computed from colMeans(ctx[["X"]][idx, ]) and the frozen base frequencies ctx[["p0"]]; they never read ctx[["f"]], so a substitution of \(\mathbf{G}\) for \(\mathbf{f}\) cannot reach them (Section 1.2.3). tests/testthat/test-metrics.R asserts that every element of ctx[["f"]] lies in \([0, 1]\), which fails if a centered matrix is stored in its place; that \(|\sum \mathbf{G}| < 10^{-6} N^2\), the centering identity of Equation 2.4 up to floating-point tolerance; and that theta_group() over the whole population returns the mean of \(\mathbf{f}\), a value well away from the zero that \(\mathbf{G}\) would give, so a switch to \(\mathbf{G}\) inside theta_group(), and hence in the metrics built on it, fails the suite. Metrics that read ctx[["f"]] directly, without going through theta_group(), are not covered by that assertion.

Chapter 3 takes \(\mathbf{f}\) and asks what happens when the population it describes is not one population, but several.
