11  Which crosses, at which locations? Joint sparse allocation

Chapter 9 chose which crosses to make from two pools, with the field design held fixed. Chapter 10 chose which locations each hybrid goes to, with the whole factorial taken as given. A programme makes both decisions out of one budget, in the same season, and each changes what the other can do: a cross that is not made cannot be planted anywhere, and a cross planted in one location says nothing about the others except through its parents. This chapter treats the two as one decision.

The search reported in Section 11.3 found strong methods for each half but none that chooses the crosses and their locations together for a two-pool hybrid programme – an absence of evidence from one search, not a proof of absence. The nearest treatments optimise the crosses and leave the environments to the trial manager (Heslot and Feoktistov 2020; Kadam et al. 2021), or optimise the environments for a fixed entry list (Cullis et al. 2020; Montesinos-López et al. 2022). This is the second gap of Section 9.9, and it needs the female x male x location connectivity metric Section 10.13 asked for.

The results this chapter defends:

  1. At a fixed budget, distinct crosses beat locations per cross for predicting all hybrids. Spending 160 plots as 160 crosses at one location each beat 80 at two, which beat 40 at four, in direction in every scenario simulated, by a margin that grows with SCA; not every step is resolved (Section 11.5). For the crosses that were made the order reverses, less firmly, so the split of the budget is a statement about what will be selected.
  2. Allocation strategy is a second-order decision. With the plan and the locations per cross fixed, a placement that keeps as much of both pools as possible in each location raises parent coverage substantially; its accuracy gain over random placement of the same plan, +0.006 correlation points on the point estimate, is not detected.
  3. The PEV criterion ranks plans, not placements. The mean PEV of GCA, computable before planting, orders the budget splits much as accuracy does. An exchange search on it, stopped at 600 proposals before convergence, lowered PEV by a fraction of a percent and bought no detected accuracy.

11.1 Why the two decisions are one

Write \(n_F\) and \(n_M\) for the female and male lines – the pool-A and pool-B candidates, \(n_A\) and \(n_B\), of Chapter 9 – \(n_c\) for the number of distinct crosses made, \(L\) for the locations, \(k\) for the number of locations each cross is planted in (as in Chapter 10; not the testers per pool of Chapter 9, which appear below only in the label tester k=2), and \(r\) for plots per cross per location. The field budget is

\[ B = n_c \, k \, r . \]

Chapter 9 varied \(n_c\) (through the crosses per line \(c\), \(n_c = c\,n_F\) for a circulant plan) at fixed \(k\); Chapter 10 varied \(k\) and the layout at fixed \(n_c = n_F n_M\). At \(r = 1\), which is the stage-1 norm, fixing \(B\) ties the two together: halving the crosses doubles the locations each one can see. The nearest earlier look at this trade is result 6 of Section 10.11.3, which at equal plots compared testing every candidate at few locations against a smaller factorial at more, and found the first better. There the excluded candidates are a smaller factorial, so their parents leave the data, and a second stage with selection follows. Here every line stays in the data, the unmade crosses are predicted from it, and the comparison is of stage-1 accuracy alone. Result 1 below points the same way as result 6.

The two terms buy different things. More crosses put more lines into data and sample more of the factorial, which is what predicts the crosses never made (Section 9.6; Melchinger et al. (2023)). More locations per cross average that cross’s genotype x location deviations, \((g^f\ell)\), \((g^m\ell)\) and \((s\ell)\) in the model below, over more environments, which is what sharpens the estimate of a cross that was made. That is the expectation under the model of Section 11.2, not a result of Chapter 10: its result 2 held the locations per hybrid fixed while widening the trial (the difference from raising \(k\) is drawn in Section 11.5), and its result 6 measured selection after two stages, not the estimate of each made cross. A parent’s GCA is averaged over the locations of all its crosses, which an allocation can widen without adding locations per cross. Which term matters more depends on the SCA:GCA variance ratio, the genotype x location share of the genetic variance and how well the relationship matrix links lines across families – the quantities a programme knows least about at stage 1.

11.2 The joint model

The model is Chapter 10’s, unchanged:

\[ y_{ijl} = \mu + \ell_l + g^f_i + g^m_j + s_{ij} + (g^f\ell)_{il} + (g^m\ell)_{jl} + (s\ell)_{ijl} + e_{ijl}, \]

where \(g^f_i\), \(g^m_j\) and \(s_{ij}\) are the \(\mathrm{GCA}^f_i\), \(\mathrm{GCA}^m_j\) and \(\mathrm{SCA}_{ij}\) of Section 10.2, \(\ell_l\) is the fixed effect of location \(l\), \(g^f \sim N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf A_f\sigma^2_f)\), \(g^m \sim N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf A_m\sigma^2_m)\), \(s \sim N(\mathbf 0, (\mathbf A_f \otimes \mathbf A_m)\sigma^2_s)\), and the three interactions with location carry the same kernels crossed with the identity \(\mathbf I_L\) over locations: \((g^f\ell) \sim N(\mathbf 0, (\mathbf A_f \otimes \mathbf I_L)\sigma^2_{f\ell})\), \((g^m\ell) \sim N(\mathbf 0, (\mathbf A_m \otimes \mathbf I_L)\sigma^2_{m\ell})\), \((s\ell) \sim N(\mathbf 0, (\mathbf A_f \otimes \mathbf A_m \otimes \mathbf I_L)\sigma^2_{s\ell})\), and \(e_{ijl} \sim N(0, \sigma^2_e)\) i.i.d. In sparse_vc() these are s2f, s2m, s2s, s2fl, s2ml, s2sl and s2e. The target is the value in the target population of environments, \(g^f_i + g^m_j + s_{ij}\).

One simplification is inherited from Chapter 10. With one plot per cross per location, \((s\ell)_{ijl}\) and \(e_{ijl}\) have the same incidence, but the simulator draws \((s\ell)\) with the kernel above, correlated between related crosses in one location, so a model could carry it as a structured term. The analysis model does not: it absorbs it into an i.i.d. residual of variance \(\sigma^2_{s\ell} + \sigma^2_e\) (vc$s2res). The BLUP and every PEV below are therefore exact for the analysis model, not for the generating one. With known components the cost is measured directly, by a literal GLS that carries the structured kernel, in the configuration of Table 11.3.

Show code 
# Literal GLS BLUP of the target g^f + g^m + s, with (s l) given the kernel
# Ksl over the hybrids within a location: A_f x A_m as simulated, or I as
# analysed. Circulant c = 4, 20 x 20 half-sib pools, 8 locations, k = 2,
# plot h2 = 0.4; 30 paired replicates per SCA level.
ms <- local({
  n <- 20; nLoc <- 8; A <- relmat_halfsib(n, 5); AA <- kronecker(A, A)
  pf <- rep(seq_len(n), each = n); pm <- rep(seq_len(n), n)    # male fastest
  set.seed(41)
  M <- joint_alloc(design_circulant(n, n, c = 4), n, n, nLoc, k = 2,
                   strategy = "coverage")
  gls <- function(ph, vc, Ksl) {
    f <- pf[ph$hyb]; m <- pm[ph$hyb]; h <- ph$hyb
    same <- outer(ph$loc, ph$loc, "==")
    V <- vc$s2f * A[f, f] + vc$s2m * A[m, m] + vc$s2s * AA[h, h] +
      same * (vc$s2fl * A[f, f] + vc$s2ml * A[m, m] + vc$s2sl * Ksl[h, h]) +
      diag(vc$s2e, nrow(ph))
    X <- model.matrix(~ factor(ph$loc)); Vi <- solve(V)
    b <- solve(crossprod(X, Vi %*% X), crossprod(X, Vi %*% ph$y))
    C <- vc$s2f * A[pf, f] + vc$s2m * A[pm, m] + vc$s2s * AA[, h]
    drop(C %*% Vi %*% (ph$y - X %*% b))
  }
  vapply(c(low = "low", high = "high"), function(sca) {
    vc <- sparse_vc(0.4, sca)
    set.seed(42)
    d <- vapply(seq_len(30), function(r) {
      s <- sparse_simulate(M, n, n, vc, A, A)
      mis <- gls(s$pheno, vc, diag(n * n))
      # The I-kernel GLS is the analysis model: it must equal sparse_blup().
      if (r == 1) stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(mis,
        sparse_blup(s$pheno, n, n, nLoc, vc, A, A)$blup, tolerance = 1e-8)))
      cor(gls(s$pheno, vc, AA), s$g_target) - cor(mis, s$g_target)
    }, 0)
    c(diff = mean(d), se = sd(d) / sqrt(length(d)))
  }, c(diff = 0, se = 0))
})
signif(ms, 2)   # accuracy, correct minus analysed kernel, over all hybrids
#>           low     high
#> diff -1.8e-05 -5.7e-05
#> se    1.9e-05  5.0e-05

Carrying the structured kernel changes accuracy over all hybrids by -0.00002 (SE 0.00002) at low SCA and -0.00006 (SE 0.00005) at high SCA: nothing detectable, because \(\sigma^2_{s\ell}\) is a small share of the residual (5 % and 14 %) and half-sib relationships of 0.25 give the kernel off-diagonals of at most 0.25. The check covers one plan at \(h^2 = 0.4\); stronger relationships or a larger \(\sigma^2_{s\ell}\) would need it repeated.

What is new is only the incidence. A crossing plan \(P \subset \{1..n_F\} \times \{1..n_M\}\) and an allocation give a 0/1 matrix \(M\) of size \(n_F n_M \times L\) whose row is zero when \((i, j) \notin P\) and sums to \(k\) otherwise. The R/17 functions take such an \(M\) directly; unmade crosses are predicted through \(\mathbf A_f\), \(\mathbf A_m\) and the GCA they share with made crosses – the CV1 prediction of Section 10.4 (T2 of Section 9.6 when both parents are in made crosses), produced by the plan rather than by a mask.

11.3 What the literature offers

Two searches were run: in October 2026 the Consensus index (Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex, PubMed), Crossref and a general web search, reaching bioRxiv and the Crop Science / Plant Genome literature; on 20 October 2026, PubMed directly. Terms combined sparse testing, multi-environment trial, allocation of genotypes / hybrids to environments, incomplete factorial, mating design, training set optimisation, CDmean, A-optimality and genetic relatedness, with and without hybrid and maize, then genotype-based allocation (stratified or representative allocation, population structure in training-set and trial design, anticlustering). Nine families emerge (Table 11.1); none was built for the joint problem and each supplies a piece. The training-set optimisation tools of Akdemir et al. (2021) and Isidro y Sanchez and Akdemir (2021) could in principle select genotype-environment pairs, but none of the papers found does so for a crossing plan.

Optimising the field for a given entry list (rows 1, 2, 5, 6) supplies the criterion. Butler et al. (2014) and Cullis et al. (2020) optimise the A-optimality of the predicted genetic effects under the mixed model that will analyse the trial, relationship matrix included, and beat designs built for unrelated entries in silico; Lisle et al. (2021) diagnose whether a multi-environment design can estimate its variance parameters at all; Rio et al. (2022) extend CDmean (Rincent et al. 2012) to G x E models, so reliability can be forecast before planting. The criterion of this chapter is that framework restricted to GCA. Incomplete-block allocation of lines to locations beat random allocation by 1.4 to 26.5 % in mean squared error (Montesinos-López et al. 2022) and was best with random close behind (Osval A. Montesinos-López et al. 2023); two of four allocation methods were slightly better, with accuracy barely lower at a 15:85 training-testing split (O. A. Montesinos-López et al. 2023); and at equal environments per line incomplete blocks were less variable than random (Montesinos-López et al. 2024) – a worst-case property a mean accuracy does not show. Lell et al. (2021) (1,604 wheat hybrids) found equal subgroups in overlapping subsets of environments best; Mothukuri et al. (2025) guided the split by relatedness to fully tested maize lines, with a weak correlation (0.20 to 0.31) between relatedness and accuracy. The overlap question is Section 10.6’s (Jarquin et al. 2020; Crespo-Herrera et al. 2021; Lubanga et al. 2025; Atanda et al. 2022). Which environments to sample is a further choice: sets chosen with a crop-growth-model criterion estimated wheat phenology parameters better than random sets twice as large (Rincent et al. 2017), mega-environmental allocation reported a 40 % gain in response for the same resources (González-Barrios et al. 2019), and Hoefler and Gutierrez (2026) optimise its replication and sparsity by simulation. The locations here are exchangeable, which a real target population of environments is not.

Choosing entries and crosses for given environments (rows 3, 4, 7) supplies the search and the coverage logic. Kadam et al. (2021) show that optimising PEV or CDmean over all possible single crosses, not only those made, gives a better training set; Heslot and Feoktistov (2020), the closest methodological precedent, choose which maize hybrids to create and phenotype by mixed binary-integer differential evolution, extended to designing a connected population before crossing; Guo et al. (2019) chose maize, wheat and rice hybrid training sets from the parents’ genotypes, beating random and PEV- or CD-optimised sets with 2 to 13 % of the hybrids. Endelman et al. (2014) asked the budget question within fixed biparental barley and maize families, choosing no cross: an unbalanced design over several locations predicted better than every entry in one, and up to 250 yield-plot equivalents per family the optimum did not genotype more progeny than were phenotyped (its relation to result 1 is in Section 11.5). Sparse factorials train hybrid prediction at least as well as tester designs, and fewer crosses per line favours the lines never crossed (Seye et al. 2020; Lorenzi et al. 2022; Melchinger et al. 2023). González-Diéguez et al. (2026) instead spread three to five testers sparsely among full-sibs (sparse testcrossing), which beat a single tester at an equal number of testcrosses and was matched, not beaten, by an approximate incomplete factorial (Section 10.8). Thomsen et al. (2026) is observational: sparse, unreplicated multi-environment data from a commercial winter oilseed rape hybrid programme, covering 1.67 % of mother x father combinations, which a GCA + SCA model still predicted (Section 9.4). Schoemaker et al. (2024), trading testers against environments in maize, found three to five well-chosen environments match ten random ones – the closest empirical look at one corner of the joint budget.

Representing genetic structure (rows 8, 9) supplies the balancing objective. For training sets, stratified sampling suited strong structure (rice) and CDmean, or CDmean within subpopulations, mild structure [wheat, all traits but test weight and heading date; Isidro et al. (2015)]; uniform sampling of the genetic space beat stratified and random sampling and matched maximising CD in maize and wheat, with all methods alike in strongly structured rice (Bustos-Korts et al. 2016); and stratified sampling with a marker-effect-weighted relationship matrix was best for forward prediction (Berro et al. 2019). Ou and Liao (2019) derived a new optimality criterion from Pearson’s correlation between the genomic estimated breeding values and the phenotypic values of a test set, under a ridge-regression whole-genome model, applicable to data with varying degrees of population structure; they illustrate it on rice (strong structure) and wheat (mild structure), implement it in the R package TSDFGS, and report it advantageous over existing methods. Akdemir and Isidro-Sánchez (2019) compared targeted training sets, optimised using the genotypes of the test set, with untargeted ones, and found the targeted sets gave the highest accuracies. Core Hunter (De Beukelaer et al. 2018) selects subsets on allele-frequency and distance criteria. All of these choose which candidates to phenotype.

Representation per environment has one precedent. Atanda et al. (2021) chose by CDmean which members of each full-sib family go to each environment, so that every biparental population is represented everywhere (their CV2), against predicting new populations tested in some environments only (CV1). Accuracy was similar. Their case for CV2 is about the data (every family phenotyped everywhere, a complete historical record), and their gain from grouping environments by growing and management conditions is a finding about the environments. Nor is CV1 against CV2 a controlled test of representation: the two differ in what is predicted (new populations against the unphenotyped half of phenotyped families) as well as in how families are spread. The formal object behind balancing is k-means anticlustering (Papenberg and Klau 2021), partitioning a set into groups of prescribed size that are as alike as possible.

Why balance at all is therefore this chapter’s own inference, with no direct empirical test in either direction. Genomic prediction across a maize hybrid diversity panel came mostly from differences in mean between breeding populations [predicting each from the others gave 0.12 for grain yield; Windhausen et al. (2012)], so subgroup means are much of what is predicted. Under the model of Section 11.2, with fixed location effects and a connected design, concentrating one subgroup’s crosses in a few locations does not bias the BLUP of its mean; it inflates the PEV of contrasts between subgroups, which pass through the effects of locations they do not share. Bias needs a subgroup x location interaction the analysis model does not carry (the zero-mean GCA x location terms do not represent one). Against the presumption, Fernandez-Gonzalez et al. (2023) found in a benchmark of training-set optimisers that a diverse training set made prediction robust to structure and clustering information was less effective. None of these papers allocates a crossing plan.

The gap is therefore precise. The criteria (PEV and CD with relatedness) and the search methods (exchange algorithms, differential evolution) exist, and each half has been optimised separately. Missing is the object that holds the crossing plan and the allocation under one budget, and evidence on how to split it.

11.4 Design criteria computable before planting

Two quantities can be read off \(M\) before a seed is sown.

The mean PEV of GCA. The left-hand side of the mixed-model equations depends on the design alone, so the prediction error variance of every GCA effect is known before any phenotype exists. joint_pev() returns its mean over the \(n_F + n_M\) GCA effects: the A-optimality criterion of Butler et al. (2014) restricted to the effects parents are selected on, exact for the analysis model of Section 11.2 with known components. Table 11.2 shows it ranking allocations of one plan. It is blind to SCA and to the difference between predicting made and unmade crosses.

Two component counts. A single graph with females, males and locations as nodes is the obvious female x male x location metric, and it is wrong: a location node joins every parent planted there, so at one location it reports one component for any plan, two disconnected NC II blocks included. A shared location links only sums of GCA. joint_connectivity() therefore returns two counts alongside the R/17 diagnostics:

  • n_comp_cross, the components of the female x male graph of the crosses that receive a plot, pooled over locations. GCA is a main effect shared by every location, so this is what links GCA contrasts by data, and at one location it is exactly the bipartite count of Section 9.2.3.
  • n_comp_loc, the components of the location graph in which two locations are joined when they share a parent. Location contrasts are linked by data only when it is 1.

Both equal to 1 is necessary, not sufficient: a relationship matrix can link families the data do not, and shared parents link locations only through those parents’ GCA. This pair is the female x male x location metric Section 10.13 asked for.

11.4.1 Allele frequencies per location

Neither criterion above reads a genotype. A plan can be connected and low in PEV and still send most crosses of one subgroup to a few locations, which costs precision in subgroup contrasts, or bias under a subgroup x location interaction the model omits (Section 11.3 gives the argument and the evidence on both sides). The quantity that sees the imbalance is computed from the hybrids’ marker genotypes, and its algebra is exact.

The decomposition. Let \(\mathbf X_P\) be the hybrid marker matrix of the planted plots: one row \(\mathbf x_u\) per plot \(u\) (a cross planted at \(k\) locations appears \(k\) times) and \(N_{SNP}\) markers coded 0/0.5/1 as everywhere in this book (the symbol of Section 12.3.2, since \(m\) marks the male pool). It has \(N_P = n_c k r\) rows, the budget in plots. Write \(n_l\) for the plots at location \(l\), \(\bar{\mathbf p}_l\) for their allele frequencies (column means) and \(\bar{\mathbf p}\) for those of all planted plots. Summing the one-way analysis-of-variance identity over markers,

\[ \sum_u \lVert \mathbf x_u - \bar{\mathbf p} \rVert^2 = \sum_l \sum_{u \in l} \lVert \mathbf x_u - \bar{\mathbf p}_l \rVert^2 + SS_B, \qquad SS_B = \sum_l n_l \lVert \bar{\mathbf p}_l - \bar{\mathbf p} \rVert^2 , \]

with \(SS_B\) the between-location sum of squares, summed over markers.

The plan and \(k\) fix the rows of \(\mathbf X_P\), hence the left-hand side; an allocation only decides which location each row goes to. Minimising \(SS_B\) is therefore maximising the within-location sum, the k-means objective, which is what Papenberg and Klau (2021) call k-means anticlustering: groups of prescribed size (which carries the loads \(n_l\)) made as alike as possible. What their formulation does not carry is the item structure: here each cross enters as exactly \(k\) copies that must go to \(k\) different locations, whereas anticlustering assigns every item once and independently.

On \(\mathbf X_P\), \(SS_B/(N_P N_{SNP})\) is the plot-weighted mean squared deviation of a location’s allele frequencies from the pooled ones. On a 0/1 matrix \(\mathbf Z\) with one column per subgroup (a family, a heterotic subgroup, a female-family x male-family class) the column means are subgroup shares, and minimising \(SS_B\) equalises them. The two objectives differ; Table 11.3 measures both.

The diversity lens. The gene diversity of location \(l\)’s plots, \(GD_l = 1 - \theta_l\) over the molecular coancestry of Section 1.3, is \(\frac{1}{N_{SNP}} \sum_v 2\bar p_{lv}(1 - \bar p_{lv})\) over markers \(v\), Nei’s gene diversity at the location’s frequencies (Section 14.2; Nei (1973)). Because \(2p(1-p)\) is quadratic in \(p\), the decomposition carries over exactly:

\[ \sum_l \frac{n_l}{N_P}\, GD_l \;=\; GD_P - \frac{2\,SS_B}{N_P\, N_{SNP}}, \]

with \(GD_P\) the gene diversity of all planted plots. Take the locations as the subpopulations of Chapter 2 and weight them, as Caballero and Toro (2002) weight theirs, by their share \(N_i/N_T = n_l/N_P\) of the plots. Then \(GD_P\) is \(GD_T = 1 - \bar f\), the left-hand side is \(GD_{WS} = 1 - \tilde f\), and

\[ \frac{2\,SS_B}{N_P\, N_{SNP}} = GD_{BS} = \tilde f - \bar f \]

exactly, for any loads; it is also the \(\bar D\) in Chapter 2’s \(F_{ST}\), and Nei’s \(D_{ST} = H_T - H_S\) (Nei 1973) with \(H_S\) the plot-weighted mean of the location diversities (the plain mean under equal loads). \(SS_B\) differs from all of these only by the factor \(N_P N_{SNP}/2\). Between heterotic pools this component is the heterosis engine, to be preserved; between the locations of one trial it is a nuisance, to be minimised.

The base. \(SS_B\) is centred on \(\bar{\mathbf p}\), the planted set’s own frequencies, not on a frozen base \(\mathbf p_0\) (Section 12.7.1). Against \(\mathbf p_0\) – here the frequencies of the complete factorial of candidate lines –

\[ \sum_l n_l \lVert \bar{\mathbf p}_l - \mathbf p_0 \rVert^2 = SS_B + N_P \lVert \bar{\mathbf p} - \mathbf p_0 \rVert^2 , \]

and the second term is fixed by the crossing plan, so with the plan fixed the two bases rank allocations identically. That term is the plan’s departure from the pool in the drift lens: \(\lVert \bar{\mathbf p} - \mathbf p_0 \rVert^2\) is the numerator of \(F_{drift}\) (Section 12.7.2; Meuwissen et al. (2020)) before each marker is standardised by \(p_{0}(1 - p_{0})\), with the planted set in the place of cycle \(t\). It is not a loss of gene diversity, which the book keeps in the other lens: per marker \(GD(p_0) - GD(\bar p) = 2(p_0 - \bar p)(1 - p_0 - \bar p)\), whose sign depends on which side of \(1/2\) the two frequencies sit. \(SS_B\) belongs to the allocation, the drift term to the crossing plan (Section 9.11).

Table 11.3 computes all of this for one plan at the scale of the sweep below, under four allocations: random; coverage; an exchange search that swaps two plots between locations when \(SS_B\) falls (loads and \(k\) unchanged); and the same search on \([\mathbf X_P,\; w\mathbf Z]\), the markers with the female-family x male-family indicators appended and \(w\) set so that both blocks carry the same total sum of squares over the planted plots. Columns: between_pct, \(100\,SS_B\) over the marker sum of squares; rms_dp, \(\sqrt{SS_B/(N_P N_{SNP})}\); gd_short_pct, the shortfall of the plot-weighted mean \(GD_l\) from \(GD_P\), in % of \(GD_P\); groups_between_pct, between_pct on \(\mathbf Z\); families and family_pairs, the share of each pool’s parent families, and of the plan’s family classes, present per location; fem_cover and pev_gca as in Table 11.2. The chunk asserts the decomposition, the \(GD\) identity on \(\mathbf f\) and \(GD_{BS} = 2\,SS_B/(N_P N_{SNP})\) for every allocation, and the change of base, which has power only on the random sparse plan checked after the table (a circulant plan uses every line equally often, so its drift term is zero). No accuracy is computed.

# Swap the locations of two plots when SS_B of Y falls: loads and k fixed.
# Y has one row per hybrid, as Xh; Y = Xh balances marker frequencies.
freq_balance <- function(M, Y, n_iter = 5000) {
  cl <- which(M == 1L, arr.ind = TRUE)
  S <- rowsum(Y[cl[, 1], , drop = FALSE], cl[, 2]); n <- colSums(M)
  for (it in seq_len(n_iter)) {
    i <- sample.int(nrow(cl), 2); x <- cl[i[1], 1]; y <- cl[i[2], 1]
    a <- cl[i[1], 2]; b <- cl[i[2], 2]
    if (a == b || M[x, b] == 1L || M[y, a] == 1L) next
    d <- Y[y, ] - Y[x, ]
    if (sum((S[a, ] + d)^2 - S[a, ]^2) / n[a] + sum((S[b, ] - d)^2 - S[b, ]^2) / n[b] < 0) {
      S[a, ] <- S[a, ] + d; S[b, ] <- S[b, ] - d
      M[x, c(a, b)] <- c(0L, 1L); M[y, c(b, a)] <- c(0L, 1L)
      cl[i, 2] <- c(b, a)
    }
  }
  M
}

11.5 What the simulation finds

The sweep crosses six configurations that all spend 160 plots (the tester design 152) – circulant plans with 8, 4 and 2 crosses per line at 1, 2 and 4 locations per cross, random sparse plans with 8 and 4, and a two-tester design at two locations – with the allocation strategies, two plot heritabilities and two SCA levels (sparse_vc(): SCA a quarter of, or equal to, a GCA variance; genotype x location half of each main effect, so a genetic correlation of 2/3 between locations). Pools are 20 x 20 in half-sib families of four; eight locations; 30 replicates per cell.

Comparisons between strategies are paired; those between configurations are not. Within one configuration and scenario each replicate draws one plan, random and coverage allocate it, and both are simulated from the same seed, so they share the true genetic values (sparse_simulate() draws every effect before it looks at the allocation). Each configuration draws its own seeds, so the budget-split comparisons of result 1 are between independent cells, with both cells’ standard errors combined. The exchange search is too slow to repeat per replicate: per cell one plan gets a coverage allocation (start) and a search from it, and those two fixed designs are simulated in every replicate with the same seed, pairing optimised with its own start.

The evidence bar. A difference is detected past two standard errors of the difference. Chapter 9 calls a difference resolved only past three; a quantity between two and three is flagged where it appears.

11.5.4 Limitations of the simulation

  • Known variance components. The criterion and the BLUP use the true values; REML on a sparse trial is the weak point of Section 10.11.3 (result 5).
  • A misspecified analysis model: \((s\ell)\) is absorbed into an i.i.d. residual, checked for one plan only (Section 11.2).
  • Exchangeable locations, genetic correlation 2/3 between every pair. Mega-environment structure (González-Barrios et al. 2019; Hoefler and Gutierrez 2026) would favour allocations that sample each mega-environment, which the coverage rule does not see.
  • Half-sib relationships, one plot per cell, no markers, spatial trend or replicated checks in the sweep; the marker term of Section 11.4.1 comes from a separate live simulation, not scored for accuracy.
  • One budget, one scale: 160 plots, 20 x 20 pools, eight locations.
  • Accuracy, not response. Selection intensity differs between 40 and 160 candidates and is not modelled.

11.6 Practical recommendations

TipA checklist for a joint crossing and testing plan
  1. Fix the budget in plots, \(B = n_c k r\), and the locations available.
  2. Decide what will be selected. Parents on GCA, or any single cross by prediction: favour distinct crosses (result 1). Only crosses already made: more locations per cross estimate them better, at a cost in selection intensity.
  3. Screen candidate splits with the mean PEV of GCA (joint_pev()), using the previous cycle’s relationship matrix and components, for the GCA / all-hybrid objective only.
  4. Treat one location per cross with caution. It was best with known components; REML has little to work with at one plot per cross (Section 10.11.3, result 5), so two locations per cross is the safer floor.
  5. Place crosses with the coverage rule (joint_alloc(strategy = "coverage")): it costs nothing and raises parent coverage, though its accuracy gain was not detected (result 2). With known subgroups, read the between-location shares of markers and subgroup indicators before planting, and balance on the indicators if subgroup shares must be equal; neither marker nor indicator balancing has been shown to raise accuracy (Section 11.4.1, including its limits).
  6. Check n_comp_cross == 1 and n_comp_loc == 1 before planting. A disconnected plan needs a new plan, not a new allocation.
  7. Reserve exchange optimisation of the placement for tester designs, as provisional (result 3), and run it well beyond 600 proposals.

11.7 Open problems

  • A joint optimiser over the plan itself. Here the plan family was enumerated and only the placement optimised. Searching crosses and locations in one binary space is the problem Heslot and Feoktistov (2020) solved for crosses alone.
  • A criterion that sees SCA and the selection objective: one on the target values of the candidates that will actually be selected among, with intensity.
  • Non-exchangeable environments. Joining mega-environments (González-Barrios et al. 2019) or a choice of environments (Rincent et al. 2017) to a crossing plan, and finding the genetic correlation between locations at which the optimum moves toward more locations per cross.
  • Estimated components. How much of the budget split survives REML, and whether the PEV screen should use a prior or the previous cycle’s estimates (Lisle et al. 2021).
  • Seed as a constraint. A cross planted at \(k\) locations needs \(k\) plots of seed; cross_cost() (Section 9.7) has not been joined to the allocation.
  • Frequency-balanced allocation and accuracy. A test of Section 11.4.1’s criterion (Papenberg and Klau 2021) needs pools whose subgroups differ in mean, as the breeding populations of Windhausen et al. (2012) did, and in genotype x location; an analysis model without that interaction; estimated components; a sweep over \(w\); and a paired comparison against the coverage rule. Why the answer should not be presumed is in Section 11.3.
  • Prospective validation. No trial has been designed jointly sparse from the start and compared against a conventional plan.

11.8 Reproducibility and assumptions

Rscript book/scripts/regenerate.R joint writes joint_core_sweep.csv (cell means and standard errors), joint_contrasts.csv (paired contrasts) and joint_optimiser.csv (the exchange-search trace) to book/data in about 30 minutes of CPU, two thirds of it in the exchange searches, and keeps the per-replicate results in report/joint_raw.rds (gitignored). Table 11.2, Table 11.3 and the misspecification check of Section 11.2 run at render time and assert their identities. tests/testthat/test-joint.R checks that:

  • every made cross gets exactly \(k\) plots and every unmade cross none;
  • the coverage rule balances loads and covers more of each pool than random placement;
  • joint_pev() equals the GCA block of the explicitly inverted coefficient matrix of sparse_blup();
  • the optimised allocation never scores worse than its start, whether the start is implicit or passed in;
  • n_comp_cross is two for NC II blocks at one location, in separate locations and in shared locations, and equals the bipartite count of design_components(); n_comp_loc is two only when the blocks are grown apart.

The numbers are findings of this configuration, not constants: they move with the budget, the pool sizes, the genetic correlation between locations and the SCA share.

11.9 Where this connects

  • Chapter 9 chose the crosses with the field fixed and Chapter 10 the field with the crosses fixed; under one budget the crossing plan is the larger lever.
  • The coverage rule is result 4 of Section 10.11.3 – parent coverage, not shared hybrids, carries connectivity – turned into an allocation rule.
  • Distinct crosses keep more lines in the data, the diversity argument of Section 9.11: a line never crossed is one the next cycle will in practice not use (Chapter 16; Chapter 22).
  • The PEV screen is the mixed-model machinery of Chapter 17, read before planting instead of after harvest.
  • The frequency term of Section 11.4.1 is the partition of Chapter 2 with locations in place of pools.

This closes the Part. The crossing plan and its field decide which hybrids, and which lines, are seen at all; the Metrics part that follows measures the diversity of the hybrids a programme has, starting with Chapter 12.
