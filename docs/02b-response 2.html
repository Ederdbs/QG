3  Response to selection: the other half of the objective

Every chapter so far measures what selection spends. None of them writes down what it buys.

That asymmetry is not cosmetic. Chapter 19 will show a constrained programme overtaking an unconstrained one after eight cycles, and the book can report that number because it simulated it – not because anything in the preceding chapters predicts it. A reader who wants to know whether the crossover arrives at cycle 8 in their programme has no way to find out except to re-run the simulation with their own parameters.

The quantitative genetics that answers such questions is older than any of the genomic machinery here, and this chapter installs it: the variance selection acts on, the equation that turns that variance into gain, and the classical index theory that turns out to be the direct ancestor of this book’s \(\alpha\) constraint.

sim <- simulate_pools(n_A = book_cfg$n_pool_A, n_B = book_cfg$n_pool_B,
                      m = book_cfg$m, fst = 0.15, n_qtl = 300, h2 = 0.5, seed = 1)
X  <- sim$X                       # N x m hybrids, 0/0.5/1
gv <- drop(X %*% sim$beta)        # true genetic value, known here by construction
I  <- sim$index                   # what the breeder actually sees
c(hybrids = nrow(X), markers = ncol(X), qtl = sum(sim$beta != 0))
#> hybrids markers     qtl 
#>     625    2000     300

3.1 The breeder’s equation, and how well it does here

Response to one round of truncation selection is

\[R \;=\; i \, r \, \sigma_A\]

with \(i\) the selection intensity (a property of the selected fraction alone), \(r\) the accuracy of the criterion as a predictor of genetic value, and \(\sigma_A\) the additive standard deviation. sel_intensity() has been in this package since Chapter 13; it has never once been used to compute a response.

n_sel <- 60
N     <- nrow(X)
i_norm <- sel_intensity(n_sel / N)
r      <- cor(I, gv)
sel    <- order(I, decreasing = TRUE)[seq_len(n_sel)]

fmt(data.frame(i = i_norm, r = r, sigma_A = sd(gv),
               predicted = i_norm * r * sd(gv),
               realised  = mean(gv[sel]) - mean(gv)), 3)
i r sigma_A predicted realised
1.774 0.702 4.742 5.908 5.116

The equation overpredicts, by about 13 percent. That is worth taking apart rather than rounding away, because both halves of the discrepancy are instructive.

i_real <- (mean(I[sel]) - mean(I)) / sd(I)
b      <- cov(gv, I) / var(I)                 # regression of value on criterion
fmt(data.frame(
  i_normal_theory = i_norm,
  i_realised      = i_real,
  R_with_realised_i = i_real * r * sd(gv),
  R_from_regression = b * (mean(I[sel]) - mean(I)),
  R_actual          = mean(gv[sel]) - mean(gv),
  index_skew        = mean(scale(I)^3)), 3)
i_normal_theory i_realised R_with_realised_i R_from_regression R_actual index_skew
1.774 1.701 5.662 5.662 5.116 -0.17

Two separate failures, and they are different in kind.

The realised intensity is below normal theory. \(i = \varphi(z)/q\) is exact for a normal criterion. This index is slightly left-skewed, so its upper tail is thinner than a normal’s and the top 60 individuals sit closer to the mean than the formula assumes. This is a property of this population, not an error.

The regression is exact, and still wrong. R_from_regression and R_with_realised_i agree to machine precision – they are the same quantity written twice, since \(b\,S_I = i\,r\,\sigma_A\) identically. Both still exceed the realised response. What fails is not the algebra but the assumption underneath it: \(R = b\,S_I\) requires \(E[g \mid I]\) to be linear in \(I\) in the selected tail, which is a bivariate-normality claim, and bivariate normality is exactly what a two-pool hybrid population does not have.

ImportantRead the breeder’s equation as an upper bound here

Every response number in this book’s cycle simulations is a realised response, measured from the simulation. Where a predicted response is quoted, it will run above the realised one for the reason above. The gap is not noise and does not shrink with more markers; it shrinks when the criterion becomes more normal and the population less structured.

3.2 Genic variance, and what it has to do with gene diversity

The additive variance in the equation above is not a fixed property of the population. Split it in two.

Genic variance is what the loci would contribute if they were independent:

\[V_{\text{genic}} \;=\; \sum_k \beta_k^2 \operatorname{Var}(x_k)\]

Realised variance is what the population actually has, \(\operatorname{Var} (\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta)\), which is the sum of the whole covariance matrix rather than only its diagonal. The difference is exactly the gametic-phase disequilibrium:

\[\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta) - V_{\text{genic}} \;=\; 2\sum_{k<l}\beta_k\beta_l \operatorname{Cov}(x_k, x_l)\]

c(realised = var(gv),
  genic    = genic_var(X, sim$beta),
  gap      = var(gv) - genic_var(X, sim$beta),
  oracle_deviation = abs(genic_var(X, sim$beta) - ref_genic_var(X, sim$beta)))
#>         realised            genic              gap oracle_deviation 
#>     2.248375e+01     2.520510e+01    -2.721357e+00     1.065814e-14

Now the sentence that ties this chapter to every one that follows.

\(V_{\text{genic}}\) is \(\sum_k \beta_k^2\,p_k q_k\) up to a coding constant, and gene diversity is \(\frac{1}{m}\sum_k 2 p_k q_k\). They are the same sum over the same loci, weighted differently – one by squared effect, one by nothing at all. That is the precise sense in which managing gene diversity manages the raw material of response, and it is why a diversity constraint is a defensible proxy for a variance constraint in the first place.

It is also the precise sense in which the proxy is incomplete. Gene diversity weights every locus equally, so it cannot distinguish a marker carrying half the genetic variance from one carrying none. And – more seriously –

WarningNo metric in this book can see the disequilibrium term

\(\theta\), \(GD\), \(N_s\), allelic richness, F_hom and F_drift are all functions of allele frequencies one locus at a time. The covariance term above does not move a single allele frequency. A flat gene diversity trajectory therefore does not imply flat additive variance, and Chapter 20 measures how far apart the two can travel.

3.3 The decomposition this book already had

Additive variance is the variance of breeding values, not of genotypic values, and in a two-pool hybrid population the average effect of an allele substitution depends on which pool it is substituted into. That sounds like it needs new machinery. It does not: Section 10.10 already built the exact orthogonal split, and general combining ability is the additive part.

set.seed(7)
d  <- rnorm(ncol(X), 0.3, 0.1)                  # dominance deviations
U  <- sim$GL[sim$pool == "A", ]
V  <- sim$GL[sim$pool == "B", ]
gs <- heterosis_gca_sca(U, V, d)
H  <- heterosis_value(X, d)

nA <- nrow(U); nB <- nrow(V)
gA <- rep(gs$gca_A, times = nB)                 # ped varies pool A fastest
gB <- rep(gs$gca_B, each  = nA)
sc <- as.vector(gs$sca)

c(reconstruction_dev = max(abs(H - (gs$mu + gA + gB + sc))),
  var_H              = var(H),
  sum_of_components  = var(gA) + var(gB) + var(sc),
  identity_dev       = abs(var(H) - (var(gA) + var(gB) + var(sc))))
#> reconstruction_dev              var_H  sum_of_components       identity_dev 
#>       5.115908e-13       3.811311e+01       3.811311e+01       9.237056e-14

The three components are orthogonal, so their variances add exactly. Read the shares:

p <- c(gca_A = var(gA), gca_B = var(gB), sca = var(sc))
fmt(data.frame(component = names(p), variance = p,
               share = p / sum(p)), 3)
component variance share
gca_A gca_A 9.064 0.238
gca_B gca_B 8.673 0.228
sca sca 20.376 0.535

gca_A and gca_B are the part of heterosis an additive model can express and that therefore behaves like additive variance under selection. sca is the part it cannot, and it does not accumulate across cycles the way the general components do. A programme selecting on an additive index is selecting on the first two and getting the third by luck.

3.4 What weights = NULL is assuming

stage2_select() defaults to equal weight on every trait, and every chapter of this book including Chapter 21 takes that default. It is a choice, not a neutral one.

The classical answer is the Smith-Hazel index (Smith 1936; Hazel 1943): the weights maximising the correlation between the index and the aggregate genotype \(H = \mathbf{a}'\mathbf{g}\) are \(\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{a}\).

P <- matrix(c(4.0, 1.2, 1.2, 1.0), 2)      # phenotypic covariance
G <- matrix(c(2.0, 0.9, 0.9, 0.3), 2)      # genetic covariance
a <- c(1, 1)                               # equal economic weight

fmt(data.frame(trait = c("yield", "moisture"),
               economic_weight = a,
               smith_hazel     = index_smith_hazel(P, G, a)), 3)
trait economic_weight smith_hazel
yield 1 0.570
moisture 1 0.516

Equal economic weights do not give equal index weights. They coincide only when \(\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{G}\) is proportional to the identity, which requires the traits to be equally variable, equally heritable and mutually uncorrelated. Nothing guarantees that, and where it fails the index being maximised is not the index anyone intended.

3.5 The \(\alpha\) constraint is a restricted index

Chapter 15 will argue that a diversity budget is approvable where a weighted sum is not: “I accept losing five percent” is a sentence a breeding committee can sign, and “diversity is worth \(\lambda\) index units” is not.

That argument is usually presented as a point about human decision-making. It is older and harder than that. Kempthorne & Nordskog (1959) posed exactly the same problem – maximise aggregate gain subject to zero expected change in a specified direction – and solved it:

\[\mathbf{b} \;=\; \mathbf{P}^{-1}\left[\mathbf{G}\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{C} (\mathbf{C}'\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{C})^{-1}\mathbf{C}'\mathbf{P}^{-1} \mathbf{G}\mathbf{a}\right], \qquad \mathbf{C} = \mathbf{G}\mathbf{R}\]

R  <- cbind(c(0, 1))                       # hold moisture at zero gain
b_r <- index_restricted(P, G, a, R)
b_u <- index_smith_hazel(P, G, a)

fmt(data.frame(
  index = c("Smith-Hazel", "restricted"),
  w_yield    = c(b_u[1], b_r[1]),
  w_moisture = c(b_u[2], b_r[2]),
  gain_in_restricted_trait = c(drop(crossprod(R, G %*% b_u)),
                               drop(crossprod(R, G %*% b_r)))), 4)
index w_yield w_moisture gain_in_restricted_trait
Smith-Hazel 0.5703 0.5156 0.668
restricted -0.1207 0.3621 0.000

The restricted index moves the restricted trait by exactly zero – not approximately, not on average. That is the property distinguishing a restriction from a penalty, and it is the property \(\alpha\) inherits.

NoteWhat is genuinely new in this book, then

Not the idea of constraining rather than pricing: that is 1959. What is new is the restricted direction. Kempthorne and Nordskog restricted a trait. This book restricts the loss of molecular gene diversity, which is not a trait, has no economic weight, and is measured on a matrix rather than a phenotype. The algebra of restriction carries over; the object being restricted does not.

Stating the lineage makes Chapter 15 stronger, not weaker. A sixty-year-old theorem stands behind the move.

3.6 Where this connects

Chapter 9 and Chapter 11 measure the frequencies whose weighted sum is the genic variance defined here, which is why a diversity constraint is a variance constraint in disguise. Chapter 14 measures the \(r\) this chapter took as given, and Finding 5 of Chapter 12 shows what happens when it is assumed rather than measured. Chapter 15 is where the restricted index becomes the \(\alpha\) constraint, and Chapter 20 is where the disequilibrium term this chapter isolated turns out to matter more than the equation suggests.
