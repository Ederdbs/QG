17  Advanced selection: structure, bounds and scale

Chapter 16 stops at a warm-started differential evolution (DE). That is a reasonable place to stop if the problem is a black box. It is not one. This chapter names the structure, and then spends it: on cheaper moves, on genuine approximation guarantees, on a bound that is actually a bound, and on an oracle that says how far from optimal the heuristics really are.

The target throughout is the scale the method has to survive: 200 hybrids chosen from 10,000, a full factorial of 100 by 100 lines. That is an ordinary size for a hybrid maize programme at the end of a cycle: two heterotic pools of a hundred inbred or doubled-haploid lines each, every cross between them a legitimate candidate, and a few hundred of them going into the next round of multi-location testing.

That is a different – and deliberately larger – “production scale” from the “package production scale” Chapter 16 uses to count dimensions: the real dataset this package was written for, 71 + 71 lines and \(N = 5041\) candidates. (Chapter 16 also quotes this chapter’s two benchmark scales, by name, and the cached run at \(N = 3{,}600\) from 120 lines, which is where its encoding measurement was made.) Here it is the 100 + 100 factorial the scaling study and the head-to-head benchmark are run at, because a cost curve needs a span of \(N\) wider than one point provides. They are not interchangeable: every per-evaluation cost and every memory figure in this chapter is at \(N = 10{,}000\).

Scales, replication and provenance. The names are those of Chapter 16. “Book scale” is the live instance of this chapter: 25 + 25 lines, 2000 markers, simulation seed 1, \(N =\) 625, \(n =\) 60 (the teaching scale of Chapter 15). The rows book and production of search_benchmark.csv are the benchmark book scale – the same \(N\), \(L\), \(n\) and dataset, with the reference gd_ref resampled \(B = 500\) times against \(B = 400\) here, and a per-line cap of 5 – and the benchmark production scale: \(N =\) 10,000, \(L =\) 200, \(n =\) 200, cap 4. The unit of replication in that file is the optimiser seed (1 to 3) on one simulated dataset per scale: the deterministic methods (greedy, greedy_cap, ocs_round, ocs_round_cap) return three identical rows, and every capped row has a seed standard deviation of 0 at the benchmark production scale. “Mean of 3 seeds” below means that, and a standard error over three seeds is a statement about the optimiser, not about the dataset.

The files read here – search_benchmark.csv, search_scaling.csv, search_anytime.csv, search_exact_gap.csv, search_anytime.png and search_scaling.png – are written by book/scripts/regenerate.R search (about 15 minutes; the exact oracle by search_exact), which quarto render never calls. They are reproducible in this repository, unlike the cached external files of Chapter 18; the indices of the deterministic methods reproduce to the digits printed, the timings (secs, us_*) come from one machine and do not. sel_exact() needs the highs package and ocs_quadprog() the quadprog package, both Suggests of hybdiv.

The evaluation budget of the differential evolutions. In the benchmark sel_de() and sel_de_fast() run NP = 100 and itermax = 300, that is 30,000 nominal evaluations (the evals column), against 30,000 proposed moves for each local search; the anytime curve of Section 17.3 runs the DE from 2,000 to 80,000 evaluations. Every statement about the DE in this chapter is about those budgets. Section 16.4.2 runs sel_de() at four times the first of them and at the printed default of 6e+05 evaluations, and finds its mean index above the best fine greedy plan there at the 1% budget, below it at 30,000; this chapter’s “DE below greedy” is the 30,000 reading and not a property of the method.

What has to be in hand, beyond Chapter 16’s three objects, is the line-level route: fast_ctx() adds ctx$parents, the \(N \times 2\) matrix of parent indices, to a context that already carries ctx$fL, the \(L \times L\) coancestry between lines. Everything in this chapter that scales is a function of those two and of nothing else.

The assumptions are Chapter 16’s, unchanged and worth restating because two of them are load-bearing here: parent lines are homozygous, which is what makes \(f_{ij}\) a function of the four parental coancestries and hence collapses the problem to \(L\) dimensions (Section 12.1); and f is molecular coancestry in \([0, 1]\), not a centred relationship matrix, which is what makes it a Gram matrix with the properties Section 17.1 needs.

17.1 Three facts about this problem

Everything below follows from three properties of the problem, each of which takes three lines to check. The first, the Gram form of f, is stated in Section 6.2.1 and checked there numerically (and Section 10.7.7 asserts the related form \(\mathbf{f} = \tfrac12 + 2\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^\top/m\), \(\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{X} - \tfrac12\)); here it is used for convexity. The second and third, the submatrix sum and the matroid, are stated here for the first time.

17.1.1 f is a Gram matrix

Molecular coancestry is \(f_{ij} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_k [x_{ik}x_{jk} + (1-x_{ik})(1-x_{jk})]\), which is an inner product of the stacked vectors \([\mathbf{x}_i;\, \mathbf{1}-\mathbf{x}_i]\). So f is not merely symmetric, it is a Gram matrix, exactly:

Show code 
V  <- cbind(ctx[["X"]], 1 - ctx[["X"]])
ev <- eigen(ctx[["f"]], symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(ctx[["f"]], tcrossprod(V) / ctx$m, tolerance = 1e-12)),
          min(ev) > -1e-10 * max(ev))     # positive semi-definite up to rounding
c(max_abs_difference = max(abs(ctx[["f"]] - tcrossprod(V) / ctx$m)),
  min_eigenvalue     = min(ev),
  min_over_max_eig   = min(ev) / max(ev))
#> max_abs_difference     min_eigenvalue   min_over_max_eig 
#>       0.000000e+00      -4.275667e-13      -1.018142e-15

The check is the whole argument, and the chunk asserts it: the difference is zero to rounding, and the smallest eigenvalue is zero to the eigensolver’s own rounding – its sign is arbitrary, 15 orders of magnitude below the largest eigenvalue. A negative eigenvalue large enough to see on that scale would mean f had been centred, rescaled, or built on the wrong marker coding – and every method in this chapter would silently lose its justification.

Two consequences carry the rest of the chapter. First, \(\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{c}\) is a convex quadratic, so the diversity budget is a convex constraint – which is what makes the exact method of Section 17.6 possible at all. Second, \(\psi_{ij} = f_{ii} + f_{jj} - 2f_{ij}\) (not the distance \(1 - f_{ij}\) of E-NE and A-NE, Appendix A) is a squared Euclidean distance, hence a distance of negative type, which is the precise hypothesis the strongest approximation results require.

17.1.2 Maximising diversity is minimising the submatrix sum

\(\theta_S = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,j \in S} f_{ij}\) (Equation 11.1; Cockerham (1967)), and at fixed \(n\) the normaliser is a constant:

Show code 
s <- sel_greedy(ctx, n_sel, w = 1)
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(theta_group(s, ctx) * n_sel^2, sum(ctx[["f"]][s, s]),
                           tolerance = 1e-12)))
# the max-sum identity of @eq-advanced-maxsum, below
fS  <- ctx[["f"]][s, s]
psi <- outer(diag(fS), diag(fS), "+") - 2 * fS
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(sum(psi[upper.tri(psi)]),
                           n_sel * sum(diag(fS)) - n_sel^2 * theta_group(s, ctx),
                           tolerance = 1e-12)))
c(theta_times_n2 = theta_group(s, ctx) * n_sel^2,
  submatrix_sum  = sum(ctx[["f"]][s, s]),
  difference     = theta_group(s, ctx) * n_sel^2 - sum(ctx[["f"]][s, s]))
#> theta_times_n2  submatrix_sum     difference 
#>   2.475329e+03   2.475328e+03   1.818989e-12

So the objective of the greedy score of Chapter 16, \(\omega\,\bar u_S - \theta_S\), is, up to the positive constant \(n^{-2}\), \(n\omega \sum_{i \in S} u_i - \sum_{i,j \in S} f_{ij}\). Here \(u_i\) is the index of hybrid \(i\) (index standard deviations), \(S\) a set of \(n\) hybrids, \(\bar u_S\) its mean, and \(\omega \ge 0\) the index weight of Appendix A, in units of group coancestry per index standard deviation. The first term is modular; the second is submodular, because \(f_{ij} \ge 0\) makes the marginal cost of adding a hybrid, \(f_{ii} + 2\sum_{j \in S} f_{ij}\), grow with the set. Writing the same thing through \(\psi_{ij}\),

\[\sum_{i<j \in S} \psi_{ij} \;=\; n\,\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{f}_S) - n^2\theta_S, \tag{17.1}\]

with \(\mathbf{f}_S\) the \(n \times n\) submatrix of \(\mathbf{f}\) on \(S\) and \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{f}_S) = \sum_{i \in S} f_{ii}\) (an identity of the definitions, asserted in the chunk above), identifies it as the max-sum diversification problem of Borodin et al. (2017). I have not found it named that way in the plant-breeding literature, and this book makes no claim about what a systematic screen would find – no such screen was run. The structural identity is the load-bearing part, and it holds whether or not anyone has written it down before.

The practical content of “fixed \(n\)” is worth spelling out: it is only at a fixed set size that maximising gene diversity and minimising the submatrix sum are the same instruction. Comparing \(\theta\) across selections of different sizes compares two different things, which is the same reason gd_ref has to be resampled at the size actually being used (Section 11.9).

17.1.3 The per-line cap is a partition matroid

“No line may parent more than max_use of the selected hybrids” is one partition matroid per heterotic pool. Together with the cardinality constraint that is an intersection of matroids – not an arbitrary combinatorial restriction, but the single best-understood constraint class in discrete optimisation.

The structure comes from the mating design, not from the mathematics. Every candidate is a cross between one pool-A line and one pool-B line, so each selected hybrid contributes exactly one use to a pool-A line and one to a pool-B line, and the counts within each pool sum to \(n\) by construction:

Show code 
cnt <- line_counts(s, ctx)
stopifnot(sum(cnt[ctx$pool == "A"]) == n_sel, sum(cnt[ctx$pool == "B"]) == n_sel)
c(uses_pool_A     = sum(cnt[ctx$pool == "A"]),
  uses_pool_B     = sum(cnt[ctx$pool == "B"]),
  n_sel           = n_sel,
  distinct_A_used = sum(cnt[ctx$pool == "A"] > 0),
  distinct_B_used = sum(cnt[ctx$pool == "B"] > 0))
#>     uses_pool_A     uses_pool_B           n_sel distinct_A_used distinct_B_used 
#>              60              60              60              10              23

That is exactly the ground-set partition a partition matroid needs. It also gives the feasibility condition for any cap \(k_{\max}\) (the per-line cap of Chapter 16, max_use in the code) directly: a plan of \(n\) crosses exists under the cap only if \(n \le L_A k_{\max}\) and \(n \le L_B k_{\max}\), with \(L_A\) and \(L_B\) the numbers of lines in the two pools. In a programme the cap is rarely chosen for theoretical reasons – it is set by how many seed-production fields a female line can support – and the point of naming the structure is that a constraint arriving from the field turns out to be the one class of constraint discrete optimisation handles best.

17.2 What lazy greedy would not buy

The obvious first move on a submodular objective is the accelerated, or “lazy”, greedy of Minoux (1978): keep stale marginal gains in a priority queue and re-evaluate only the top one, which is valid precisely because submodularity makes a stale gain an upper bound on the current one. Two distinct results are easy to conflate here, so name them apart: the acceleration is Minoux’s, while the worst-case guarantee for the plain greedy it accelerates is Nemhauser et al. (1978)’s and is quoted separately below. It is the standard accelerator for this objective class, and here it is worthless.

sel_greedy() already carries the running vector s of coancestry sums against the chosen set, so every candidate’s marginal gain is \(O(1)\) and the whole step is one vectorised pass. Lazy evaluation optimises precisely the cost that is already free. Reaching for it would add a heap, add bugs, and save nothing.

The guarantee is worth stating even so, because it settles a question rather than improving a number: greedy under our constraints cannot do worse than \(1/(p+1)\) of optimal, with \(p\) the number of matroids intersected – here the cardinality constraint plus one partition matroid per pool.

State it with its hypotheses, though, because they are not all met. The \(1 - 1/e\) and \(1/(p+1)\) results of Nemhauser et al. (1978) and Fisher et al. (1978) are proved for monotone, non-negative submodular functions. Our objective is neither in general: the index is standardised to mean zero over the candidate pool, so \(\bar u_S\) can be negative, and adding a hybrid increases the coancestry term, so the objective is monotone only where the merit term dominates. The guarantee therefore belongs in this chapter as a statement about the constraint class – the cap is not an arbitrary restriction – and not as a guarantee for any particular run. The bound this chapter actually uses is the Lagrangian bound of Section 17.4, and Section 17.6 measures what the local search does in fact achieve.

17.3 The move that was already paid for

Section 12.1 established \(\theta_S = \mathbf{w}'\mathbf{f}^L\mathbf{w}\) (Equation 12.2), and theta_state()/theta_swap() have been in the package ever since, maintaining group coancestry in \(O(L)\) per swap. Nothing consumed them. Chapter 16 even ends with a callout recommending local search, citing De Beukelaer et al. (2018) – and then does not run one.

Show code 
theta_swap
#> function (st, out_h, in_h, ctx) 
#> {
#>     cnt <- st$cnt
#>     v <- st$v
#>     po <- ctx$parents[out_h, ]
#>     pi_ <- ctx$parents[in_h, ]
#>     for (a in po) {
#>         cnt[a] <- cnt[a] - 1L
#>         v <- v - ctx$fL[, a]
#>     }
#>     for (a in pi_) {
#>         cnt[a] <- cnt[a] + 1L
#>         v <- v + ctx$fL[, a]
#>     }
#>     s <- st$s
#>     list(cnt = cnt, v = v, s = s, theta = sum(cnt * v)/(s * s))
#> }
#> <bytecode: 0x78a09ace78>
#> <environment: namespace:hybdiv>

Four rank-one column updates. No N x N matrix is touched, and the cost depends on neither \(N\) nor \(m\). The state it maintains is three objects: the line-count vector cnt, the product v = fL %*% cnt, and their inner product divided by \(s^2\), which is \(\theta\). Removing a hybrid subtracts the two columns of fL belonging to its parents; adding one adds the two columns of the entrant’s parents. Because a swap keeps the set size fixed, the normaliser \(s = 2n\) never changes.

It is an exact update, not an approximation, and that is checkable in three lines:

Show code

out_h <- s[1]
in_h  <- setdiff(seq_len(ctx$N), s)[1]
st2   <- theta_swap(st, out_h, in_h, ctx)
after <- c(setdiff(s, out_h), in_h)
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(st2$theta, theta_group(after, ctx))),
          isTRUE(all.equal((2 * n_sel)^2 / sum(st2$cnt^2), ne_parents(after, ctx))))
c(incremental = st2$theta, recomputed = theta_group(after, ctx))
#> incremental  recomputed 
#>   0.6863819   0.6863819

If that identity ever fails, the state and the selection have drifted apart – the usual cause being a move that was accepted in the selection vector but not applied to the state, or the reverse.

That last property is the one that matters, and it is worth being precise about where the saving actually comes from, because the obvious answer is wrong. The line encoding does not make an evaluation cheaper. Taking two rows of Table 17.18, in Table 17.2:

The line encoding is slower per evaluation, because decoding a weight vector into a selection has to rank every candidate. What it buys is a smaller search space, not a cheaper objective. The order-of-magnitude saving belongs to the swap – and the swap is available only to a method that moves in the space of selections rather than decoding one from scratch each time.

args(sel_local_search)

Three modes, one driver, differing only in the temperature schedule. The driver does the following, and each step is a decision a breeder can inspect:

  1. Start from a plan, never from noise. start = NULL builds a warm start: capped truncation, plus the greedy sweep whenever an \(N \times N\) f is present (the test is that ctx[["f"]] is a matrix with \(N\) rows), and it is worth being accurate about which branch produced the numbers in this chapter: the production benchmark of Section 17.7 deliberately forms the 0.745 GiB matrix so that the routes needing it can be timed at all, so its rows took the greedy branch. Among the candidates it takes the highest-index one that meets the budget, and if none does, the one with the smallest loss. As in sel_de(), NULL never means “start cold”.

    The greedy branch is guarded, but the ranking that follows it is not: the warm start scores every candidate with alpha_loss(), which reads ctx[["f"]]. So start = NULL needs the \(N \times N\) matrix whichever branch it takes, and on a context built without one it stops with the message ctx[["f"]] (hybrid coancestry) is missing, as Table 17.19 shows. Supplying an explicit start = skips the whole function, and from there the search is fL-only; that is the call to make if f does not fit in memory, and Section 17.7.1 is where the two are run side by side.

  2. Build the state. theta_state() on the starting selection, plus the running index sum and a logical membership vector, so that every later question is \(O(1)\) or \(O(L)\).

  3. Calibrate the temperature. With t0 = NULL and a stochastic mode, the driver proposes up to 200 random swaps, records the median absolute change in the objective, and uses that as the starting temperature. Nothing about the scale of the index is assumed, so the same call transfers between datasets. mode = "hill" skips this entirely, since it never accepts a worsening move.

  4. Propose a feasible move. Pick one of the \(n\) selected crosses at random to leave; draw entrants at random until one is found whose parents stay under max_use once the leaving cross is removed, giving up after 20 attempts. The cap is tested, never punished.

  5. Score it in \(O(L)\). theta_swap() for the new \(\theta\), one addition and one subtraction for the new index sum, and the objective is \(\bar u_S\) minus penalty times the budget violation expressed in units of the budget itself – the violation is divided by alpha_max, so a 5 becomes an effective slope of 500 index standard deviations per unit of relative loss at a 1% budget, and 5000 at 0.1%. The tighter the budget, the harder a violation is punished, which is the behaviour you want and is not what make_fitness()’s fixed multiplier of 1000 on the absolute violation does.

  6. Accept or reject. Improving moves are always accepted; worsening ones with probability \(\exp(\Delta / t_k)\) at the current temperature. The best-ever selection is tracked separately from the current one and is what is returned – so a chain that wanders away at high temperature loses nothing.

Two design points are worth stopping on.

The cap is tested, not punished. Whether a swap keeps every line under max_use is an \(O(1)\) question given the count vector, so infeasible moves are never proposed. Penalising them instead spends the evaluation budget on being rejected – with a tight cap that was the difference between the search improving on its warm start and not moving at all.

The temperature calibrates itself. A constant would not transfer between datasets, so t0 = NULL samples the median absolute objective change over a short burn-in and uses that.

One run of each is not a comparison. Twenty seeds (1 to 20) of each mode, at 20,000 and at 30,000 proposed moves – 30,000 is the budget of the benchmark of Table 17.5. The seeds are the same at both budgets but the draws are not, because the number of moves differs; sel_local_search() sets the seed itself, immediately before its own draws. Table 17.3 gives the indices and the paired differences, and Table 17.4 the realised loss, \(N_{e,\text{par}}\) and largest line use of each mode at 20,000 moves.

Note that iters is the total number of proposed moves summed over chains, so the three modes cost about the same wall-clock at equal iters: at the benchmark book scale their mean times are 0.222 to 0.232 seconds for 30,000 moves. In tempering mode those moves are split across n_chains replicas on a geometric temperature ladder spanning three decades, exchanging adjacent rungs every swap_every moves.

The realised alpha of Table 17.4 sits against the 1% budget in every mode: the constraint is binding, and the search is spending the diversity it was allowed.

Three checks before a plan from this function is used, in this order: realised \(\alpha\) against the budget (a plan far inside it means the search never reached the boundary, or the penalty was too steep); max_line against the cap you set; and \(N_{e,\text{par}}\), which is free and catches the case where \(\theta\) is satisfied by a handful of lines used many times each.

Interpretation, and what limits it. Neither paired difference between hill and anneal is as large as two standard errors, and the two have opposite signs, so this run does not separate the two schedules at either budget; it also does not show that they are equal – a standard error of about one thousandth bounds what 20 seeds on one dataset can resolve. A change of sign between the budgets is therefore not evidence of an ordering that depends on the budget. Tempering’s shortfall is resolved at both budgets (below both in every seed). A candidate cause is the splitting of a fixed move budget across the replicas; that was not tested (no run at a larger budget, and n_chains and the ladder were not varied), so the shortfall is a measurement of this implementation at its default settings, on 625 candidates, and not of parallel tempering. What this panel supports is that at a fixed move budget the schedule is a second-order choice (the three modes differ by at most 1.5% of the index, and the spread is tempering’s). One dataset, one simulation seed: a different dataset is not measured.

17.4 The bound was not a bound

Section 15.4 introduces the continuous relaxation and its two statements, Equation 15.6 (weak duality) and Equation 15.7 (the Lagrangian bound). The convergence check of Chapter 16 was originally written to take, as an upper bound, the best relaxation point whose own \(\alpha\) falls inside the budget. As used, that construction is not valid, and this section shows it with the experiment that found it – and says what the experiment does and does not show.

The relaxation at a given \(\lambda\) maximises \(\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{u} - \lambda\,\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{c}\) (Equation 15.5), a penalised objective, not the index. By Equation 15.6 a relaxation point bounds every plan at least as diverse as itself, and at exactly equal diversity a plan cannot beat it. What Equation 15.6 does not give is a bound for a plan that spends the whole budget when the point lies strictly inside it: that plan is less diverse than the point, and the bound it satisfies is the tangent of slope \(\lambda\) at the point, which lies above the point’s height (Section 15.4, statement 3). A grid of \(\lambda\) values makes “strictly inside” the rule: the last grid point inside the budget is the one the construction uses. Table 17.11 gives the experiment.

lagrangian_2000 is the Lagrangian form evaluated at a deliberately cheap setting – 12 multipliers, 2,000 projected-gradient iterations – and it is here only to show that the form survives where the construction does not. Its validity needs \(V(\lambda)\) to be computed to convergence, which this setting does not guarantee, so nothing downstream of this chunk quotes it; the value quoted as the bound comes from ocs_bound() below.

The correct statement is Equation 15.7, derived from Equation 15.6 in Section 15.4 and not re-derived here: for every \(\lambda \ge 0\) and every plan \(S\) of \(n\) hybrids with \(\theta_S \le \theta_{\max}\), \(\bar u_S \le V(\lambda) + \lambda\,\theta_{\max}\), with \(V(\lambda)\) the optimum of the relaxation. The tightest such statement is the minimum over \(\lambda\), and a coarse grid costs tightness, not validity – unlike the old construction, where a coarse grid could silently produce a “bound” below the achievable.

17.5 Rounding the relaxation into a plan

With a bound in hand, the relaxation stops being only a diagnostic. Solve the optimal-contribution programme in line space, where quadprog returns instantly because the dimension is \(L\), round the contributions to integer line usage, and assign concrete crosses under both pools’ caps. That two-stage shape – contributions first, mate allocation second – is exactly Waldmann (2025)’s, whose mate-allocation stage solves in under 0.01 seconds.

The five steps inside ocs_round(), in the order they run:

  1. Line merit. Each line is scored by the mean index of the hybrids it parents in the candidate set, the line_merit of Chapter 15. That quantity is a general combining ability: a property of the line against the opposite pool as represented in this factorial, never a property of the line alone (Section 5.11). Change the tester pool and the score changes.
  2. Contributions. ocs_quadprog() on ctx$fL with those merits, at a given \(\lambda\), with each line’s contribution capped at \(\texttt{max\_use} / 2n\) – the cap expressed as a share of the \(2n\) parent slots, as in Chapter 15. As there, the quadratic programme has a single margin (the contributions of all \(L\) lines sum to one) and no per-pool sum; the two pools’ totals are equalised only in step 4, because every cross spends one slot of each pool.
  3. Rounding. Contributions times \(2n\), floored, and the shortfall handed to the largest remainders; then clipped at max_use again.
  4. Mate allocation. Walk the candidates in decreasing index and take each cross whose two parents still have quota and are under the cap – a greedy b-matching. If quota runs out before \(n\) crosses are found, top up on the cap alone, because the quota is a rounding artefact and the cap is the real restriction.
  5. Choose \(\lambda\) by budget, not by preference. The whole sweep is evaluated and the plan returned is the best-index one whose realised \(\alpha\) respects alpha_max. The exchange rate stays an internal detail; the budget remains the interface.

17.6 Exact, and how far the heuristics really are

Everything so far is a heuristic with a bound. The remaining question is what the true optimum is, and the honest answer requires solving the problem exactly at a scale where that is possible.

The obvious route is closed. Maximising a linear index subject to a quadratic diversity constraint over binary variables is a mixed-integer quadratically constrained programme, and HiGHS – like most open solvers – does not accept a quadratic term together with integer variables at all.

Convexity opens a different one. Because f is positive semi-definite, \(\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{x}\) is convex, so its tangent at any point is a valid linear under-estimate. Write \(\mathbf{x}\) for the 0/1 indicator vector of the selection (\(N\) binary variables) and \(b = n^2\,\theta_{\max}\) for the budget in submatrix-sum units (step 1 below); at any point \(\bar{\mathbf{x}}\) the tangent gives the cut

\[\bar{\mathbf{x}}'\mathbf{f}\bar{\mathbf{x}} + 2(\mathbf{f}\bar{\mathbf{x}})'(\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}) \;\le\; b \tag{17.3}\]

and Equation 17.3 is a linear relaxation of \(\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{x} \le b\). Solve the resulting mixed-integer linear programme (MILP), add a cut wherever the solution violates the true constraint, re-solve. This is outer approximation (Duran and Grossmann 1986), it needs only a MILP solver, and it terminates: each cut strictly removes the infeasible point that generated it.

The loop, step by step:

  1. Translate the budget. The diversity constraint becomes \(\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{x} \le n^2\,\theta_{\max}\) with \(\theta_{\max} = 1 - \text{gd\_ref}(1 - \alpha_{\max})\) – the same quantity as in the Lagrangian bound, expressed in submatrix-sum units because that is what the solver sees.
  2. Write the structural rows. One cardinality row summing to \(n\), and one row per line for the partition matroid when max_use is set. Those are linear and exact; only the quadratic needs approximating.
  3. Carry an incumbent from the start. The warm start is the same sel_local_search() warm start; if it satisfies the budget it becomes the incumbent and is handed to the solver as a starting point, so that a run stopped by the clock still reports a real interval.
  4. Solve, cut, repeat. If the MILP solution satisfies the true quadratic constraint, it is optimal for the current relaxation and the loop can stop; otherwise a gradient cut at that point is added and the MILP re-solved.
  5. Read the dual bound from the right field. objective_value is an upper bound only for a MILP solved to optimality; the moment the solver hits its own time limit the bound must come from mip_dual_bound. Cuts only tighten the relaxation, so the running minimum over iterations stays valid.

17.7.2 The head-to-head at production scale

Nothing broke at 10,000 and nothing here claims it did: the benchmark of Table 17.20 forms the 0.745 GiB matrix on purpose, and the hybrid route still evaluates in 132 us. The wall is further out, and it is documented rather than predicted – at \(N = 22{,}500\) the matrix is 3.8 GiB and Chapter 12’s cost benchmark declines to form it at all, so every metric that needs it has no timing entry at that scale. The line route has one.

Two things in Table 17.20 are worth more than the ranking.

The cap binds, the budget does not. At the benchmark production scale every capped method reaches essentially the same index (the spread is given below), at a diversity loss far inside the 1% allowed. With \(n = 200\) over \(L = 200\) lines the mean usage is already 2, so a cap of 4 leaves very little to choose; the restriction that is actually deciding the plan is the one nobody wrote a budget for. Check max_line and realised alpha together before concluding that a diversity constraint is doing any work.

Read down the capped rows only, as the rule given for Table 17.5 requires. At the benchmark production scale the five capped routes span 0.0002 index units (greedy_cap 1.1225, the other four 1.1223), each with a seed standard deviation of 0 (asserted above), on one dataset. With no seed dispersion that difference is deterministic on this dataset, greedy_cap ahead by 0.0002, exactly as the book-scale differences below are. “Tie” in this chapter is therefore a statement about a margin and not about dispersion: routes are called tied when their mean indices lie within 0.001 index units, about 0.09% of the index of these rows. The margin is a convention named here, not derived from any sampling distribution (there is none to derive it from), and the five production rows satisfy it (asserted above) while the book-scale rows do not. They differ in cost, greedy_cap taking 0.329 s and tempering_cap 0.466 s. At the benchmark book scale the same five rows do not tie. They span 6.6 thousandths: greedy_cap 1.1122 in 0.008 s, de_hybrid_cap 1.1097 and tempering_cap 1.1097 (0.286 s for the latter), and de_lines_cap 1.1056 and ocs_round_cap 1.1056, the two lowest. Every one of these rows has a seed standard deviation of 0 (asserted above), so the differences are not seed noise; they are differences between routes on one dataset. Interpretation, and what limits it: at the production scale no capped route is distinguished from the others on index at that margin; at the book scale the capped greedy sweep is highest, by 2.5 thousandths over the capped hybrid DE and the capped tempering and by 6.6 over ocs_round(max_use) and the line-encoded capped DE. A seed standard deviation of 0 measures nothing about the dataset, and with one dataset per scale this file does not resolve whether that order, or the agreement within the margin at production, holds on other datasets or for capped problems in general.

The diagnosis is arithmetic and takes a minute: compare \(2n/L\), the mean number of uses per line, against the cap. If the cap is a small multiple of the mean, it is the binding restriction and the diversity budget is decoration. Raising the cap, or enlarging the candidate pool, changes the plan; tightening the budget does not, until it starts to bind.

The bound does not converge at this scale, and no gap is reported. ocs_bound() doubles its iteration count until the value stops moving by more than a relative 0.001 (the stalling criterion of Section 17.4). At the benchmark book scale it stops; at \(N = 10{,}000\) it did not within the one doubling the benchmark allowed (4,000 and 8,000 iterations, 9 multipliers; Table 17.21). The reason offered is the step size, \(1/(2\lambda \max_i \sum_j f_{ij})\), whose denominator grows with \(N\), so that the same iteration count that converges on 625 candidates is not enough on 10,000; this was not tested by running more doublings at this scale. An under-converged relaxation reports \(V(\lambda)\) too small, which makes the “bound” too small, and it stops bounding anything.

That is a finding, not a defect, and it is why the function warns and why the benchmark records no gap at this scale. At the benchmark production scale you have heuristics that agree with each other (in the capped case, to within 0.0002 index units on this dataset, inside the 0.001 margin of the capped-routes paragraph above) and no bound that has converged, so no statement of how close any of them is: the honest position, and a better one than a gap column computed from a quantity that is not a bound.

17.8 Multi-objective, and why not NSGA-II

stage2_select() traces the frontier by sweeping alpha_max and running a full optimisation at each point: the budget is a constraint, not a weight (Chapter 15), so each point is a plan a committee can approve, expressed in a budget rather than in an exchange rate.

A saving is available that is not a Pareto algorithm. Consecutive budgets have nearly identical solutions – an expectation, not measured here – so the plan found at \(\alpha_k\) is the obvious warm start for \(\alpha_{k+1}\), and stage2_select() does not pass one: its call to sel_de() carries no start argument (R/11_stage2.R). sel_local_search() already has a start = argument.

NSGA-II (non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm II) (Deb et al. 2002) returns an approximation of the whole front in one run. I have not run it on this problem, so nothing in this book says it would give worse plans per point than the sweep. The reason not to reach for it is narrower: it is built for trade-offs with no interpretable scale for one objective, and here the trade-off is already parameterised by a budget the breeder chooses, so a front is not what is being asked for. That is a design argument, not a measurement.

17.9 What not to reach for

Reinforcement learning. Younis et al. (2024) cast a breeding programme as a Markov decision process, with the number of crosses made in each generation as the action and a delayed reward, in an environment they name BREEDING GYM. Those are sequential decisions with delayed reward. Choosing 200 hybrids from a fixed candidate pool, once, with a closed-form objective, is not. The tool is aimed at a different question.

Bayesian optimisation. Built for expensive black-box objectives with a handful of continuous inputs. The swap that sel_local_search() uses costs 4.2 microseconds at \(N = 10{,}000\) (a state update, not the objective); the fitness a surrogate-based method would call costs 132 to 169 microseconds (Table 17.2); and the problem has 200 integer dimensions at the target scale. I did not run a Bayesian optimiser; this is an argument from cost and dimension.

Sampling from a determinantal point process. Determinantal point processes (DPPs) (Kulesza and Taskar 2012) are the natural probabilistic model of a diverse subset, and \(\log\det(\mathbf{I} + \beta\mathbf{f}_S)\), with \(\beta \ge 0\) a free scale, is submodular, as Chen et al. (2018) state for the DPP log-probability. That it is also monotone and non-negative is the book’s own argument, not theirs: \(\mathbf{f}\) is positive semi-definite (the Gram form of Section 17.1.1), so every eigenvalue of \(\mathbf{I} + \beta\mathbf{f}\) is at least 1, and so is that of each of its principal submatrices; the Schur complement of an added element is the reciprocal of a diagonal entry of an inverse whose eigenvalues are at most 1, hence at least 1, and each added element adds at least \(\log 1 = 0\). Monotone, non-negative and submodular, the greedy then carries the \(1 - 1/e\) guarantee of Nemhauser et al. (1978) under a cardinality constraint – a guarantee for that objective, which is not the one the programme constrains. Sampling from a DPP gives diversity in distribution when what is wanted is a single best plan, and the log-determinant lens is not \(\theta\): adopting it would change the quantity the programme is constraining. The assumption that the constraint pins one lens, \(\theta\), chosen deliberately (Chapter 15, assumption 5; Chapter 16, assumption 3), applies.

17.10 Which method to use

Table 17.22 gives the route for each situation; the protocol after it gives the order in which to run them.

As a protocol at production scale:

  1. Build ctx through fast_ctx(), and decide up front whether the \(N \times N\) matrix is going to exist, because that decision selects the rest of the protocol (Section 17.7.1). ctx$fL and ctx$parents are required by every fast route; f is required by null_distribution(), sel_greedy(), ocs_round() and sel_local_search()’s automatic warm start. At \(8N^2\) bytes it fits at \(N = 10{,}000\) and the benchmark of Section 17.7.2 forms it. If it does not fit, steps 2-4 change as noted, and the f-free subset is what you have.
  2. Get gd_ref from a resampling at the target n_sel, and set \(\alpha_{\max}\) below the attainable ceiling (Chapter 15). With no f, null_distribution() is unavailable and the resampling has to be mean(replicate(B, 1 - fast_theta(sample.int(ctx$N, n_sel), ctx))), which is fL-only and returns the same gd_ref at the same seed (Section 17.7.1).
  3. Run the sweeps that cost milliseconds – sel_truncation_cap() and, when f exists, sel_greedy() over an \(\omega\) grid spanning your data’s crossover, passing max_use if a cap applies – and record what they achieve. They are the baseline every later number is judged against. Under a cap, the capped greedy sweep is within 0.001 index units of the other capped routes at the benchmark production scale (the margin of Section 17.7.2) and is the highest of them at the benchmark book scale (Table 17.5, Table 17.20 and the capped-routes paragraph of Section 17.7.2), on one dataset per scale.
  4. Run sel_local_search() for as many seconds as you are willing to spend, with the cap passed as max_use rather than enforced afterwards. With f present, leave start = NULL and let it build the warm start; with no f, pass start = sel_truncation_cap(...) explicitly, which is the only configuration of this route that runs without the matrix. The mode is the least consequential choice in this list: over 20 seeds "hill" and "anneal" are 0.9 and 0.9 thousandths of an index unit apart at 20,000 and 30,000 moves, with a standard error of about one thousandth and no resolved sign, while "tempering" is below both in every seed. Take the simplest one unless your own data says otherwise – but those runs are uncapped, and the only mode benchmarked under a cap is "tempering".
  5. Compute ocs_bound() and check its converged attribute. Converged and the gap small: the gap is the most further search can gain on this dataset and budget, if the stalling criterion is to be trusted; stopping is a decision for the programme. Converged and the gap large: keep going. Not converged: report no gap at all, and judge the plan by agreement between independent methods instead.
  6. Validate the returned plan on the lens the constraint does not pin – F_drift and allelic richness – and on the pool decomposition, before it is used (Chapter 25).

What the chapter measured, and what it did not. The problem is \(L\)-dimensional in its state: \(\theta\) is a function of the line-usage vector and of nothing else. What was measured to follow from that is cost: an \(O(L)\) swap (4.2 us at \(N = 10{,}000\) against 132 to 169 us for a full fitness evaluation, Table 17.2), a 0.3 MiB matrix and a 200-variable contributions programme. What was not measured is how much of any difference in index between routes is due to the state: the swap-based local search differs from sel_de() in neighbourhood, acceptance rule and warm start together, so that comparison is of whole algorithms (Section 17.3), and no experiment here ranks the state collapse against the other changes. The comparison closest to isolating the encoding, sel_de() at \(n_{sel} = 200\) against sel_de_fast() at \(L = 200\), at 30,000 evaluations on boxes of the same width, still differs in the seeding and the decoder of sel_de_fast() (Section 17.3.2.1); it favours sel_de() uncapped, by 0.3 (s.e. 0.009) at the benchmark book scale and 0.11 (s.e. 0.032) at the benchmark production scale (the production difference is not resolved by three seeds), on one dataset per scale (capped: 0.004 and 0). The guarantees, the Lagrangian bound (converged at the benchmark book scale, not at production) and the oracle (\(N = 36\) only) bound how far a plan can be from the optimum where they apply, and no further.

Name the structure before choosing the algorithm: it changes what a move costs (4.2 us for the swap, against 132 us for a hybrid-encoding evaluation at \(N = 10{,}000\)) and, in the line encoding, what an evaluation costs (169 us: more, not less). Whether it also changes the index a method reaches is a separate claim that this chapter does not settle. The one comparison near it, sel_de() against sel_de_fast(), still differs in seeding and decoder as well as in encoding: uncapped, sel_de() is ahead by 0.3 (s.e. 0.009) at the benchmark book scale, which three seeds resolve, and by 0.11 (s.e. 0.032) at the benchmark production scale, which they do not; capped, the differences are 0.004 and 0, and a standard error of 0 does not mean the difference is known. One dataset per scale throughout.
