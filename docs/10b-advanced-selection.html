17  Advanced selection: structure, bounds and scale

Chapter 16 stops at a warm-started differential evolution. That is a reasonable place to stop if the problem is a black box. It is not one. This chapter names the structure, and then spends it: on cheaper moves, on genuine approximation guarantees, on a bound that is actually a bound, and on an oracle that says how far from optimal the heuristics really are.

The target throughout is the scale the method has to survive: 200 hybrids chosen from 10,000, a full factorial of 100 by 100 lines. That is an ordinary size for a hybrid maize programme at the end of a cycle: two heterotic pools of a hundred inbred or doubled-haploid lines each, every cross between them a legitimate candidate, and a few hundred of them going into the next round of multi-location testing.

That is a different – and deliberately larger – “production scale” from the one Chapter 16 quotes. There, production means the real dataset this package was written for, 71 + 71 lines and \(N = 5041\) candidates, which is the size the encoding argument is made at. Here it is the 100 + 100 factorial the scaling study and the head-to-head benchmark are run at, because a cost curve needs a span of \(N\) wider than one point provides. Neither is wrong and they are not interchangeable: every per-evaluation cost and every memory figure in this chapter is at \(N = 10{,}000\), and the dimension counts in Chapter 16 are at \(N = 5041\).

What has to be in hand, beyond Chapter 16’s three objects, is the line-level route: fast_ctx() adds ctx$parents, the \(N \times 2\) matrix of parent indices, to a context that already carries ctx$fL, the \(L \times L\) coancestry between lines. Everything in this chapter that scales is a function of those two and of nothing else.

c(candidates_N = ctx$N,
  lines_L      = ctx$n_lines,
  hybrids_kept = n_sel,
  budget_pct   = 100 * am,
  gd_ref       = gd,

#>       fL_MiB

The assumptions are Chapter 16’s, unchanged and worth restating because two of them are load-bearing here: parent lines are homozygous, which is what makes \(f_{ij}\) a function of the four parental coancestries and hence collapses the problem to \(L\) dimensions (Section 12.1); and f is molecular coancestry in \([0, 1]\), not a centred relationship matrix, which is what makes it a Gram matrix with the properties the next section needs.

17.1 Three facts about this problem

Everything below follows from three properties of f, each of which takes three lines to check and none of which has been stated in this book so far.

17.1.1 f is a Gram matrix

Molecular coancestry is \(f_{ij} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_k [x_{ik}x_{jk} + (1-x_{ik})(1-x_{jk})]\), which is an inner product of the stacked vectors \([\mathbf{x}_i;\, \mathbf{1}-\mathbf{x}_i]\). So f is not merely symmetric, it is a Gram matrix, exactly:

V  <- cbind(ctx[["X"]], 1 - ctx[["X"]])
ev <- eigen(ctx[["f"]], symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values
c(max_abs_difference = max(abs(ctx[["f"]] - tcrossprod(V) / ctx$m)),
  min_eigenvalue     = min(ev),
  min_over_max_eig   = min(ev) / max(ev))
#> max_abs_difference     min_eigenvalue   min_over_max_eig 
#>       0.000000e+00      -4.275667e-13      -1.018142e-15

The check is the whole argument: the difference is exactly zero, and the smallest eigenvalue is zero to the eigensolver’s own rounding – its sign is arbitrary 15 orders of magnitude below the largest eigenvalue. A negative eigenvalue large enough to see on that scale would mean f had been centred, rescaled, or built on the wrong marker coding – and every method in this chapter would silently lose its justification.

Two consequences carry the rest of the chapter. First, \(\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{c}\) is a convex quadratic, so the diversity budget is a convex constraint – which is what makes the exact method of Section 17.6 possible at all. Second, \(d_{ij} = f_{ii} + f_{jj} - 2f_{ij}\) is a squared Euclidean distance, hence a distance of negative type, which is the precise hypothesis the strongest approximation results require.

17.1.2 Maximising diversity is minimising the submatrix sum

\(\theta_S = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,j \in S} f_{ij}\), and at fixed \(n\) the normaliser is a constant:

s <- sel_greedy(ctx, n_sel, w = 1)
c(theta_times_n2 = theta_group(s, ctx) * n_sel^2,
  submatrix_sum  = sum(ctx[["f"]][s, s]),
  difference     = theta_group(s, ctx) * n_sel^2 - sum(ctx[["f"]][s, s]))
#> theta_times_n2  submatrix_sum     difference 
#>   2.475329e+03   2.475328e+03   1.818989e-12

So the objective is \(\lambda \sum_{i \in S} u_i - \sum_{i,j \in S} f_{ij}\): a modular term plus a submodular one, since the marginal cost of adding a hybrid grows with the set. Writing the same thing through \(d_{ij}\), \(\sum_{i<j} d_{ij} = n\,\mathrm{tr}(f_S) - n^2\theta_S\), identifies it as the max-sum diversification problem of Borodin et al. (2017). I have not found it named that way in the plant-breeding literature, and this book makes no claim about what a systematic screen would find – no such screen was run. The structural identity is the load-bearing part, and it holds whether or not anyone has written it down before.

The practical content of “fixed \(n\)” is worth spelling out: it is only at a fixed set size that maximising gene diversity and minimising the submatrix sum are the same instruction. Comparing \(\theta\) across selections of different sizes compares two different things, which is the same reason gd_ref has to be resampled at the size actually being used (Section 11.9).

17.1.3 The per-line cap is a partition matroid

“No line may parent more than max_use of the selected hybrids” is one partition matroid per heterotic pool. Together with the cardinality constraint that is an intersection of matroids – not an arbitrary combinatorial restriction, but the single best-understood constraint class in discrete optimisation.

The structure comes from the mating design, not from the mathematics. Every candidate is a cross between one pool-A line and one pool-B line, so each selected hybrid contributes exactly one use to a pool-A line and one to a pool-B line, and the counts within each pool sum to \(n\) by construction:

cnt <- line_counts(s, ctx)
c(uses_pool_A     = sum(cnt[ctx$pool == "A"]),
  uses_pool_B     = sum(cnt[ctx$pool == "B"]),
  n_sel           = n_sel,
  distinct_A_used = sum(cnt[ctx$pool == "A"] > 0),
  distinct_B_used = sum(cnt[ctx$pool == "B"] > 0))
#>     uses_pool_A     uses_pool_B           n_sel distinct_A_used distinct_B_used 
#>              60              60              60              10              23

That is exactly the ground-set partition a partition matroid needs. It also gives the feasibility condition for any cap \(c\) directly: a plan of \(n\) crosses exists under the cap only if \(n \le L_A c\) and \(n \le L_B c\). In a programme the cap is rarely chosen for theoretical reasons – it is set by how many seed-production fields a female line can support – and the point of naming the structure is that a constraint arriving from the field turns out to be the one class of constraint discrete optimisation handles best.

17.2 What lazy greedy would not buy

The obvious first move on a submodular objective is the accelerated, or “lazy”, greedy of Minoux (1978): keep stale marginal gains in a priority queue and re-evaluate only the top one, which is valid precisely because submodularity makes a stale gain an upper bound on the current one. Two distinct results are easy to conflate here, so name them apart: the acceleration is Minoux’s, while the worst-case guarantee for the plain greedy it accelerates is Nemhauser et al. (1978)’s and is quoted separately below. It is the standard accelerator for this objective class, and here it is worthless.

sel_greedy() already carries the running vector s of coancestry sums against the chosen set, so every candidate’s marginal gain is \(O(1)\) and the whole step is one vectorised pass. Lazy evaluation optimises precisely the cost that is already free. Reaching for it would add a heap, add bugs, and save nothing.

The guarantee is worth stating even so, because it settles a question rather than improving a number: greedy under our constraints cannot do worse than \(1/(p+1)\) of optimal, with \(p\) the number of matroids intersected – here the cardinality constraint plus one partition matroid per pool.

State it with its hypotheses, though, because they are not all met. The \(1 - 1/e\) and \(1/(p+1)\) results of Nemhauser et al. (1978) and Fisher et al. (1978) are proved for monotone, non-negative submodular functions. Our objective is neither in general: the index is standardised to mean zero over the candidate pool, so \(\bar u_S\) can be negative, and adding a hybrid increases the coancestry term, so the objective is monotone only where the merit term dominates. The guarantee therefore belongs in this chapter as a statement about the constraint class – the cap is not an arbitrary restriction – and not as a certificate for any particular run. The certificate this chapter actually uses is the Lagrangian bound of Section 17.4, and Section 17.6 measures what greedy does in fact achieve.

17.3 The move that was already paid for

Section 12.1 established \(\theta_S = \mathbf{w}'\mathbf{f}^L\mathbf{w}\), and theta_state()/theta_swap() have been in the package ever since, maintaining group coancestry in \(O(L)\) per swap. Nothing consumed them. Chapter 16 even ends with a callout recommending local search, citing De Beukelaer et al. (2018) – and then does not run one.

theta_swap
#> function (st, out_h, in_h, ctx) 
#> {
#>     cnt <- st$cnt
#>     v <- st$v
#>     po <- ctx$parents[out_h, ]
#>     pi_ <- ctx$parents[in_h, ]
#>     for (a in po) {
#>         cnt[a] <- cnt[a] - 1L
#>         v <- v - ctx$fL[, a]
#>     }
#>     for (a in pi_) {
#>         cnt[a] <- cnt[a] + 1L
#>         v <- v + ctx$fL[, a]
#>     }
#>     s <- st$s
#>     list(cnt = cnt, v = v, s = s, theta = sum(cnt * v)/(s * s))
#> }
#> <bytecode: 0xb2f4c3ab8>
#> <environment: namespace:hybdiv>

Four rank-one column updates. No N x N matrix is touched, and the cost depends on neither \(N\) nor \(m\). The state it maintains is three objects: the line-count vector cnt, the product v = fL %*% cnt, and their inner product divided by \(s^2\), which is \(\theta\). Removing a hybrid subtracts the two columns of fL belonging to its parents; adding one adds the two columns of the entrant’s parents. Because a swap keeps the set size fixed, the normaliser \(s = 2n\) never changes.

It is an exact update, not an approximation, and that is checkable in three lines:

st    <- theta_state(s, ctx)
out_h <- s[1]
in_h  <- setdiff(seq_len(ctx$N), s)[1]
st2   <- theta_swap(st, out_h, in_h, ctx)
after <- c(setdiff(s, out_h), in_h)
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(st2$theta, theta_group(after, ctx))))
c(incremental = st2$theta, recomputed = theta_group(after, ctx))
#> incremental  recomputed 
#>   0.6863819   0.6863819

If that identity ever fails, the state and the selection have drifted apart – the usual cause being a move that was accepted in the selection vector but not applied to the state, or the reverse.

That last property is the one that matters, and it is worth being precise about where the saving actually comes from, because the obvious answer is wrong. The line encoding does not make an evaluation cheaper. Taking two rows of Section 17.7’s table:

route at \(N = 3600\) at \(N = 10{,}000\)
hybrid encoding, mean(f[idx, idx]) 40 us 132 us
line encoding, full fitness 83 us 169 us
of which decode_lines(), which sorts all \(N\) candidates 77 us 159 us
incremental swap, no decode at all 3.3 us 4.2 us

The line encoding is slower per evaluation, because decoding a weight vector into a selection has to rank every candidate. What it buys is a smaller search space, not a cheaper objective. The order-of-magnitude saving belongs to the swap – and the swap is available only to a method that moves in the space of selections rather than decoding one from scratch each time.

args(sel_local_search)
#> function (ctx, n_sel, alpha_max, gd_ref, start = NULL, max_use = NULL, 
#>     mode = c("hill", "anneal", "tempering"), iters = 20000, n_chains = 8, 
#>     penalty = 5, t0 = NULL, swap_every = 50L, seed = NULL, trace = FALSE) 
#> NULL

Three modes, one driver, differing only in the temperature schedule. The driver does the following, and each step is a decision a breeder can inspect:

  1. Start from a plan, never from noise. start = NULL builds a warm start: capped truncation, plus the greedy sweep whenever an \(N \times N\) f is present. The test is literally nrow(ctx[["f"]]) == ctx$N, and it is worth being accurate about which branch produced the numbers in this chapter: the production benchmark of Section 17.7 deliberately forms the 0.745 GiB matrix so that the routes needing it can be timed at all, so its rows took the greedy branch. Among the candidates it takes the highest-index one that meets the budget, and if none does, the one with the smallest loss. As in sel_de(), NULL never means “start cold”.

    The greedy branch is guarded, but the ranking that follows it is not: the warm start scores every candidate with alpha_loss(), which reads ctx$f. So start = NULL needs the \(N \times N\) matrix whichever branch it takes, and on a context built without one it fails – subscript out of bounds, measured in Section 17.7.1. Supplying an explicit start = skips the whole function, and from there the search is fL-only; that is the call to make if f does not fit in memory, and Section 17.7.1 is where the two are run side by side.

  2. Build the state. theta_state() on the starting selection, plus the running index sum and a logical membership vector, so that every later question is \(O(1)\) or \(O(L)\).

  3. Calibrate the temperature. With t0 = NULL and a stochastic mode, the driver proposes up to 200 random swaps, records the median absolute change in the objective, and uses that as the starting temperature. Nothing about the scale of the index is assumed, so the same call transfers between datasets. mode = "hill" skips this entirely, since it never accepts a worsening move.

  4. Propose a feasible move. Pick one of the \(n\) selected crosses at random to leave; draw entrants at random until one is found whose parents stay under max_use once the leaving cross is removed, giving up after 20 attempts. The cap is tested, never punished.

  5. Score it in \(O(L)\). theta_swap() for the new \(\theta\), one addition and one subtraction for the new index sum, and the objective is \(\bar u_S\) minus penalty times the budget violation expressed in units of the budget itself – the violation is divided by alpha_max, so a 5 becomes an effective slope of 500 index standard deviations per unit of relative loss at a 1% budget, and 5000 at 0.1%. The tighter the budget, the harder a violation is punished, which is the behaviour you want and is not what make_fitness()’s fixed multiplier of 1000 on the absolute violation does.

  6. Accept or reject. Improving moves are always accepted; worsening ones with probability \(\exp(\Delta / t_k)\) at the current temperature. The best-ever selection is tracked separately from the current one and is what is returned – so a chain that wanders away at high temperature loses nothing.

Two design points are worth stopping on.

The cap is tested, not punished. Whether a swap keeps every line under max_use is an \(O(1)\) question given the count vector, so infeasible moves are never proposed. Penalising them instead spends the evaluation budget on being rejected – with a tight cap that was the difference between the search improving on its warm start and not moving at all.

The temperature calibrates itself. A constant would not transfer between datasets, so t0 = NULL samples the median absolute objective change over a short burn-in and uses that.

One run of each is not a comparison. Three seeds – 1, 2 and 3, the same three the cached benchmark two subsections below uses, so the two panels are read against the same draws:

Interpretation, and what limits it. Splitting a fixed move budget across tempering’s replicas costs more than the exchanges return at this size – a statement about iters = 20000 on 625 candidates, not about parallel tempering. The hill-versus-anneal ordering in particular does not survive a change in budget, which is why the second panel prints both: at 20,000 moves "hill" leads "anneal" by 6.2 thousandths of an index unit – more than the 3.7-thousandth within-mode standard deviation, so not noise at that budget – while at the benchmark’s 30,000 moves the sign flips to 0.2 thousandths in "anneal"’s favour. That ordering is therefore a property of the move budget and not of the schedule. Read this panel as evidence that the temperature schedule is a second-order choice at fixed move budget – not as a ranking of the three, and not as a claim that the move rather than the schedule is what buys the index. That claim is a comparison against the other methods and it belongs to the benchmark below, which has the dispersion to support it.

Interpretation. What moves the index at both scales measured is the neighbourhood the search moves in – the \(O(L)\) swap, available only to a method that does not decode a selection from scratch – rather than the schedule wrapped around it. Two things limit that: the ratio is 4 times smaller at production than at book scale, so it narrows with \(N\) rather than holding constant; and it rests on three seeds per cell, where the DE arms carry the larger dispersion. It is a comparison of two families at two sizes, not a law.

TipWhat the literature says about picking a metaheuristic

De Beukelaer et al. (2018), on core-collection subsets, found a plain stochastic hill-climber already competitive with a considerably more complex genetic algorithm, and parallel tempering winning by combining many simple local searches rather than by being individually cleverer.

Yadav et al. (2025) compared GA, DE, PSO and SA head to head on parent selection in wheat and ranked them by convergence AUC at 0.879, 0.860, 0.820 and 0.769, with DE running two to three times slower than GA for a marginally worse result. The current tools agree: TrainSel (Akdemir et al. 2021), which drives MateR (Fernández-González et al. 2026), is a genetic algorithm hybridised with simulated annealing.

So the differential evolution of Chapter 16 is not the obvious choice, and never was. What it had going for it was the warm start. What it lacked was a cheap move.

17.3.2 The encoding, wired up at last

Chapter 16 measures the line encoding against a fully \(N\)-dimensional one – one random key per hybrid – and finds a 5.7x margin in the line encoding’s favour at equal budget. What the call does, in order:

  1. 17.4 The bound was not a bound

Section 15.2.1 introduces the continuous relaxation as an upper bound, and the convergence check of Chapter 16 was originally written to take the best relaxation point whose own alpha falls inside the budget. That construction is not valid, and it is worth showing rather than asserting.

The relaxation at a given \(\lambda\) maximises \(\mathbf{c}'\mathbf{u} - \lambda\, \mathbf{c}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{c}\) – a penalised objective, not the index. Its optimum happens to land at some diversity, but nothing makes it the best index achievable at that diversity. A discrete plan can beat it, and does:

lagrangian_2000 is the correct form evaluated at a deliberately cheap setting – 12 multipliers, 2,000 projected-gradient iterations – and it is here only to show that the form survives where the construction does not. The correct statement is a Lagrangian one, and it takes a line. Any selection \(S\) of size \(n\) has an equal-weight contribution vector feasible for the relaxation, so with \(V(\lambda)\) the relaxation’s optimal value,

\[V(\lambda) \;\ge\; \bar u_S - \lambda\,\theta_S \;\ge\; \bar u_S - \lambda\,\theta_{\max}\]

for every \(S\) meeting the budget, and therefore \(\bar u_S \le V(\lambda) + \lambda\,\theta_{\max}\) for every non-negative \(\lambda\). The tightest such statement is the minimum over \(\lambda\).

17.5 Rounding the relaxation into a plan

With a bound in hand, the relaxation stops being only a diagnostic. Solve the optimal-contribution programme in line space, where quadprog returns instantly because the dimension is \(L\), round the contributions to integer line usage, and assign concrete crosses under both pools’ caps. That two-stage shape – contributions first, mate allocation second – is exactly Waldmann (2025)’s, whose mate-allocation stage solves in under 0.01 seconds.

The five steps inside ocs_round(), in the order they run:

  1. Line merit. Each line is scored by the mean index of the hybrids it parents in the candidate set. That quantity is a general combining ability: a property of the line against the opposite pool as represented in this factorial, never a property of the line alone (Section 10.10). Change the tester pool and the score changes.
  2. Contributions. ocs_quadprog() on ctx$fL with those merits, at a given \(\lambda\), with each line’s contribution capped at \(\texttt{max\_use} / 2n\) – the cap expressed as a share of the \(2n\) parent slots.
  3. Rounding. Contributions times \(2n\), floored, and the shortfall handed to the largest remainders; then clipped at max_use again.
  4. Mate allocation. Walk the candidates in decreasing index and take each cross whose two parents still have quota and are under the cap – a greedy b-matching. If quota runs out before \(n\) crosses are found, top up on the cap alone, because the quota is a rounding artefact and the cap is the real restriction.
  5. Choose \(\lambda\) by budget, not by preference. The whole sweep is evaluated and the plan returned is the best-index one whose realised \(\alpha\) respects alpha_max. The exchange rate stays an internal detail; the budget remains the interface.

17.6 Exact, and how far the heuristics really are

Everything so far is a heuristic with a bound. The remaining question is what the true optimum is, and the honest answer requires solving the problem exactly at a scale where that is possible.

The obvious route is closed. Maximising a linear index subject to a quadratic diversity constraint over binary variables is a mixed-integer quadratically constrained programme, and HiGHS – like most open solvers – does not accept a quadratic term together with integer variables at all.

Convexity opens a different one. Because f is positive semi-definite, \(\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{x}\) is convex, so its tangent at any point is a valid linear under-estimate, and

\[\bar{\mathbf{x}}'\mathbf{f}\bar{\mathbf{x}} + 2(\mathbf{f}\bar{\mathbf{x}})'(\mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}) \;\le\; b\]

is a linear relaxation of \(\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{x} \le b\). Solve the resulting mixed-integer linear programme, add a cut wherever the solution violates the true constraint, re-solve. This is outer approximation (Duran and Grossmann 1986), it needs only a MILP solver, and it terminates: each cut strictly removes the infeasible point that generated it.

The loop, step by step:

  1. Translate the budget. The diversity constraint becomes \(\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{f}\mathbf{x} \le n^2\,\theta_{\max}\) with \(\theta_{\max} = 1 - \text{gd\_ref}(1 - \alpha_{\max})\) – the same quantity as in the Lagrangian bound, expressed in submatrix-sum units because that is what the solver sees.
  2. Write the structural rows. One cardinality row summing to \(n\), and one row per line for the partition matroid when max_use is set. Those are linear and exact; only the quadratic needs approximating.
  3. Carry an incumbent from the start. The warm start is the same sel_local_search() warm start; if it satisfies the budget it becomes the incumbent and is handed to the solver as a starting point, so that a run stopped by the clock still reports a real interval.
  4. Solve, cut, repeat. If the MILP solution satisfies the true quadratic constraint, it is optimal for the current relaxation and the loop can stop; otherwise a gradient cut at that point is added and the MILP re-solved.
  5. Read the dual bound from the right field. objective_value is an upper bound only for a MILP solved to optimality; the moment the solver hits its own time limit the bound must come from mip_dual_bound. Cuts only tighten the relaxation, so the running minimum over iterations stays valid.

At tiny scale the loop proves optimality, and the local search matches the proven optimum to machine precision at every budget – the gap is not small, it is zero. That is the result worth having: on instances where the truth is knowable, the heuristic is not leaving anything on the table at all.

At book scale the loop does not converge, and would not at production scale either. The scale caveat cuts both ways and belongs here rather than in a footnote: “the heuristic is exactly optimal” is measured at \(N = 36\), on instances small enough to prove. It is evidence that the move and the objective are right, not evidence that the same holds at 625 or at 10,000, where the true optimum is unknown and only the interval between the best plan and the certified bound is reportable.

Memory. f is \(8N^2\) bytes: 0.745 GiB at \(N = 10{,}000\) and 3.8 GiB at \(N = 22{,}500\), which is why Chapter 12’s benchmark refuses to form it there. \(\mathbf{f}^L\) is \(200 \times 200\), or 0.3 MiB. Fixed by the line route.

Search dimension. Not what the line encoding fixes – not for the two differential evolutions, at any rate. sel_de() searches \(n_{sel}\) continuous coordinates and sel_de_fast() searches \(L\); at this chapter’s target both are 200, which is also the figure Section 17.9 quotes. The 10,000-dimensional alternative is one key per hybrid, the encoding Chapter 16 rejects and no route in this package uses. What the collapse genuinely moves to \(L\) is the contributions space: ocs_quadprog() inside ocs_round() solves a 200-variable quadratic programme where ocs_relaxation() works on a 10,000-variable contribution vector, which is why ocs_round() (uncapped, no max_use) sweeps its ten \(\lambda\) values and returns a plan in 157 ms at production scale. That number is the rounding and nothing else: the benchmark passes bound = a value computed once, outside the timed block, and at \(N = 10{,}000\) that value comes back uncertified, so the plan is what is fast here and there is no certificate at this scale to be fast about. The paragraph after the table below is where that is measured. Fixed for the quadratic programme; already true for the DE.

Cost per evaluation. mean(f[idx, idx]) is \(O(n^2)\) in the selection, so it degrades exactly as the problem gets interesting – but decode_lines() is \(O(N \log N)\), so the line encoding does not fix this and at these sizes is slightly worse. Fixed only by not decoding, which is what the swap neighbourhood does.

Scaling up is therefore not a matter of buying time. It is a matter of not forming f at all: build ctx$fL and ctx$parents, and use the routes that read only those. That last clause is a real restriction and not a formality, because most of this package still reaches for the \(N \times N\) matrix, and fast_ctx() does not change that – it adds parents to whatever context it is handed and neither builds f nor drops it. The constructor with the switch is ctx_from_pools(sim, with_f = FALSE), and the next section is what actually happens on the context it returns.

Had the selection been small enough to index a \(20 \times 20\) matrix legally, the same line would have returned a diversity computed from the wrong matrix, silently.

So the honest scaling recipe has two branches. If f fits, form it: the benchmark below does, on purpose, and the whole chapter’s numbers come from that branch. If it does not fit, the f-free subset is the whole pipeline you get – sel_truncation_cap() for a starting plan, fast_theta() over random subsets for gd_ref in place of null_distribution() (same seed, same draws, the same gd_ref to machine precision, which the chunk above asserts), sel_local_search(start = ...) and sel_de_fast() for the search, ocs_quadprog() and ocs_bound() for the contributions and the bound – and the greedy sweep, the resampled null and the rounding route are simply not available.

The diagnosis is arithmetic and takes a minute: compare \(2n/L\), the mean number of uses per line, against the cap. If the cap is a small multiple of the mean, it is the binding restriction and the diversity budget is decoration. Raising the cap, or enlarging the candidate pool, changes the plan; tightening the budget does not.

The certificate does not scale, and the bound is reported as uncertified. ocs_bound() doubles its iteration count until the value stops moving. At book scale it converges; at \(N = 10{,}000\) it does not, within any budget worth spending. The reason is in the step size, \(1/(2\lambda \max_i \sum_j f_{ij})\), whose denominator grows with \(N\) – so the same iteration count that converges on 625 candidates is nowhere near enough on 10,000. An under-converged relaxation reports \(V(\lambda)\) too small, which makes the “bound” too small, and it stops bounding anything.

17.8 Multi-objective, and why not NSGA-II

stage2_select() traces the frontier by sweeping alpha_max and running a full optimisation at each point. That is already the epsilon-constraint method, and it is the right one: each point is a plan a committee can approve, expressed in a budget rather than in an exchange rate (Chapter 15).

The available saving is not a Pareto algorithm. It is that consecutive budgets have nearly identical solutions, so the plan found at \(\alpha_k\) is the obvious warm start for \(\alpha_{k+1}\) – which the sweep currently throws away. That is a start = argument, not a new optimiser.

NSGA-II would return the whole front in one run, at worse quality per point and with a crowding heuristic that has nothing to say about a constraint the breeder has already chosen. For a one-dimensional trade-off already parameterised by an interpretable budget, it is machinery in search of a problem.

17.9 What not to reach for

Reinforcement learning. BreedGym and its successors (Younis et al. 2024) cast the breeding programme as a Markov decision process: how to allocate resources across cycles, when to recycle, how many crosses to make each generation. Those are sequential decisions with delayed reward. Choosing 200 hybrids from a fixed candidate pool, once, with a closed-form objective, is not. The tool is aimed at a different question.

Bayesian optimisation. For expensive black-box objectives with a handful of continuous inputs. Ours costs 5 microseconds and has 200 integer dimensions.

Sampling from a determinantal point process. DPPs are the natural probabilistic model of a diverse subset, and \(\log\det(\mathbf{I} + \beta \mathbf{f}_S)\) is genuinely submodular and monotone, so its greedy carries a \(1 - 1/e\) guarantee (Chen et al. 2018). But sampling from a DPP gives diversity in distribution when what is wanted is a single best plan, and the log-determinant lens is not \(\theta\) – adopting it would quietly change the quantity the programme is constraining. Chapter 11’s rule applies: one diversity lens, chosen deliberately, reported against a null.

As a protocol at production scale:

  1. Build ctx through fast_ctx(), and decide up front whether the \(N \times N\) matrix is going to exist, because that decision selects the rest of the protocol (Section 17.7.1). ctx$fL and ctx$parents are required by every fast route; f is required by null_distribution(), sel_greedy(), ocs_round() and sel_local_search()’s automatic warm start. At \(8N^2\) bytes it fits at \(N = 10{,}000\) and the benchmark above forms it. If it does not fit, steps 2-4 change as noted, and the f-free subset is what you have.
  2. Get gd_ref from a resampling at the target n_sel, and set \(\alpha_{\max}\) below the attainable ceiling (Chapter 15). With no f, null_distribution() is unavailable and the resampling has to be mean(replicate(B, 1 - fast_theta(sample.int(ctx$N, n_sel), ctx))), which is fL-only and returns the same gd_ref at the same seed (Section 17.7.1).
  3. Run the sweeps that cost milliseconds – sel_truncation_cap() and, when f exists, sel_greedy() over a \(w\) grid spanning your data’s crossover, passing max_use if a cap applies – and record what they achieve. They are the baseline every later number is judged against, and under a cap they are also, at both scales measured here, the winner (the table above).
  4. Run sel_local_search() for as many seconds as you are willing to spend, with the cap passed as max_use rather than enforced afterwards. With f present, leave start = NULL and let it build the warm start; with no f, pass start = sel_truncation_cap(...) explicitly, which is the only configuration of this route that runs without the matrix. The mode is the least consequential choice in this list: on the benchmark above "hill" and "anneal" are 0.2 thousandths of an index unit apart at book scale and 2.4 at production, with the sign of the difference changing between them, while "tempering" trails at both. Take the simplest one unless your own data says otherwise – but note that those three are the uncapped rows, and the only mode benchmarked under a cap is "tempering".
  5. Compute ocs_bound() and check its converged attribute. Certified and the gap small: stop. Certified and the gap large: keep going. Uncertified: report no gap at all, and judge the plan by agreement between independent methods instead.
  6. Validate the returned plan on the lens the constraint does not pin – F_drift and allelic richness – and on the pool decomposition, before it is used (Chapter 21).

The honest summary is that one change matters more than the rest put together, and it is not an algorithm and not an encoding either. The problem was always \(L\)-dimensional in its state – \(\theta\) is a function of the line-usage vector and of nothing else – and that is what buys the \(O(L)\) swap, the 0.3 MiB matrix and the 200-variable contributions programme. What it does not buy is a better parameter vector for a population method: sel_de() at \(n_{sel} = 200\) and sel_de_fast() at \(L = 200\) search boxes of the same width, and the measured difference between them is small and runs the other way. Everything else in this chapter – the guarantees, the corrected bound, the oracle – is there to tell you when you are done, which turns out to be much earlier than the size of the candidate set suggests.

Name the structure before choosing the algorithm. The difficulty in this problem was in the state, not in the optimiser – and a claim about an encoding is only as good as the two things it was actually measured between.
