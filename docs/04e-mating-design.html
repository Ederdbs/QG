18  How few hybrids are enough? Mating designs for a two-pool programme

The selection problems of the Optimisation part (Chapter 15, Chapter 16, Chapter 17) start from a list of hybrids with predicted values, and Chapter 6 builds that list as the complete \(A \times B\) factorial: every cross formed once, every value known. Earlier chapters did ask where such a list comes from, and each deferred the answer to this one: Chapter 1 describes the evaluation of lines in cross, against testers or in a sparse factorial; Chapter 9 (“How many testers, how many locations”) gives a tester-design accuracy for a simulated programme; Section 5.13.6 checks that a crossing layout identifies what one intends to fit. None of them chooses the crossing plan. This chapter does, and it therefore removes the assumption, made by the Optimisation part, that the values of the whole factorial are known: what that part receives after a sparse plan is a prediction, and this chapter measures how good it is.

The list comes from a crossing plan, and the crossing plan is the most constrained decision a hybrid programme makes. Two pools of 200 candidate lines each define a factorial of 40,000 single crosses. A field holds 1,000 to 3,000 plots. Every hybrid programme is therefore choosing a sample of a factorial whose cells are overwhelmingly empty, and the classical tester – every candidate crossed to one or two lines of the opposite pool – is one sample among many. This chapter treats the choice of sample as the design problem it is, and defends three results:

  1. At equal field size, a connected sparse factorial among the candidates estimates general combining ability better than a tester design, at every budget studied, by 0.082 to 0.125 correlation points.
  2. The analysis matters most for the sparsest designs. Analysed by least squares, the sparsest factorials are the worst designs in the study; analysed by BLUP, they climb out of the bottom, \(c = 3\) into the top third and \(c = 2\) to mid-table (at 2,000 plots, circulant \(c = 3\) moves from rank 23 to rank 7 of 24 designs, \(c = 2\) from 24 to 14). The cost of least squares is not general: under least squares at 2,000 plots, random sparse \(c = 10\), NC II and circulant \(c = 10\) still exceed the best tester (tester k=4) in the cached sweep, by 0.036 to 0.086 correlation points; the in-repo sweep reproduces this for random \(c = 10\) and NC II but not for circulant \(c = 10\) (Table 18.11, Section 18.10).
  3. Genomic kernels did not raise GCA accuracy for lines that were crossed; they exceeded phenotypic BLUP only in two designs that field a quarter of the candidates. Where every candidate is in the field, genomic kernels did not raise GCA accuracy. They exceeded phenotypic BLUP in two designs that field only a quarter of the candidates, chosen by CDmean – one cached run, three distinct cells, one of them below this chapter’s own threshold – and in those the genomic accuracy was not distinguished from that of randomly chosen lines, while the phenotypic arm fell (Section 18.6). The interpretation, not a result, is that the value of genomic information lies in predicting lines that were never crossed, for which the phenotypic model gives no prediction at all; the sweep does not quantify a gain for them, only the hybrid-level accuracy of untested-parent hybrids (T0), reported in Section 18.6.

One word, three objects. In the Optimisation part a hybrid is a cross between a pool-A and a pool-B line, and selection acts on its predicted value. In this chapter, unqualified, a cross is a filled cell of the design: an \(A \times B\) single cross that is made and planted to be tested; the cells not in the design are untested hybrids, which can only be predicted. A third object, the within-pool cross made to produce the next generation of lines (stage S2 of Table 1.1), is the subject of the literature reviewed in Section 18.6 and is not designed here.

The running example throughout is one simulated maize programme: two heterotic pools, 200 doubled-haploid candidates per pool, a field of 1,000 to 3,000 hybrid plots, and a winter nursery that must physically produce the seed of every cross that enters the field.

18.1 Why the crossing plan is the binding decision

The crossing plan, not the statistical model and not the marker platform, sets the ceiling on what a hybrid programme can learn in a cycle. Which lines can be ranked, whether specific combining ability is estimable at all, how many plots each hybrid can occupy, whether untested combinations can be predicted – every one of these is fixed the moment the nursery plan is written. A mixed model fitted afterwards can only extract what the plan put there.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. A 200 x 200 factorial against 1,000 to 3,000 plots is a sampling fraction of 2.5 to 7.5 % at one plot per hybrid, and 1.25 to 3.75 % at two plots per hybrid, the minimum for replication. Three decisions are usually conflated inside “the testing plan”, and it pays to separate them:

  • which lines enter the field at all – every candidate, or a subset chosen by some criterion;
  • which crosses among those lines are made – to a common tester, to each other in a sparse pattern, or in complete blocks;
  • how many plots each cross receives – replicates and locations.

The organising question of the chapter follows: for a fixed budget, which sample of the factorial maximises the accuracy of combining-ability estimates and the selection response they support? And one constraint is stated now, because it is the one most often forgotten: a crossing plan is not a matrix, it is a nursery. Each filled cell is a pollination that must yield enough seed for its plots.

The three canonical samples drawn over the same grid make the difference visible before any statistics. The plans below are produced by the package’s generators, at 40 x 40 so that individual cells can be seen:

set.seed(3)

The tester fills two full columns and two full rows: every line meets the same partners, so lines are compared on a common background but the background is narrow. The circulant design spreads the same number of crosses as thin diagonals: every line meets different partners, every line is crossed exactly four times, and – the property that the next section shows to matter – every line is linked to every other through a chain of shared partners. NC II fills more cells than either and still cannot compare a line in the first block with a line in the second.

18.2 Combining ability as an estimation problem

General and specific combining ability are properties of a reference population of crosses, and a design estimates them only to the extent that it samples that population. The concepts are classically attributed to Sprague and Tatum (1942); the distinction between fixed-effect inference about a specific set of parents and random-effect inference about the population they were drawn from is classically attributed to Griffing (1956) (page-level support not checked for this book). The North Carolina layouts, in which a factorial between two sets of parents separates the components of variance, are attributed to Comstock and Robinson (1948); that attribution, and the assignment of Design II to this paper rather than to a later publication, was not verified for this book. Reciprocal recurrent selection, the scheme that uses both combining abilities, is the subject of Comstock et al. (1949), cited here for its title and existence only.

18.2.1 The model

At the level of a cross mean the model is Equation 1.1, \(\bar y_{ij} = \mu + g^A_i + g^B_j + s_{ij} + \bar e_{ij}\), with \(\bar y_{ij}\) the mean of cross \(i \times j\) over its \(r_{ij}\) plots, \(\mu\) the mean of the reference set of crosses, \(g^A_i\), \(g^B_j\) the GCA of the pool-A and pool-B parent, \(s_{ij}\) the SCA of the pair and \(\bar e_{ij}\) the plot error of the mean. This chapter adds the error variance,

\[\mathrm{Var}(\bar e_{ij}) = \sigma^2_e / r_{ij}, \tag{18.1}\]

with \(\sigma^2_e\) the plot-error variance (s2e, defined from a single-plot heritability of 0.3) and \(r_{ij} \ge 1\) the plots the cross received. The other variances are \(\sigma^2_{gA}\) and \(\sigma^2_{gB}\), over the candidate lines of each pool (vc$A, vc$B), and \(\sigma^2_s\), that of \(s_{ij}\) (vc$S; written \(\sigma^2_{SCA}\) in Chapter 5, where it is a closed form for the heterosis part). Where one GCA variance is needed, as in every closed form below, it is \(\sigma^2_g = (\sigma^2_{gA} + \sigma^2_{gB})/2\), the mean of the two pools. \(n_A\) and \(n_B\) are the candidate lines per pool, and \(n_A'\), \(n_B'\) those that receive at least one cross. In Chapter 19 and Chapter 20 the pool-A line is the female and the pool-B line the male. Two ratios will be quoted and must not be confused: the SCA:GCA ratio of this chapter is \(\sigma^2_s/\sigma^2_g\), and the SCA share is \(\sigma^2_s/(\sigma^2_{gA} + \sigma^2_{gB} + \sigma^2_s)\), the proportion of the genetic variance among cross means. In the cached run they are 0.378 and 0.159. Neither is the SCA share of the heterosis variance of Chapter 5, which excludes the additive variance and assumes neutral divergence.

Section 5.11 derived this decomposition from allele frequencies for the dominance part of the hybrid value, and showed that a pool-A line’s GCA is an average effect evaluated at pool B’s frequencies. There \(g^A_i\) is that dominance part alone, which that chapter calls the GCA for heterosis; here \(g^A_i\) is the whole GCA of the cross mean. With an additive effect \(a_k\) added to the dominance effect \(d_k\) at locus \(k\), the general combining ability of pool-A line \(i\) in the factorial of the candidate lines is

\[g^A_i = \sum_k \bigl[a_k + d_k (1 - 2\bar v_k)\bigr]\,\delta u_{ik}, \qquad \delta u_{ik} = u_{ik} - \bar u_k, \tag{18.2}\]

with \(u_{ik}, v_{jk} \in \{0, 1\}\) the genotype of inbred pool-A line \(i\) and pool-B line \(j\) at locus \(k\), and \(\bar u_k\), \(\bar v_k\) their means over the candidate lines of the factorial, not over the pool (the two converge as the number of lines grows; the identity below holds for the former only). Genotypic value is \(a_k(u + v - 1) + d_k(u + v - 2uv)\) per locus, so the row effect of the product term \(-2d_k u_{ik}v_{jk}\) is \(-2d_k\,\delta u_{ik}\bar v_k\) and the rest is linear. The structure – an average effect of substitution against the other population – is that of Stuber and Cockerham (1966) for a cross between two populations; the equation is checked here, not taken from that paper. The additive part adds a term and changes nothing else, which is quick to confirm: the row effects of a complete factorial of genotypic values equal cU %*% a plus the gca_A of heterosis_gca_sca(), with no second implementation. The same chunk shows what the identity does not cover: with the frequencies of the pool that generated the lines in place of \(\bar u_k\), \(\bar v_k\), the two sides differ.

Show code 
set.seed(7)
m  <- 300
pA <- runif(m, 0.1, 0.9); pB <- runif(m, 0.1, 0.9)
U  <- matrix(rbinom(30 * m, 1, rep(pA, each = 30)), 30)   # 30 inbred lines, pool A
V  <- matrix(rbinom(25 * m, 1, rep(pB, each = 25)), 25)   # 25 inbred lines, pool B
a  <- rnorm(m, 0, 0.3); d <- rnorm(m, 0.2, 0.2)
# Genotypic value of every single cross: a(u + v - 1) + d * heterozygosity
G  <- outer(seq_len(30), seq_len(25), Vectorize(function(i, j)
        sum(a * (U[i, ] + V[j, ] - 1) + d * (U[i, ] + V[j, ] - 2 * U[i, ] * V[j, ]))))
cs <- heterosis_gca_sca(U, V, d)
gcaA_formula <- drop(sweep(U, 2, colMeans(U)) %*% a) + cs$gca_A
row_eff <- rowMeans(G) - mean(G)
# @eq-mating-gca with candidate-set frequencies, and with the pool frequencies
gca_cand <- drop(sweep(U, 2, colMeans(U)) %*% (a + d * (1 - 2 * colMeans(V))))
gca_pool <- drop(sweep(U, 2, pA) %*% (a + d * (1 - 2 * pB)))
c(max_abs_diff_heterosis_gca_sca = max(abs(row_eff - gcaA_formula)),
  max_abs_diff_candidate_freq    = max(abs(row_eff - gca_cand)),
  max_abs_diff_pool_freq         = max(abs(row_eff - gca_pool)),
  sd_row_effect                  = sd(row_eff))
#> max_abs_diff_heterosis_gca_sca    max_abs_diff_candidate_freq 
#>                   2.042810e-14                   1.909584e-14 
#>         max_abs_diff_pool_freq                  sd_row_effect 
#>                   9.489157e-01                   2.165922e+00
stopifnot(max(abs(row_eff - gcaA_formula)) < 1e-10,
          max(abs(row_eff - gca_cand)) < 1e-10,
          max(abs(row_eff - gca_pool)) > 1e-3)

Result. The row effects of the complete factorial equal Equation 18.2 with the candidate-set frequencies, and the gca_A of heterosis_gca_sca() plus the additive term, to machine precision (asserted below \(10^{-10}\)); with the pool frequencies the largest difference is 0.95 against a standard deviation of the row effects of 2.17, at 30 + 25 lines and 300 loci. Interpretation. “True GCA” in this chapter is always the quantity on the left – the row and column effects a breeder would estimate by growing every hybrid of the candidate lines without error – and every accuracy reported is meant as a correlation with it, over all candidates of a pool, crossed or not (for the cached sweep this is the estimand as the study describes it; the repository holds only the realised factorial variances of check V2). The population GCA, at pool frequencies, is a different object, which the finite panel approximates; this chapter does not use it.

18.2.2 Four quantities that control a design

The third row is the quantitative form of the classical argument for using more than one tester (attributed to Sprague and Tatum (1942); not checked at page level here), and the fourth is its square root as an accuracy: the square root of the heritability, for GCA, of the mean of a line’s \(k\) cross means. Both describe least squares in a tester design, and the derivation uses that: the \(k\) partners are the same for every candidate, so their GCA is a constant that the within-pool centring removes, and the error of a line’s estimate is the mean of \(k\) independent \(s_{ij}\) and \(\bar e_{ij}\). In a sparse factorial each line has its own partners, which are themselves unknown candidates, and nothing in the derivation covers that noise. gca_accuracy_theory(c, r, ...) can nevertheless be evaluated at \(c\) crosses per line, and it is useful to see what it then returns against the cached accuracies of the two estimators, at 2,000 plots:

The expression is a reliable description of least squares on a tester design and optimistic for any sparse design, under BLUP as well as under least squares; a shrinkage estimator on a connected sparse design recovers much of what least squares loses, which is why Section 18.5 exists, but not all of it, and the table does not decompose the shortfall. What limits the inference: one cached run, accuracies at nominal \(c\) and mean realised \(r\), and the closed form taken at the mean of the two pools’ GCA variances.

18.2.3 Connectivity, in one paragraph

ImportantThe rank rule

A mating design is a bipartite graph: pool-A lines on one side, pool-B lines on the other, an edge for every cross. GCA contrasts are estimable within a connected component of that graph and not between components, so the rank of the GCA design matrix is \(n_A' + n_B' - (\text{components})\), one lost constraint per component. That is the formal content of what breeders call “ties between sets”, and it was settled long ago: Weeks and Williams (1964) gave the criterion for an \(N\)-way classification and Fernando et al. (1983) an algorithm for the connected subsets of a two-way one.

design_diagnostics() computes both sides of the rule independently – the numerical rank by QR, the components by union-find over the graph – so a disagreement would be a bug, not a modelling choice:

Show code 
set.seed(2026)
plans <- list("tester k=1"        = design_tester(n, n, 1),
              "tester k=2"        = design_tester(n, n, 2),
              "random sparse c=2" = design_random_sparse(n, n, 2),
              "circulant c=2"     = design_circulant(n, n, 2),
              "circulant c=5"     = design_circulant(n, n, 5),
              "NC II 10x10 sets"  = design_nc2(n, n, 10, 10))
diag_tab <- do.call(rbind, lapply(names(plans), function(nm) {
  d <- design_diagnostics(plans[[nm]], n, n)
  data.frame(design = nm, crosses = d$n_crosses,
             untested = 2 * n - d$lines_A - d$lines_B,
             components = d$components, rank_gca = d$rank_gca,
             rank_expected = d$rank_expected, df_sca = d$df_sca)
}))
stopifnot(all(diag_tab$rank_gca == diag_tab$rank_expected))
diag_tab

Result. The rank equals the graph prediction in every row. The NC II layout – twenty disconnected 10 x 10 blocks – has rank 380, not 399: twenty components, twenty lost constraints. The random sparse design at two crosses per line leaves 53 candidates with no cross at all and splits the rest into 11 components; the circulant design with the same number of crosses is a single component with every line crossed exactly twice. One tester per pool is connected and has zero residual df: \(n_A + n_B - 1\) crosses for \(n_A + n_B - 1\) GCA parameters. The GCA model is saturated and SCA is not merely imprecise, it is not identifiable.

What “not estimable” means is checked directly. A contrast between two lines of the same NC II block lies in the row space of the design matrix; a contrast between lines of different blocks does not:

Show code 
nc <- plans[["NC II 10x10 sets"]]
l_within  <- c(0, 1, -1, rep(0, 2 * n - 2))           # A1 - A2, same block
l_between <- c(0, 1, rep(0, 9), -1, rep(0, 2 * n - 11)) # A1 - A11, next block
rbind(within  = unlist(design_estimable(nc, n, n, l_within)),
      between = unlist(design_estimable(nc, n, n, l_between)))
#>         estimable rel_residual
#> within          1 3.831056e-16
#> between         0 2.236068e-01

The within-block residual is rounding error; the between-block residual is 0.224, a contrast nowhere near the row space. Any least-squares ranking that crosses block boundaries in this design is a statement about the analyst’s centring convention, not about the lines.

BLUP does rank across blocks, and in the simulations below NC II scores well on all-lines accuracy. It can, because a random-effects model supplies the missing constraint from its prior: every block’s lines are assumed drawn from the same pool distribution, so every block mean is shrunk to the same value. That assumption is exactly true in a simulation that assigns lines to blocks at random, and exactly what fails when blocks are families, years or breeders’ sub-programmes with real mean differences. Disconnection is not repaired by the estimator; it is replaced by an assumption, and the assumption should be stated.

18.2.4 SCA and the cross-level residual are the same thing in cross means

One more property of the model must be stated before any variance component is reported. Each cross has its own SCA, so in cross-mean data \(s_{ij}\) and \(\bar e_{ij}\) occupy exactly the same position: only their sum \(\sigma^2_s + \sigma^2_e / r\) is identified. The cached study separated them by an external plot-error variance and estimating \(\sigma^2_s\) (the validation file does not record how \(\sigma^2_e\) was fixed; this is as the study describes it); an independent REML implementation (sommer) was compared with the in-house one (check V6), and the validation file records that only the sum is identified there, which the two agreed on to 2e-06 relative error. A reported \(\sigma^2_s\) from cross means is only as good as the external plot-error estimate it was separated with. fit_gca() in this package sidesteps the question by taking the components as known, and weights each cross mean by \(1/(\sigma^2_s + \sigma^2_e/r)\) for exactly this reason.

18.3 The classical tester approach

The tester design answers the sampling problem by collapsing one margin of the table. Each candidate is crossed to \(k\) testers of the opposite pool, and in the usual reciprocal arrangement each pool’s candidates are tested against the other’s testers, giving \(k(n_A + n_B) - k^2\) crosses. Evaluating inbred lines in crossbred combinations is an old practice, and Jenkins and Brunson (1932) compared methods for it; the argument for general versus specific combining ability is classically attributed to Sprague and Tatum (1942) and Hull (1945) (not checked at page level here), and the modern treatment of tester choice is Hallauer et al. (2010).

18.3.1 What a tester is for, and what it trades

A tester is a deliberate variance trade. It buys cheap seed production and perfect connectivity – every line tied to every other through the common testers – at the price of confounding a line’s GCA with its SCA to the testers used, and of a training set that covers the relationship space of the opposite pool very thinly. The testcross mean of line \(i\) estimates \(g^A_i\) plus the mean of \(k\) SCA deviations, so its error variance carries \(\sigma^2_s/k\) and \(\sigma^2_e/(k r)\). Adding testers buys down both terms; adding replicates buys down only one. That is why the allocation literature treats tester number as a decision variable jointly with lines, locations and stages (C. Longin et al. 2006), and why it is not interchangeable with replication.

18.3.2 Choice of tester

The long-standing debate is between broad-base testers, elite inbreds and elite single crosses. The theoretical argument against a high-performing tester is Smith (1986)‘s: for many loci with substantial dominance, favourable dominant alleles carried by a good tester mask the candidate’s alleles, reducing the variance among testcrosses and with it the correlation between testcross and line value. A good tester discriminates less. The counter-argument is operational: an elite tester produces hybrids that are themselves candidate products, so the testcross trial doubles as a product trial in the background where the line will actually be sold. Programmes have resolved the tension by staging – discriminating testers early, elite testers late, single-cross evaluation last – and the number of testers per stage is one of the levers of the scheme. In a deterministic budget model of one Zimbabwean maize programme – not a simulation of genotypes, and by its authors’ own statement not extrapolable to other programmes – replacing a 4-1-1 tester plan with a 2-2-1 plan raised gain per dollar by 8.2 % and cut the cost of a unit of gain by 7.4 %; combined with doubled haploids and a higher selection intensity at the first two stages, the figures were 43.1 % and 28.5 % (Chaingeni et al. 2025).

18.3.3 What testers cannot deliver

Three limitations recur, and the simulation bears on two of them.

No SCA in the reference population. With \(k = 1\) the GCA model is saturated and \(\sigma^2_s\) is not identifiable at all (df_sca = 0 in Table 18.4). With \(k \ge 2\) it is identified. The model gives a reason to expect it to be estimated with the testers: the testcross mean of a line contains the mean of the same \(k\) SCA deviations for every line, so what the design samples is the interaction of the candidates with those \(k\) partners, not with the population of crosses the programme will sell. A thin training set. All hybrids of a candidate share one or two partners, so the design explores a narrow slice of the hybrid relationship space. Testcross was the worst of the designs compared as a training set for predicting the single crosses of a full diallel (Fritsche-Neto et al. 2018), and simulation agrees that the deficit grows with the SCA share (Seye et al. 2020).

18.3.4 When the tester is still right

None of this makes testers obsolete. They remain the right answer when seed production rather than plot capacity binds – a tester cross is made in bulk in an isolation block or on a male-sterile female, a sparse factorial needs many small hand pollinations (Section 18.7) – when the candidate set is so large that the first screen must be cheap per line, in programmes that cannot fit a mixed model and whose field holds only two or three crosses per line (Section 18.5), and in the first cycle of a new pool, where there is no relationship information to exploit. González-Diéguez et al. (2026) make the case for a hybrid of the two logics: three to five testers allocated sparsely across families rather than every tester crossed to every line.

18.4 Sparse and incomplete factorials

Once candidates are crossed to each other rather than to a common tester, the design becomes a sparse bipartite graph, and two things matter: how many crosses each line gets, \(c\), and whether the graph is connected. Which particular partners are drawn matters much less.

18.4.1 The design family

The classical theory is the partial diallel of Kempthorne and Curnow (1961): each parent crossed to a fixed number \(s\) of others, with the sampling variances of the genetic components derived as functions of \(s\). Its two-pool analogue is the incomplete factorial, each pool-A line crossed to \(c\) of the \(n_B\) pool-B lines. The members studied here:

  • random sparse – \(c \cdot n_A\) cells drawn at random. Lines receive a Binomial number of crosses, some none; the graph may be disconnected.
  • circulant (round-robin) – pool-A line \(i\) crossed to pool-B lines \((i - 1 + o) \bmod n_B + 1\) for \(c\) offsets \(o\). With \(n_A = n_B\) every line in both pools has exactly \(c\) crosses, and choosing offsets with \(\gcd(n_B, \Delta o) = 1\) guarantees one component. design_circulant() nudges the last offset until that holds.
  • NC II in sets – complete \(a \times b\) factorials on disjoint line sets (the layout attributed to Comstock and Robinson (1948)); deliberately disconnected.
  • criterion-optimised – greedy exchange towards A-optimality, D-optimality or CDmean (Rincent et al. 2012); relationship-guided – partners chosen to minimise genomic similarity between parents. Both appear in the cached sweep only.

18.4.2 How sparse is sparse enough – the published evidence

Remarkably sparse, provided the design is structured and analysed well. The most informative single data point is operational rather than simulated: a commercial winter oilseed rape programme ran 4,000 hybrids over 61 unreplicated trials covering 1.67 % of all mother x father combinations, and a GCA + SCA model still reached accuracies of 0.63, 0.84 and 0.80 for yield, oil and protein after a further 50 % of the observations were artificially removed (Thomsen et al. 2026). Across maize, wheat and rice, training sets of 2 to 13 % of the hybrid set sufficed for genomic prediction (Guo et al. 2019). A 363-hybrid dent x flint factorial predicted an independent 951-hybrid factorial as well as or better than tester designs at equal numbers of hybrids and lines (Lorenzi et al. 2022); NC II was the best and testcross the worst training set for predicting a full diallel (Fritsche-Neto et al. 2018); and in simulation the factorial’s advantage grew with the SCA share (Seye et al. 2020).

The design principle that this chapter’s own sweep supports is to spend the hybrid budget on parents, not on depth per parent – up to the point where \(c\) is so small that connectivity and within-line precision fail – and the published evidence says that the point depends on what is to be predicted. Melchinger et al. (2023) found, in their first data set (DS1) at a training-set size of 96 hybrids, for hybrids with both parents untested (H0), the accuracy maximal at \(c = 1\), each parent in a single cross, exceeding \(c = 2\) and \(c = 4\) by about 1 to 3 % and 5 to 10 % (largest differences at high heritability); for hybrids with one or both parents in the training set (H1, H2), and for the GCA of lines that are in it, \(c = 1\) gave the lowest accuracy in DS1, and the optimum \(c\) across hybrid types depends on the size and resources of the programme. Their second data set (DS2) is described as showing essentially the same picture with some trends amplified: at 96 training hybrids the difference between \(c = 1\) and \(c = 4\) for H0 hybrids was larger than in DS1 in all six scenarios, with an attainable accuracy at \(c = 1\) of 0.50 to 0.65 at low and 0.70 to 0.85 at high heritability, while the accuracy of H1 hybrids hardly differed between \(c = 1\), 2 and 4. The percentages above are DS1 figures; no DS2 percentage is quoted here. That is a trade between predicting new lines and estimating the ones already crossed, at a fixed number of hybrids; the sweep below fixes plots, not hybrids, and scores GCA of lines in the field, so it is not a test of that paper (Table 18.15).

18.4.5 What is lost

SCA is weakly estimated at low \(c\). The circulant design with \(c = 2\) has exactly 1 residual degree of freedom at cross-mean level (Table 18.4) – enough to fit a model and not enough to estimate a variance component – and the cached REML SCA:GCA ratio there is off the reference by 23.9 % as a ratio of replicate-mean components (34.4 % as a mean of ratios), the largest error in Table 18.5 under either statistic. The same table shows errors of 12.1 % at \(c = 5\), so the cached REML evidence does not by itself separate \(c = 2\) from higher \(c\); the structural fact, one residual df at \(c = 2\) against 601 at \(c = 5\) (Table 18.4), does. The recommendation therefore splits by objective: \(c = 2\) to rank lines, \(c \ge 3\) before any statement about SCA variance.

Sparsity in crosses also interacts with sparsity in environments. A parallel literature, mostly from CIMMYT, allocates entries to subsets of environments and predicts the unobserved line-by-environment cells (Jarquin et al. 2020; Atanda et al. 2021, 2022; Montesinos-López et al. 2023). When the genetic correlation between managements is as low as 0.37 (Atanda et al. 2021), a line observed only in one is weakly informative about the other. A design observing 2 % of the hybrid factorial and 30 % of the environment cells within that observes well under 1 % of the information space, and the two sparsities have not been studied together for hybrids (Section 18.9). Chapter 19 takes the environment axis on its own and Chapter 20 the two together.

Interpretation of the estimator contrast. That the circulant design loses more to least squares than the random one at the same \(c\) is not a contradiction of the previous section. The circulant design’s partners are each other’s partners in long chains, which is what BLUP can exploit and least squares cannot; this is a mechanism proposed here, not one the sweep isolates.

The practical consequence is conditional on \(c\) and on the layout: a programme that cannot or will not fit a mixed model should avoid the sparsest layouts (\(c \le 3\) per line, circulant \(c = 5\)) – under least squares they are worse than a tester – while a design with \(c = 10\) or an NC II layout retains an advantage over the best tester in this sweep (Table 18.11). The literature that motivated BLUP of single crosses (Bernardo 1994, 1996) and the allocation tools that assume it (Endelman et al. 2014; Mi et al. 2014) are cited here for that context; this chapter has not checked, page by page, what they state about the estimator.

18.6 Genomic information for deciding which crosses to make

Genomic information changes the question from how well can I estimate the lines I crossed? to which crosses should I make, and what can I say about the ones I did not? Its value is in extrapolation and in the choice of what to field, not in improving estimates for lines that are already there.

18.6.1 From relatedness to hybrid prediction

The idea that untested single crosses can be predicted from tested ones through marker relatedness is Bernardo (1994)’s: random subsets of 10 to 30 hybrids from a 54-hybrid factorial, and RFLP relationships recovering single-cross performance. Bernardo (1996) scaled it to operational data, with correlations of 0.43 to 0.76 for yield in heterotic patterns with more than 100 tested crosses. Massman et al. (2012) compared marker-relatedness BLUP with ridge-regression marker effects on 479 single crosses. The modern version models hybrid value as pool-specific additive kernels for GCA plus a dominance or product kernel for SCA (Technow et al. 2012; Technow et al. 2014). This book does not fit those models: Chapter 14 fits one additive GBLUP kernel over all hybrids, with no pool split, on a trait with no SCA, and Section 14.4 validates that fit by masking hybrids.

The kernel the cached sweep used for SCA follows from the algebra of Section 18.2: \(s_{ij} = -2\sum_k d_k\,\delta u_{ik}\,\delta v_{jk}\) is linear in the elementwise product of the two parents’ centred genotypes, so the natural SCA kernel is the linear kernel on that product. It is not the Hadamard product of the two pool kernels, which adds cross-locus terms the model does not contain.

18.6.2 The T0 / T1 / T2 hierarchy

The standard currency for comparing designs is the split of untested hybrids by how many of their parents appear in the training set: both (T2), one (T1), neither (T0). Grain-yield accuracies of 0.91, 0.85 and 0.77 with 300 training hybrids (Technow et al. 2014), 0.92, 0.88 and 0.80 under a GCA model on the Hohenheim series (González-Diéguez et al. 2021), and 0.28 to 0.77 with the ordering T2 > T1-female > T1-male > T0 (Kadam et al. 2016). The hierarchy also explains why a tester is a poor training set: it generates only T1-type information for new lines, because the tester is always a tested parent and the candidate never is.

These figures share a property that must be stated: they come from material with strong family structure, where an untested line has close relatives in the training set. Technow (2019) found that the difference in accuracy between two training-set types, F1 hybrids and F1:2 bulks, was largest under a divergent heterotic structure and ranged from 0.15 correlation points for hybrids that are themselves in the training set (T3) to 0.06 for T0, at constant residual variation. Published T0 accuracies are not programme-independent, and the simulated counter-example below makes the point concrete.

18.6.4 Which hybrids to phenotype, and which crosses to make

Once prediction is in place the design question becomes which cells to observe to calibrate it. CDmean (Rincent et al. 2012) beat random sampling and PEVmean for inbred calibration sets because it accounts for the redundancy among related individuals; for hybrids, sets built by clustering, network analysis and mating-scheme considerations beat both (Guo et al. 2019), which suggests that the structure that matters for a hybrid training set is bipartite. In a cross-validation on one Brazilian maize data set, reviewed in a roadmap paper, pre-selecting the best 30 % of single crosses on genomic prediction with additive and non-additive effects retained 85 to 95 % of the top 5 % hybrids for ear height, 75 to 85 % for grain yield and 70 to 80 % for plant height (Fritsche-Neto et al. 2021).

A different question is which within-pool crosses to make to produce the next generation of lines (not the \(A \times B\) crosses of the design sections). The usefulness criterion adds the within-cross selection differential to the predicted mean, and selecting on it added 0.14 genetic standard deviations of gain at 1 % selected per cross (Lehermeier et al. 2017); within-cross variance is usable for ranking but the marker model’s estimate was biased downward by 28 to 60 % (Lian et al. 2015). At the portfolio level, optimal cross selection chooses a set of crosses under a constraint on diversity: 15 to 78 % more genetic gain than truncation selection with four cycles per year in Gorjanc et al. (2018), and more again once within-family variance enters the objective (Allier et al. 2019). Kinghorn (2011) gives an algorithm for mate selection under a group mating permission matrix, reported several hundred times faster than penalising solutions in the scenario he tested. Whether it, or any such algorithm, makes optimising a crossing list over a 200 x 200 candidate space under nursery constraints routine is this chapter’s conjecture, not a result of that paper; nothing in this book implements it. Nor does this book solve the assignment of contributions to crosses that Gorjanc et al. (2018) call optimal cross selection (named in Chapter 15 for the within-pool crosses): the contributions of Chapter 15 attach to hybrids, one per \(A \times B\) cross, and the design problem treated here is a sampling problem for estimation, not an assignment of contributions.

The long-term caution is this book’s own: cross selection controls inbreeding and the divergence of the pools, and a design chosen once should not be held fixed across cycles. Lorenzi et al. (2024) reported SCA shares that differ by trait and between two selection cycles of one silage maize programme (up to 30 % in one cycle’s data and up to 10 % in the other, depending on the trait), so the variance structure that every design calculation in this chapter takes as given may not be stationary.

18.6.5 What the simulation adds to the literature

18.7 Seed production and the cost of a crossing plan

The crossing plan has to be made in a nursery. Treating hybrid seed production as a scalar cost term – as the published cost models do (Marulanda et al. 2016; Longin et al. 2015) – hides the constraint that actually binds sparse designs: the seed quantity per cross limits how many plots and locations each hybrid can occupy.

18.7.1 Three cost centres

A mating design has three cost centres that scale differently. Making the crosses – pollinations, ears, nursery rows, isolation blocks – scales with the number of distinct crosses. Testing them scales with crosses x locations x replicates. Genotyping scales with the number of lines, not crosses, which is the structural reason genomics favours designs with many lines and few crosses each. A tester design makes few crosses with much seed each; a sparse factorial makes many crosses with little seed each. The two can occupy the same plots and need completely different nurseries.

cross_cost() writes the accounting chain from plots back to the nursery: seed needed per cross is plots x seed per plot; ears are that over seed per ear, never fewer than an insurance minimum; pollinations are ears over pollination success; female rows follow from the pollinations each pool-A parent carries, male rows from the pollinations each pool-B parent serves, several per plant. Four designs at the same 2,000 plots:

That missing term is gap 1 of Section 18.9, and it is the term a real programme must add before comparing designs on cost. The other term is seed per cross as a cap on replication. At the placeholder levels one ear gives 250 kernels against 60 per plot, that is 4.2 plots per ear, and the two-ear minimum 8.3; so seed caps replication only above about 8 plots per cross, which no design here approaches (the sweep caps replication at three). The constraint would bind where seed per ear is small against seed per plot; at these levels it does not. Operational sparse designs such as Thomsen et al. WarningThe cost levels are placeholders

The structure – which quantity multiplies which, which capacity binds – is what this section offers. Re-parameterise before any number below means anything for a real programme.

18.7.2 Budget-constrained optimisation

The cached optimiser searched over lines tested per pool, design family, \(k\) or \(c\), plots per cross and whether to genotype every candidate, subject to field, nursery and money constraints, and maximised expected response \(R = i\,\rho\,\sigma_g\) – not accuracy. That distinction is the trade-off: raising \(c\) raises \(\rho\) but, at fixed budget, lowers the number of lines tested and with it the selection intensity \(i\). It scores \(\rho\) with the least-squares closed form of Table 18.1 at the design’s \(c\), whatever the family. Table 18.2 shows that for sparse designs that score is above the cached accuracy of both estimators, so it is optimistic for them, not conservative.

The published allocation results come from other designs and scales and are not tested by this grid; they are cited as context, not as confirmation. A training population of 500 at one replicate was only 4 % more accurate than 125 at four, and a second replicate added 0.09 correlation points (Lorenz 2013); selection gain levels off quickly with budget, so additional money is better spent on more populations than on deeper testing (Riedelsheimer and Melchinger 2013); and the optimum number of testers, locations and stages is a joint decision (C. F. H. Longin et al. 2006; C. Longin et al. 2006; Mi et al. 2014; Marulanda et al. 2016).

18.8 A decision framework

The chapter collapses into a procedure a breeder can apply in a planning meeting.

  1. Can you fit a mixed model? If not, avoid the sparsest layouts (\(c \le 3\) per line, circulant \(c = 5\)): under least squares at 2,000 plots they are worse than the best tester (Table 18.11). Designs with \(c = 10\) or NC II sets exceeded that tester in the cached sweep (the circulant \(c = 10\) row is not reproduced in-repo), and a tester loses little to least squares (Table 18.10); if the field holds only two or three crosses per line, a tester with \(k\) from the curve of Figure 18.2 is the safe choice. Markers are not needed at this step; they enter at step 4.
  2. How many candidates must be ranked, and can the field hold a cross for each? If it can hold \(c \ge 2\) crosses per candidate, prefer a connected sparse factorial (Table 18.6).
  3. Choose \(c\) from the objective. \(c = 2\) ranks lines; \(c \ge 3\) is required before any statement about SCA variance. Do not take \(c\) from the optimiser of Table 18.17, whose score is optimistic for sparse designs (Table 18.2); read it from the accuracy of the sweep (Figure 18.4). Build the layout, do not sample it, and confirm with design_diagnostics() that it is one component.
  4. Choose the line set, not just the crosses. When the field cannot hold every candidate, GBLUP is the only route to the lines left out (Table 18.12). Which lines to field is a separate choice; in this sweep CDmean sets did not raise the genomic accuracy above random sets (Table 18.14), so the decision rests on other grounds than the sweep.
  5. Check the nursery before committing: ears, rows and isolation blocks, not just plots (Table 18.16).
  6. Re-estimate every cycle. Published SCA shares differ between cycles (Lorenzi et al. 2024), and the pools drift (Section 18.11).

18.9 Open problems

Five gaps remain, each defined by the evidence that would close it.

  1. Seed production as an explicit constraint. None of the cost studies cited here (Marulanda et al. 2016; Longin et al. 2015) models seed yield per pollination, isolation capacity or seed per plot inside the optimisation of a mating design; they carry hybrid-seed production as a scalar (no systematic search beyond them was made). cross_cost() gives the structure; the levels are missing.
  2. Joint sparsity in crosses and environments. The cross-sparsity literature (Lorenzi et al. 2022; Melchinger et al. 2023) holds the environment design fixed; the environment-sparsity literature (Jarquin et al. 2020; Atanda et al. 2022; Montesinos-López et al. 2023) works with line or testcross entries. Thomsen et al. (2026) is the closest to a joint treatment, and it is observational. Chapter 20 takes this gap up.
  3. Connectivity reported, structured layouts compared. The tools are seventy years old (Weeks and Williams 1964; Fernando et al. 1983); design papers rarely report the connectivity of the layouts they use. Table 18.8 closes the matched-\(c\) comparison in simulation only.
  4. Tropical maize. Under-represented relative to its share of breeding effort; Fritsche-Neto et al. (2021) and Chaingeni et al. (2025) are the main exceptions.
  5. Multi-cycle validation. The long-term consequences of genomics-driven cross selection for pool divergence are modelled (Gorjanc et al. 2018; Allier et al. 2019; Melchinger and Frisch 2023) but not validated over real cycles, and the SCA shares that differ between cycles in Lorenzi et al. (2024) are the warning case. The design of the crossing plan across cycles is also not treated in Chapter 23, which evaluates every \(A \times B\) hybrid in each cycle and has no crossing design.

Assumptions, stated in the chapter rather than a footnote.

  • Thirty replicates per cell. Every row of the cached sweep has n_rep =
    1. Whether a cached replicate is a new founder set or new phenotypes on one founder set is not recorded in this repository, and the z-scores of this chapter are unpaired (Table 18.6, Table 18.8, Table 18.12); they are conservative if arms share the replicate’s truth. The standard error of a cell mean ranges from 0.0014 to 0.018 (median 0.0042), so a fixed width in correlation points is not a threshold: this chapter calls a difference resolved when its unpaired z exceeds 3, and says where one does not. The single cached run is the unit; none of the cross-budget or cross-design results is replicated across founder sets.
  • One trait, one marker density (1,000 markers, as described by the sparsex study; not checkable here), no epistasis, no selection during line development, no spatial trend, no missing plots.
  • Genotype x location is folded into plot error. An extra location and an extra replicate are treated as equivalent, which is exact under that assumption and optimistic when G x L variance is large. Chapter 19 did not compare a location with a replicate. With G x L in the model, its result 2 (Section 19.11.3) held two plots per hybrid fixed and found that spreading them over more trial locations, 2 to 10, raised accuracy: once G x L exists, the number of environments a trial samples matters, which this assumption cannot represent.

18.11 Where this connects

This chapter settled which crosses to make with the field held fixed: a connected sparse factorial, analysed by BLUP, with \(c\) chosen by the objective (from the sweep, not from the optimiser, Section 18.7). It left the field itself – how many locations each cross is planted in, and which – to Chapter 19, and the trade between crosses and locations under one budget to Chapter 20.

What the Optimisation part receives. Chapter 15, Chapter 16 and Chapter 17 take the values of the whole \(A \times B\) factorial as known. A sparse plan fields 1 to 5 % of it, so the rest are predictions, with the accuracies measured here (all-lines GCA accuracy from 0.52 to 0.83 across the 200-line designs at the three budgets; genomic accuracy for hybrids of untested lines, T0, about 0.14). Which values a sparse plan passes on for the unfielded cells – the GCA sum without SCA, or a genomic prediction – is a choice this chapter does not make, and none of the selection problems of that part accounts for the prediction error; Chapter 14 and the Finding 5 of Section 12.5 are where the two meet.

A hypothesis, not a result: the diversity of the line set. The crossing plan decides which lines are seen. A design that fields 50 lines per pool selected by CDmean ranks those 50, so the next cycle’s parents may in practice come from them, and a loss of gene diversity of the kind that Chapter 13 tracks across the pipeline would then start at the choice of lines (line selection, stage S3 of Table 1.1, which Chapter 13 already measures), not at the later selection of hybrids. No diversity metric of a design – \(GD\), \(\theta\), alleles retained – is computed in this chapter, so this is the book’s subject offered as a question about nursery plans, not a finding of the sweep. A sparse factorial that crosses every candidate keeps every candidate line in the field, which is a statement about which lines are tested, not about the diversity of the hybrids fielded; the latter depends on which cells are filled, and the identities of Section 1.2 that tie it to \(GD_T + GD_{BS}\) are stated for the complete factorial.

Section 5.11 is where the dominance part of the GCA and SCA used here was derived from allele frequencies, and Section 5.13.6 is the finite-tester version of Figure 18.2. Chapter 5 reports, under neutral divergence and dominance only, a share of SCA in the heterosis variance that falls as the pools diverge – a different statistic from the SCA:GCA ratio of this chapter, defined above, and under narrower assumptions – so the direction suggests that the number of testers and the crosses per line a programme needs are not constants of the crop, without giving their value. Chapter 14 fits an additive GBLUP on a trait with no SCA; it does not fit the pool-specific or SCA kernels, and the genomic accuracies of this chapter come from the cached sparsex study (Section 18.10), not from that chapter. Chapter 23 evaluates every \(A \times B\) hybrid in each cycle and has no crossing design: how a design chosen once behaves while the pools drift is not tested there, and not in this book. The contributions of Chapter 15 attach to hybrids; the literature on optimal cross selection (Gorjanc et al. 2018) concerns within-pool crosses, and nothing in this book implements it.
